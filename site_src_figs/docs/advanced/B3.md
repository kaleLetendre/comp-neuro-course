## Lesson spec

# B3. Dendritic computation

**Prerequisites:** the point-neuron LIF model; E/I and Dale's principle

**Size:** one to two sittings

**Source material:** **D&A ch. 6** *The Cable Equation* and *Multi-compartment Models* — the passive grounding for why location is a weight, and the machinery for compartmental models. Not in D&A: the two-layer and apical-gating accounts — Poirazi, Brannon & Mel 2003 (Neuron); Larkum 2013 (TINS); London & Häusser 2005 for an overview.

**Local copies:** none — all three need institutional access (Poirazi doi:10.1016/S0896-6273(03)00149-1; Larkum doi:10.1016/j.tins.2012.11.006; London & Häusser doi:10.1146/annurev.neuro.28.061604.135703). D&A ch. 6 covers the cable-theory half.

**Goal:** Replace the point neuron — soma sums weighted inputs — with dendrites as active, spatially structured compartments. Why a synapse's location on the tree changes its effective weight, why a branch can spike on its own, and how apical input can gate somatic firing: a top-down prediction mechanism inside a single cell, before any network-level PC machinery.

**Scope:**
- Passive cable properties: distal current attenuates before reaching the soma, so location is an implicit weight independent of synaptic strength
- Active dendrites: NMDA and calcium spikes as branch-local thresholded events, distinct from the somatic Na⁺ spike
- Poirazi & Mel's two-layer-network-in-a-neuron: branches as a hidden layer of nonlinear subunits feeding a somatic output unit, and the rough capacity gain over a linear point neuron
- Larkum's apical-tuft mechanism: apical (feedback) and basal (feedforward) input on separate compartments, and how coincidence at the tuft gates or amplifies the somatic response
- Not covered: which cortical layers apical and basal inputs come from (B2), spine pruning (B4)

**You should be able to answer:**
1. Two synapses deliver the same peak current, one proximal, one 300 μm out on a distal branch — which produces the larger somatic EPSP, and why in terms of cable properties?
2. What distinguishes an NMDA spike from the somatic Na⁺ spike — what triggers it, and does it stay local to the branch?
3. In Poirazi & Mel's model, what are the two layers, and roughly how much does capacity rise compared to a linear point neuron?
4. In Larkum's model, which compartment receives basal input and which apical, and what happens to somatic output when both arrive together vs apical alone?
5. If basal input alone produces a normal spike but apical input alone produces none, what does that predict about a neuron receiving strong bottom-up drive with no matching top-down signal?

**Project tie-in:** Step 4's stress mechanism currently has one lever when a neuron cannot predict its input — grow a new neuron pair. Active dendrites suggest a second: give the existing prediction neuron more internal capacity instead.

**Done when:** All five answered unprompted, and the learner can state the two-lever design tension (grow a pair vs add dendritic capacity) that B3 hands to Step 4.

## Piece 1 — Distance as an implicit weight

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 45 min

**Learning goals**
- Compute a passive cable's length constant from membrane/axial parameters.
- Apply steady-state exponential attenuation to get a numeric somatic-EPSP ratio.
- State, without hand-waving, why synapse location is a weight independent of synaptic strength.

**Task**

A dendritic branch is modelled as a passive cylindrical cable with:
- specific membrane resistance R_m = 10,000 Ω·cm²
- axial resistivity R_L = 200 Ω·cm
- diameter d = 2 μm

1. Compute the length constant λ = √(R_m·d / (4·R_L)). Show units at each step (convert d to cm before plugging in).
2. Two synapses deliver **identical peak current**, producing an identical local peak depolarization of 5 mV at their own injection site. Synapse P sits 20 μm from the soma; synapse D sits 300 μm from the soma. Using steady-state attenuation V(soma) = V(local)·exp(−x/λ) for a semi-infinite cable, compute the somatic EPSP for each.
3. Compute the ratio EPSP_P / EPSP_D.
4. In 3–5 sentences, explain why this ratio exists even though the two synapses are electrically identical at the point of injection — i.e., why "location on the dendrite" is itself a weight, separate from synaptic conductance.
5. One sentence: is the steady-state exp(−x/λ) attenuation an over- or under-estimate of attenuation for a fast transient EPSP (as opposed to a sustained current step), and why (hint: capacitive shunting is frequency-dependent).

**Deliverable**

A short written page (typed or a clear photo of handwriting) showing: the λ calculation with units carried through, both attenuation factors to 4 significant figures, both somatic EPSP values in mV, the ratio, and the two short-answer explanations (Q4, Q5).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | λ correct with units shown; both EPSPs and the ratio correct to ≥3 sig figs; Q4 explanation names axial current leaking across the membrane along the path (not "signal gets weaker" vagueness); Q5 correctly says steady-state underestimates real transient attenuation. |
| Adequate | λ and EPSPs correct but units skipped or ratio rounded early causing drift beyond 3 sig figs; Q4 correct in substance but generic ("distance matters"); Q5 attempted but reasoning thin or absent. |
| Not yet | λ formula misapplied (e.g. wrong factor of 4, or d not converted to cm) causing an order-of-magnitude error; or ratio computed with additive instead of multiplicative attenuation; or Q4 conflates location-dependence with synaptic-strength-dependence. |

## Piece 2 — Branches as a hidden layer: a capacity demo

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 90 min

**Learning goals**
- Implement per-branch nonlinearity as a structural (block-sparse) constraint, not just "add a hidden layer."
- Observe, with numbers, a task a linear point neuron cannot solve that a branched unit can.
- Connect the empirical gap back to AC3's "two-layer-network-in-a-neuron" framing.

**Task**

Build a self-contained PyTorch (or numpy, if you implement gradients by hand) script, under ~100 lines, with:

1. **Data**: N = 4000 samples, x ∈ {0,1}⁸ drawn uniformly at random. Partition the 8 inputs into 4 disjoint pairs: (x₀,x₁), (x₂,x₃), (x₄,x₅), (x₆,x₇). Label y = 1 if **any** pair has both bits = 1 (an OR-of-ANDs / DNF target), else y = 0. Split 80/20 train/test.
2. **Model A — point neuron**: a single linear unit over all 8 inputs + sigmoid (`nn.Linear(8,1)` → sigmoid), trained with BCE loss.
3. **Model B — dendritic unit**: 4 independent "branch" subunits, each a linear+sigmoid over *only its own pair* of inputs (`nn.Linear(2,1)` → sigmoid, applied 4 times — this is the block-sparse constraint that makes it a branch, not a fully-connected hidden layer), concatenated and fed into one somatic linear+sigmoid unit (`nn.Linear(4,1)` → sigmoid).
4. Train both models for the same number of epochs with the same optimizer/learning rate. Report final train and test accuracy for both in a printed table.
5. Add a 3–5 sentence comment/docstring explaining, in terms of AC3, which "layer" of Model B is the branches and which is the soma, and why Model A's single linear boundary structurally cannot represent an OR-of-ANDs target (a single hyperplane can't carve out this decision region) while Model B can (each branch pre-computes one AND, the soma only needs an OR over already-nonlinear branch outputs).

**Deliverable**

The script (`.py` file) plus its captured stdout showing the final accuracy table for both models on held-out test data.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Model B's first layer is genuinely block-sparse (each branch only ever sees its own 2 inputs — verified by inspecting the code, not just the docstring); test accuracy gap is clearly demonstrated (Model B ≥ 97%, Model A visibly and reproducibly lower, ideally by ≥5 points); explanation correctly identifies branch layer vs. somatic layer and gives the hyperplane argument for why Model A is structurally capped. |
| Adequate | Correct task/data and both models trained and evaluated correctly, gap is present and reported, but the explanation is vague about *why* (e.g. "the dendrite model has more parameters" without the linear-separability argument). |
| Not yet | Model B is actually fully-connected (4-unit hidden layer with no block-sparsity, i.e. an ordinary MLP) — this is not a dendrite model, it's just a bigger network, and it defeats the point of the exercise; or the label function is accidentally linearly separable (e.g. built from overlapping input pairs) so no capacity gap appears. |

## Piece 3 — D&A ch. 6 + active-dendrite primer

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 75 min

**Learning goals**
- Read the passive cable equation and multi-compartment formalism precisely enough to explain each term physically.
- Connect the textbook's passive machinery to the (non-textbook) active-dendrite mechanisms the project actually needs.

**Task**

**Part A — D&A ch. 6, *The Cable Equation* and *Multi-compartment Models*.** Answer:

1. Write the passive cable equation in terms of V(x,t), λ, and τ_m. Identify, in words, which term is the capacitive current, which is the leak current, and which is the axial (longitudinal) current.
2. State the steady-state solution to the cable equation for a semi-infinite cable with current injected at x = 0. What role does λ play in this solution, concretely?
3. Using λ = √(r_m/r_l), explain why a **thinner** dendrite has a **shorter** length constant than a thicker one with identical membrane properties (connect to how r_l scales with 1/d² and r_m scales with 1/d).
4. In the multi-compartment model, each compartment is a lumped RC circuit connected to its neighbors by an axial coupling conductance. Using this circuit picture, explain why adding more compartments between a synapse and the soma reduces the somatic EPSP without changing the synapse's own conductance at all.
5. D&A's compartments are passive (linear) by default. What, mechanically, would you need to add to one compartment's differential equation to make it behave as a thresholded nonlinear subunit instead (the branch nonlinearity from Part B)?

**Part B — active-dendrite primer** (not in D&A; short grounding text, read before answering):

> Two active nonlinearities live in real dendrites that the passive cable equation doesn't capture. An **NMDA spike** is a local, regenerative depolarization confined to a single branch: it is triggered when enough co-active glutamatergic synapses on that branch push local voltage past the Mg²⁺-block threshold of NMDA receptors, producing calcium/NMDA-receptor-driven current that stays largely local to the branch rather than actively propagating like the somatic Na⁺ spike (which is a single global all-or-nothing event triggered at the axon initial segment once *total* somatic input crosses threshold). Separately, many pyramidal neurons physically segregate inputs onto two compartments: **basal** dendrites (near the soma) carry feedforward, bottom-up drive, while the **apical** tuft (far from the soma, at the top of the cell) carries feedback/top-down input. Basal input alone can already drive a normal somatic spike. Apical input alone, arriving at the distal tuft, is too attenuated to fire the soma by itself — but if it arrives *together with* basal input, the resulting **calcium spike at the apical trunk** boosts the somatic response well above what basal input alone would produce. Apical input alone: no somatic effect. Apical + basal together: amplified somatic output.

6. What distinguishes an NMDA spike from the somatic Na⁺ spike — what triggers each, and does the NMDA spike propagate outside its branch?
7. In the apical-gating picture, which compartment carries basal (feedforward) input and which carries apical (feedback) input? What happens to somatic output when both arrive together, versus apical alone?
8. If basal input alone produces a normal spike but apical input alone produces none, what does that predict about a neuron receiving strong bottom-up drive with no matching top-down signal?

**Deliverable**

Written answers to all 8 questions, 2–5 sentences each, Q3 and Q4 must name the specific scaling relationship or circuit element (not just "distance matters" or "more compartments = more attenuation").

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All 8 answered correctly and specifically; Q3 explicitly uses r_l ∝ 1/d² and r_m ∝ 1/d to get λ ∝ √d (thinner ⇒ smaller λ); Q6–8 correctly distinguish local/branch-confined (NMDA) from global/regenerative (Na⁺), and correctly state apical-alone gives no somatic effect. |
| Adequate | 6–7 of 8 correct; minor imprecision (e.g. Q4 says "attenuation increases" without invoking the RC/coupling-conductance picture); Q8's prediction is directionally right but not clearly connected to a "no top-down confirmation ⇒ suppressed/undistinguished response" statement. |
| Not yet | Fewer than 6 correct, or Q6 conflates NMDA spike with the somatic spike (says both are global), or Q7/Q8 swap which compartment is basal vs. apical. |

## Piece 4 — Written defence: growing a pair vs. adding dendritic capacity

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 60 min · **Length:** 300–600 words

**Learning goals**
- Turn AC3–AC5 into a concrete design rule for Step 4, not just a restatement of the biology.
- Identify what the stress variable would have to measure to distinguish the two cases.
- State the tradeoff (parameter/graph cost vs. representational limit) explicitly.

**Task**

Step 4's stress mechanism currently has exactly one response when a neuron's persistent prediction error (stress) is high: grow a new neuron pair (prediction neuron + error neuron). This lesson has introduced a second lever — give the existing prediction neuron more internal (dendritic-style) capacity instead, e.g. splitting its inputs across nonlinear branch subunits before somatic combination, without changing the graph.

Write 300–600 words arguing for one of: (a) always grow a pair, (b) always add dendritic capacity, (c) a hybrid rule. Your answer must include:

1. A **stated rule** for when each lever applies — not "it depends," a concrete trigger condition.
2. What the **stress variable would have to measure** to distinguish the two cases (a single scalar "unresolved error" can't tell them apart — say what additional signal or second scalar would).
3. The **cost tradeoff**: what growing a pair costs (new graph edges, more parameters, potential network-wide instability) versus what adding dendritic capacity costs (per-neuron parameters only, no topology change).
4. Confirmation that whichever lever is chosen stays consistent with the project's hard constraint that all learning is local (a per-neuron branch-split is still a local change; check that your argument doesn't smuggle in a global signal to decide between the two levers).

**Deliverable**

The 300–600 word written argument, as a single piece of prose (not a bulleted outline standing in for prose — bullets are fine as sub-structure but the core argument must read as an argument).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Concrete, checkable trigger rule (e.g. "if stress stays high after a branch-capacity increase, only then grow a pair" or equivalent nested-threshold logic); explicitly names a second observable/signal the stress variable lacks today (e.g. residual error *after* a capacity increase, or a measure of whether the error is separable across input subsets vs. genuinely unrepresentable by any recombination); cost tradeoff stated in concrete terms (edges/parameters/stability vs. per-neuron parameters); explicitly checks the rule against the locality constraint. |
| Adequate | Takes a clear position with a rule, but the rule is soft ("usually add capacity first, and grow a pair if that doesn't work" with no operational definition of "doesn't work"); cost tradeoff mentioned but generic; locality check is asserted, not demonstrated. |
| Not yet | No concrete trigger rule (settles for "both have their uses"); doesn't say what the stress variable would need to measure differently; ignores cost tradeoff or locality constraint entirely. |
