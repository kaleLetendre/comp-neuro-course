## Lesson spec

**Goal:** Establish decoding as the inverse of encoding — given a response, infer the stimulus — by Bayes' rule rather than a new formalism. Cover discrimination via ROC, four population estimators, the Fisher-information bound, and linear spike-train reconstruction as instances of one inference problem.

??? note "Scope — what the lesson covers"

    - Encoding p(r|s) versus decoding p(s|r): why they are different problems, and how Bayes connects them
    - Two-alternative discrimination, ROC curves, and the result that a single well-tuned neuron can match an animal's psychophysical performance
    - Population decoding compared — population vector, maximum likelihood, MAP, Bayesian — what each assumes and when the population vector is biased
    - Fisher information and the Cramér-Rao bound on decoding accuracy, and its relation to tuning-curve width
    - Spike-train decoding: reconstructing a time-varying stimulus from spike timing
    - Not covered: quantifying readout capacity in bits (F4)

**You should be able to answer:**
1. Write Bayes' rule relating p(r|s) and p(s|r). Which does a single-neuron recording directly estimate, and what extra term do you need for the other?
2. In a two-alternative task, which single number computed from the ROC curve equals proportion correct, and how did that compare to the animal's own threshold in the classic MT experiment?
3. Write the population vector estimator. Under what condition on the tuning curves is it unbiased, and what happens when that condition fails?
4. State the Cramér-Rao bound linking Fisher information to estimator variance. As tuning curves narrow with neuron count fixed, does accuracy improve monotonically — what is the trade-off?
5. Write the linear estimator reconstructing a time-varying stimulus from a spike train as a sum over spike times of a kernel.

**Feeds:** F4 (mutual information formalises how much can be read out), F11 (the same inference machinery moved inside the model).

**Done when:** Given a population of tuning curves and a spike-count vector, you computes the maximum-likelihood and population-vector estimates by hand and says which is biased and why.

=== "Piece 1 — Population and Fisher-information decoding by hand"

    **Type:** derivation · **Time:** ~2 hours

    **Learning goals**
    - Compute a population-vector estimate from a spike-count vector and a set of cosine tuning curves.
    - Compute a maximum-likelihood estimate by evaluating a Poisson log-likelihood on a grid.
    - State the condition under which the population vector is unbiased, and show numerically what happens when it fails.
    - Derive the Fisher information for a Poisson neuron with a Gaussian tuning curve, and use it to see that narrowing tuning curves does not monotonically improve population accuracy.

    **Task**

    All three sub-tasks use the Poisson encoding model: neuron *i* has expected spike count $E[n_i] = f_i(s)\,T$ over trial length $T$, and the actual count $n_i$ is drawn Poisson with that mean.

    **Task A — uniform population, population vector vs. ML.**
    Eight neurons have cosine tuning $f_i(s) = 20 + 20\cos(s - \theta_i)$ Hz, with preferred directions $\theta_i$ evenly spaced every 45°: 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°, 225°, 270°, 315°. Over a $T=1\,\mathrm{s}$ trial with true stimulus $s = 30°$, the observed spike counts are:

    | $\theta_i$ | 0° | 45° | 90° | 135° | 180° | 225° | 270° | 315° |
    |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
    | $n_i$ | 37 | 39 | 30 | 15 | 3 | 1 | 10 | 25 |

    1. Compute the population-vector estimate: $\vec v = \sum_i n_i(\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)$, then $\hat s = \operatorname{atan2}(v_y, v_x)$.
    2. Compute the Poisson log-likelihood (drop the $\log n_i!$ constant) $\ell(s) = \sum_i \big[n_i \ln f_i(s) - f_i(s)\big]$ at $s = 20°, 30°, 40°$, and report which grid point maximizes it.
    3. State whether the two estimators agree with each other and with the true stimulus, to within a few degrees.

    **Task B — non-uniform population, same cosine tuning.**
    Keep $f_i(s) = 20 + 20\cos(s-\theta_i)$ but now the eight preferred directions are clustered: five near 0° (at $-30°,-20°,-10°,0°,10°$... use exactly $\theta_i \in \{-30°, 10°, 20°, 30°, 340°\}$ — five directions within 60° of 0°) and three spread out at $\{120°, 200°, 280°\}$. Using the **noiseless** expected rates (i.e. treat $f_i(90°)$ itself as $n_i$, no Poisson step) for a true stimulus $s = 90°$:
    1. Compute $f_i(90°)$ for each of the eight $\theta_i$.
    2. Compute the population-vector estimate from these noiseless rates.
    3. State, in one sentence, why the estimate is far from 90° even with zero noise, and name the general condition on tuning-curve coverage under which the population vector is unbiased.

    **Task C — Fisher information and tuning-curve width.**
    Five neurons have Gaussian tuning $f_i(s) = 50\exp\!\big(-(s-\theta_i)^2/2\sigma^2\big)$ Hz with $\theta_i \in \{-40°,-20°,0°,20°,40°\}$, evaluated at true stimulus $s=10°$, $T=1\,\mathrm{s}$. For a Poisson neuron, the Fisher information about $s$ is $I_i(s) = T\,f_i'(s)^2 / f_i(s)$, and for independent neurons the population information adds: $I_{\text{pop}}(s) = \sum_i I_i(s)$.
    1. Derive $I_i(s)$ in closed form for the Gaussian tuning curve above (show $f_i'(s)$ first).
    2. Evaluate $I_{\text{pop}}(10°)$ for $\sigma = 20°$, $\sigma=10°$, $\sigma=5°$, and $\sigma=3°$.
    3. Using the Cramér-Rao bound $\mathrm{Var}(\hat s) \geq 1/I_{\text{pop}}(s)$, report the implied lower bound on the standard deviation of any unbiased estimator at each $\sigma$.
    4. State whether accuracy (the CR bound) improves monotonically as $\sigma$ shrinks, and explain the trade-off in one or two sentences.

    **Deliverable**

    A written or typed solution set showing every intermediate number (not just final answers) for Tasks A, B, and C, plus one sentence per task answering its final question.

        | Adequate | Gets the arithmetic mostly right (minor rounding/sign slips) and reaches the right qualitative conclusions for at least two of the three tasks, but Task C's non-monotonicity is asserted rather than shown in the numbers, or one task's final-question sentence is vague. |

=== "Piece 2 — Simulating three population decoders"

    **Type:** simulation · **Time:** ~1.5 hours

    **Learning goals**
    - Implement Poisson spike-count generation from tuning curves and three decoders (population vector, ML, MAP) from scratch.
    - Observe, across many trials, that population-vector error explodes when tuning-curve coverage is non-uniform while ML stays accurate — the bias from Task B, now shown statistically rather than in one hand-picked case.
    - Observe that a MAP estimator with a strong prior is pulled away from the ML estimate toward the prior mode.

    **Task**

    Run the script below (or reimplement it if you prefer to type it yourself — that's more valuable than pasting it). It is under 100 lines, pure NumPy, no downloads.

    ```python
    import numpy as np

    rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
    T = 1.0
    R0, RMAX = 20.0, 20.0

    def rates(thetas, s):
        return R0 + RMAX * np.cos(np.deg2rad(s - thetas))

    def pop_vector(n, thetas):
        vx = np.sum(n * np.cos(np.deg2rad(thetas)))
        vy = np.sum(n * np.sin(np.deg2rad(thetas)))
        return np.rad2deg(np.arctan2(vy, vx)) % 360

    def loglik(n, thetas, grid):
        f = rates(thetas[:, None], grid[None, :])
        f_safe = np.clip(f, 1e-9, None)
        return np.sum(n[:, None] * np.log(f_safe) - f, axis=0)

    def ml_estimate(n, thetas, grid):
        return grid[np.argmax(loglik(n, thetas, grid))]

    def map_estimate(n, thetas, grid, prior_logpdf):
        return grid[np.argmax(loglik(n, thetas, grid) + prior_logpdf(grid))]

    def circ_err(est, true):
        return (est - true + 180) % 360 - 180

    grid = np.arange(0, 360, 1.0)
    thetas_uniform = np.arange(0, 360, 22.5)                    # 16 neurons, evenly spaced
    thetas_clustered = np.concatenate(
        [np.linspace(-30, 30, 10), np.linspace(60, 300, 6)]) % 360  # 10 of 16 packed near 0 deg

    def run_trials(thetas, n_trials=1000):
        pv_e, ml_e = [], []
        for _ in range(n_trials):
            s_true = rng.uniform(0, 360)
            f = rates(thetas, s_true)
            n = rng.poisson(f * T)
            pv_e.append(circ_err(pop_vector(n, thetas), s_true))
            ml_e.append(circ_err(ml_estimate(n, thetas, grid), s_true))
        return np.array(pv_e), np.array(ml_e)

    for name, thetas in [("uniform", thetas_uniform), ("clustered", thetas_clustered)]:
        pv_e, ml_e = run_trials(thetas)
        print(f"{name}: PV mean={pv_e.mean():.2f} std={pv_e.std():.2f} MAE={np.abs(pv_e).mean():.2f} | "
              f"ML mean={ml_e.mean():.2f} std={ml_e.std():.2f} MAE={np.abs(ml_e).mean():.2f}")

    def vm_logpdf(x, mu=90.0, kappa=4.0):
        return kappa * np.cos(np.deg2rad(x - mu))

    s_true = 200.0
    diffs = []
    for _ in range(200):
        f = rates(thetas_uniform, s_true)
        n = rng.poisson(f * T)
        ml = ml_estimate(n, thetas_uniform, grid)
        mp = map_estimate(n, thetas_uniform, grid, vm_logpdf)
        diffs.append(circ_err(mp, ml))
    print(f"MAP-vs-ML shift toward prior (true={s_true}): mean={np.mean(diffs):.1f} std={np.std(diffs):.1f}")

    s_fixed = 90.0
    pv_fixed, ml_fixed = [], []
    for _ in range(200):
        f = rates(thetas_clustered, s_fixed)
        n = rng.poisson(f * T)
        pv_fixed.append(pop_vector(n, thetas_clustered))
        ml_fixed.append(ml_estimate(n, thetas_clustered, grid))
    print(f"clustered, s_true=90 fixed: PV mean estimate={np.mean(pv_fixed):.1f} (true=90) | "
          f"ML mean estimate={np.mean(ml_fixed):.1f}")
    ```

    Answer, from the actual printed output:
    1. Compare the `clustered` row's PV mean-absolute-error to the `uniform` row's. By roughly what factor is it larger?
    2. Compare `clustered` PV MAE to `clustered` ML MAE. Which estimator degrades under non-uniform coverage and which doesn't?
    3. In the fixed-stimulus (`s_true=90`) run, how far is the average PV estimate from the true 90°, and how does that compare to the average ML estimate? Relate this to Task B of Piece 1.
    4. In the MAP line, which direction did the estimate move relative to ML (toward 90° or away from it), and why is that the expected, not broken, behavior of a MAP estimator?

    **Deliverable**

    The script's console output (paste it) plus your four answers above, each 1-3 sentences.
