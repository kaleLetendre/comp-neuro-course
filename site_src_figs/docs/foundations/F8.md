## Lesson spec

**Goal:** Move from single neurons to populations described by firing rates, and show that a linear recurrent network's behaviour is fully characterised by the eigenstructure of its weight matrix. Establish selective amplification, effective time constants and stability as the load-bearing ideas that A2 and A3 each extend in a different direction.

??? note "Scope — what the lesson covers"

    - The firing-rate model as an approximation to a spiking population, with its assumptions stated and a regime where it breaks
    - Feedforward networks: what a weight matrix does to an input pattern
    - Linear recurrent networks by eigen-decomposition: selective amplification of eigenvector-aligned components, the effective time constant per mode, and the stability condition
    - Nonlinear saturating recurrent networks: winner-take-all and gain control as consequences of saturation
    - Excitatory-inhibitory networks: the conditions for stable, damped or oscillatory return to equilibrium
    - Stochastic networks as a bridge to energy functions and noise, introduced but not developed
    - Not covered: attractors, basins and Hopfield memory built from the energy function (A2); E-I dynamics developed into rhythms, bands and synchrony (A3)

**You should be able to answer:**
1. A linear recurrent network has largest real eigenvalue 0.8. Given membrane time constant tau, what is that mode's effective time constant, and what happens as the eigenvalue approaches 1?
2. State the stability condition for a linear recurrent network in terms of the eigenvalues of the recurrent weight matrix.
3. In a linearised E-I network, what condition on the eigenvalues distinguishes damped oscillatory return to equilibrium from monotonic decay?
4. For an input aligned with an eigenvector of eigenvalue lambda, give the expression for steady-state amplification compared to the feedforward-only case.
5. Name two computations saturation enables that a linear recurrent network cannot perform, and say specifically why linearity precludes each.

**Feeds:** A2 (energy functions and recurrent dynamics), A3 (E-I dynamics), F9 (plasticity acts on these architectures).

**Done when:** From a given recurrent weight matrix you computes eigenvalues and predicts which input components are amplified, how fast each mode relaxes, and whether the network is stable — without reaching for attractor or oscillation vocabulary.

=== "Piece 1 — Eigenmode analysis of a linear recurrent network"

    **Type:** derivation · **Time:** 90-120 min

    **Learning goals**
    - Derive the effective time constant and steady-state gain of a single eigenmode of a linear recurrent network, from the network's defining ODE.
    - State and justify the stability condition for the full network in terms of the eigenvalues of the recurrent weight matrix.
    - Reduce a linearised excitatory-inhibitory network to a 2x2 eigenvalue problem and derive the algebraic condition that separates damped oscillatory return to equilibrium from monotonic decay.

    **Task**

    Part A — single-population eigenmode. Take the standard linear recurrent network equation from D&A §7.3:
    tau du/dt = -u + h + W u,
    where u(t) is the vector of population firing rates, h is the (constant, step) feedforward drive, and W is the recurrent weight matrix. Let e_a be an eigenvector of W with real eigenvalue lambda_a, and suppose the input is aligned with that eigenvector: h = h_a e_a, u(0) = 0.

    1. Write u(t) = c(t) e_a, substitute, and reduce the vector ODE to a scalar ODE for c(t). Solve it in closed form.
    2. Read off the effective time constant tau_eff(lambda_a) and the steady-state value c_ss.
    3. Define the amplification factor A(lambda_a) as the ratio of c_ss to the steady-state value the same input would produce with W = 0 (feedforward-only). Give A(lambda_a) as a function of lambda_a alone.
    4. Evaluate tau_eff and A at lambda_a = 0.8 with tau = 10 ms. State in words what happens to tau_eff as lambda_a -> 1^-, and why lambda_a = 1 is a singular point of your formula.
    5. Generalise: for an N-dimensional W with eigenvalues lambda_1, ..., lambda_N (assume W diagonalisable), state the condition on {lambda_a} under which every mode of the network is stable (bounded as t -> infinity for bounded h). Justify it from the sign of (1 - lambda_a) in your part-2 solution.

    Part B — excitatory-inhibitory 2x2 reduction. Consider the linearised two-population E-I system from D&A §7.5, written in the same form as Part A with u = (v_E, v_I)^T and a single shared tau:
    tau du/dt = -u + h + W u, W = [[w_EE, -w_EI], [w_IE, -w_II]], all four w's >= 0.

    6. For W_1 = [[0.5, -1.0], [1.5, -0.5]], compute trace(W_1) and det(W_1), form the characteristic polynomial lambda^2 - trace(W_1) lambda + det(W_1) = 0, and solve for the eigenvalues. State whether they are real or complex.
    7. Repeat for W_2 = [[0.5, -1.0], [0.2, -0.5]].
    8. From your part-1 solution, the decay/growth rate of a mode is governed by (lambda_a - 1)/tau. For each of W_1 and W_2, compute the eigenvalues of (W - I) and state (i) whether the network is stable, and (ii) whether its return to equilibrium after a small perturbation is monotonic or has a damped oscillatory component. Give the general algebraic rule (in terms of trace and det of the 2x2 W, or equivalently the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial) that decides real vs. complex eigenvalues, independent of the specific numbers.
    9. For the complex case, give the angular frequency of the decaying oscillation (omega, in rad/ms) and the decay time constant, using tau = 10 ms.

    **Deliverable**

    A written derivation (LaTeX, Markdown with math, or clearly legible scanned handwriting) covering all 9 steps, with every intermediate line of algebra shown — not just final formulas. Final numeric answers for steps 4, 6, 7, 9 must be stated explicitly, not left as unevaluated expressions.

    ??? info "Rubric — how this is marked"

        | Band | Criteria |
        |---|---|
        | Strong | All 9 steps correct and shown in full. Correctly identifies 1 - lambda_a as the mode's decay rate and derives A(lambda_a) = 1/(1 - lambda_a) from first principles (not by pattern-matching to a remembered formula). Correctly states the general stability condition as max_a Re(lambda_a) < 1 (not just "lambda < 1" for a single real eigenvalue). Correctly derives and states the trace/det (or discriminant) rule for real vs. complex eigenvalues of a general 2x2 matrix, then applies it to both W_1 and W_2. |
        | Adequate | Steps 1-4 and 6-8 correct with correct final numbers, but the general N-dimensional stability condition (step 5) is stated loosely (e.g. "eigenvalues less than 1" without addressing complex eigenvalues via their real part), or the general trace/det rule in step 8 is not stated explicitly (only the two worked numeric cases are done). |
        | Not yet | Arithmetic errors in the closed-form solution, effective time constant, or eigenvalue computations; amplification factor derived by guessing rather than substitution; oscillatory vs. monotonic distinction not tied to real vs. complex eigenvalues. |

=== "Piece 2 — Verifying eigenmode predictions in a linear recurrent network, then breaking it"

    **Type:** simulation · **Time:** 75-100 min

    **Learning goals**
    - Build a linear recurrent network whose weight matrix has a known, controlled eigenstructure, and recover that eigenstructure numerically.
    - Verify the effective-time-constant and steady-state-amplification formulas from Piece 1 against direct numerical integration, per eigenmode.
    - Observe the stability boundary directly (an eigenvalue crossing 1 produces unbounded growth) and observe that a saturating nonlinearity restores boundedness without restoring linearity's clean per-mode formulas.

    **Task**

    Construct a 3-neuron linear recurrent network with weight matrix
    W = 0.8 (e1 e1^T) + 0.3 (e2 e2^T) + (-0.5) (e3 e3^T),
    using the fixed orthonormal vectors
    e1 = (1,1,1)/sqrt(3), e2 = (1,-1,0)/sqrt(2), e3 = (1,1,-2)/sqrt(6).
    (These three vectors are mutually orthogonal and unit-norm; you may verify this by hand before coding.) This construction guarantees W is symmetric with exact eigenvalues 0.8, 0.3, -0.5 and eigenvectors e1, e2, e3.

    Simulate tau du/dt = -u + h + Wu by explicit Euler integration with tau = 10 ms, dt = 0.1 ms, for at least 300 ms, starting from u(0) = 0.

    Part 1: For each eigenvector e_a in turn, drive the network with h = 1.0 * e_a (a step input aligned with that eigenvector, held constant). Project u(t) onto e_a at every time step. Record the simulated steady-state value and the simulated time to reach 63.2% of that steady-state value (the empirical effective time constant). Compare both against the analytic tau_eff(lambda_a) and steady-state amplification formulas from Piece 1.

    Part 2: Rebuild W with the first eigenvalue changed from 0.8 to 1.2 (keep 0.3 and -0.5). Drive with h = 1.0 * e1 as before and simulate for 200 ms. Confirm the projection onto e1 grows rather than saturates, and compare its growth to the analytic solution from Piece 1's step-1 formula evaluated at lambda_a = 1.2 (same closed form, now with 1 - lambda_a < 0).

    Part 3: Keep the unstable W from Part 2 (lambda_1 = 1.2). Add a saturating nonlinearity: replace the recurrent drive Wu with an elementwise cap, f(x) = r_max * tanh(x / r_max) with r_max = 5.0, applied to the vector Wu before it enters the ODE. Simulate for 400 ms with the same h = 1.0 * e1. Confirm the projection onto e1 now approaches a finite steady value instead of diverging.

    Write this as under ~100 lines of self-contained Python using only numpy (no downloads, no external data). Print a small table for Part 1 (lambda, predicted tau_eff, predicted steady-state, simulated steady-state, simulated tau_eff) and the key checkpoint values for Parts 2 and 3.

    **Deliverable**

    The `.py` script, plus its printed output, plus 3-5 sentences per part stating whether the numerical result matched the analytic prediction from Piece 1 (and by how much, if not exact).

    ??? info "Rubric — how this is marked"

        | Band | Criteria |
        |---|---|
        | Strong | W constructed correctly (numpy's eigendecomposition recovers 0.8, 0.3, -0.5 and eigenvectors matching e1, e2, e3 up to sign); Part 1 table matches analytic predictions to within a few percent for all three modes; Part 2 shows clear unbounded growth matched against the analytic exponential; Part 3 shows convergence to a finite value and you correctly identifies that the saturating network no longer obeys the simple 1/(1-lambda) amplification law because superposition (linearity) has been broken. |
        | Adequate | Part 1 and Part 2 done correctly with correct qualitative conclusions, but Part 3 only shows "it doesn't blow up" without engaging the amplification-formula-breaks-down point, or the effective-time-constant measurement method (63.2% threshold) is applied inconsistently across modes. |
        | Not yet | W's eigenvalues/eigenvectors not verified against the target values before use; input not actually aligned with an eigenvector (so the projection mixes modes and the comparison to Piece 1 is invalid); Part 2's instability not connected back to the sign of (1 - lambda_a); Part 3 nonlinearity applied in a way that makes it a different network (e.g. saturating u instead of the recurrent drive) without noting the difference. |

=== "Piece 3 — Guided reading: D&A chapter 7"

    **Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 45-60 min

    **Learning goals**
    - Extract the firing-rate model's defining assumptions and the regime in which the text says it breaks down.
    - Extract the functional description of what a feedforward weight matrix does to an input pattern, and what a linear recurrent network's eigenbasis represents.
    - Extract the conditions the text gives for E-I stability and the qualitative behaviours (nonlinear saturation, stochastic units) that the chapter previews without fully developing.

    **Task**

    Read D&A chapter 7, sections *Introduction*, *Firing-Rate Models*, *Feedforward Networks*, *Recurrent Networks*, *Excitatory-Inhibitory Networks*, and *Stochastic Networks*, in that order. Answer the following, citing the section (not a page number) each answer comes from:

    1. (*Firing-Rate Models*) What quantity does a firing-rate model track instead of individual spikes, and what averaging assumption (over time, over a population, or both) justifies replacing the spiking description with it? Name one experimental or coding scenario the text raises where this averaging assumption fails.
    2. (*Firing-Rate Models*) What is the general functional form the text gives for how a firing rate depends on the summed synaptic input (linear vs. rectified vs. saturating), and which of these does the chapter use for most of the subsequent feedforward/recurrent analysis?
    3. (*Feedforward Networks*) In a purely feedforward network, what mathematical operation does the weight matrix perform on an input pattern? Give the example the text uses (e.g. a specific receptive-field or filtering construction) of a feedforward weight matrix computing something interpretable.
    4. (*Recurrent Networks*) Why does the text diagonalise/eigendecompose the recurrent weight matrix rather than working with the original coordinates? What does it mean, physically, for an eigenvector of W to be "selectively amplified"?
    5. (*Recurrent Networks*) What does the text call the network's behaviour as the largest eigenvalue approaches its stability boundary, and what does it say happens to the network's response time and gain there?
    6. (*Excitatory-Inhibitory Networks*) What are the two population variables in the E-I model, and what sign convention does the text use for excitatory vs. inhibitory weights in the recurrent matrix?
    7. (*Excitatory-Inhibitory Networks*) What condition(s) does the text give on the linearised system for the equilibrium to be stable, and does it separate oscillatory from non-oscillatory decay explicitly, or only implicitly through the eigenvalues?
    8. (*Stochastic Networks*) What update rule does the text give for a stochastic binary unit (what determines the probability it turns on), and what quantity does the text introduce that plays a role analogous to potential energy in this stochastic dynamics? (Answer only what the text states about this quantity's definition — do not attempt to characterize its long-run behaviour or interpret what it predicts about the network's fixed points.)
    9. (Synthesis, *Feedforward* vs. *Recurrent Networks* vs. the saturating-nonlinearity remarks in the chapter) The chapter notes that some population computations require nonlinearity. Find and quote (in your own words) one such computation the text mentions that a purely linear network cannot perform, and what property of linear systems the text says is responsible.

    **Deliverable**

    Written answers to all 9 questions, 1-4 sentences each, each tagged with its source section.

=== "Piece 4 — Written explanation: why linearity is not enough"

    **Type:** written explanation · **Time:** 45-60 min

    **Learning goals**
    - Explain, in prose, why a linear recurrent network's entire behaviour (amplification, relaxation speed, stability) reduces to its eigenstructure.
    - Name and justify, using the linearity properties (superposition, homogeneity), two computations a saturating nonlinear network can do that a linear one structurally cannot.

    **Task**

    Write 300-600 words, aimed at a reader who has done Piece 1 and Piece 2 but not read D&A directly, explaining:
    (a) why decomposing a linear recurrent network into eigenmodes lets you predict its entire time course and stability from a list of numbers (the eigenvalues) rather than simulating the full system;
    (b) what "selective amplification" and "effective time constant" mean physically, using your lambda = 0.8 numbers from Piece 1 as a worked reference;
    (c) name two specific computations (e.g. winner-take-all selection between competing inputs, and gain control/normalization that keeps output bounded regardless of input strength) that require a saturating nonlinearity, and for each, state precisely which linear-system property (superposition or homogeneity/scale-invariance) is violated by the computation, and why that property being violated is exactly why a linear network cannot do it.

    You may briefly note (1-2 sentences, no more) that the chapter also introduces stochastic units with an energy-like quantity as a preview of a different kind of network description — but do not develop what that quantity implies about network behaviour; that is out of scope for this lesson.

    **Deliverable**

    A 300-600 word written explanation, prose (not bullet points except perhaps for the two named computations in part (c)).
