## 8.1 Introduction: a weight that changes

Up to now a synaptic weight has been a parameter set by hand. Chapter 8 turns it into a dynamical variable with its own differential equation, driven by the same pre- and postsynaptic activity it helps produce. The empirical anchor is long-term potentiation and depression: in a rat hippocampal slice, 1 second of 100 Hz stimulation at the Schaffer collateral–CA1 synapse raises the evoked field potential and holds most of that rise for at least 15 minutes; 10 minutes of 2 Hz stimulation afterward drags the response back down to roughly the midpoint between baseline and the potentiated level.

{{fig:8.1|LTP induced by 100 Hz stimulation, then LTD induced by 2 Hz stimulation, at the same hippocampal synapse}}

High-frequency drive, which reliably evokes a postsynaptic spike, strengthens the synapse; low-frequency drive, which does not, weakens it — Hebb's 1949 conjecture made quantitative. Three training regimes recur through the rest of the course: unsupervised learning, where only the input is imposed and the network's own dynamics set the output; supervised learning, where a teacher also imposes the desired output; and reinforcement learning (chapter 9), where only a scalar evaluation of performance is available. Here u (presynaptic) and v (postsynaptic) are continuous firing rates, with v = w · u at steady state.

## 8.2 Synaptic plasticity rules

**The basic Hebb rule.** τ_w dw/dt = vu: weights grow in proportion to the product of pre- and postsynaptic activity. Averaged over training, with v = w · u substituted in, this becomes τ_w dw/dt = Q · w, where Q = ⟨uu⟩ is the input correlation matrix. Dot the un-averaged rule with w itself: τ_w d|w|²/dt = 2v², positive for any nonzero v. The weight vector's length grows without bound, monotonically, forever — nothing in the rule stops it. An upper saturation bound has to be imposed by hand, and even then the rule fails to make synapses compete: every weight can drift up to the same ceiling, at which point the neuron has lost all selectivity among its inputs.

**The covariance rule.** Real synapses depress as well as potentiate, and LTD needs some postsynaptic activity to occur — pure silence does not weaken a synapse, low activity does. Subtracting a threshold fixes this: τ_w dw/dt = (v − θ_v)u, with θ_v set to the running average ⟨v⟩. Averaged over training this becomes τ_w dw/dt = C · w, where C = ⟨(u − ⟨u⟩)(u − ⟨u⟩)⟩ is the input covariance matrix, not the raw correlation matrix. It is still unstable, by the same argument applied to v(v − ⟨v⟩), and still non-competitive on its own.

**The BCM rule.** τ_w dw/dt = vu(v − θ_v) requires both presynaptic activity and a postsynaptic deviation from threshold, so a silent input is never modified. Stability and competition come from letting the threshold slide: τ_θ dθ_v/dt = v² − θ_v, a low-pass filter on v² running faster than the weights change but slower than individual stimulus presentations. As one synapse strengthens and drives v up, θ_v tracks it, making it harder for every synapse onto that neuron to strengthen further — a threshold coupled to its own neuron's recent output, turning a rule with no explicit normalization term into a stable, competitive one.

**Synaptic normalization.** A more direct fix constrains the weights explicitly. Subtractive normalization holds Σw_b fixed by subtracting the same quantity from every weight regardless of size: τ_w dw/dt = vu − v(n · u)n/N_u, with n the all-ones vector. That subtracted term is identical in absolute terms across synapses — large relative to a small weight, small relative to a large one — so small weights are driven toward zero fast: strongly competitive, and, left unchecked, prone to leaving all weights but one at zero. Multiplicative normalization, the Oja rule, instead holds Σw_b² fixed: τ_w dw/dt = vu − αv²w. The correction term is proportional to w itself, so small weights are barely touched while large ones are reined in, leaving a smoother distribution with no collapse onto a single nonzero weight.

{{fig:8.3|Hebbian weight dynamics with saturation: which corner of weight space the trajectory reaches depends on the starting point}}

**Timing-based rules.** All the rules above use instantaneous or averaged rates. Real synapses care about order: a presynaptic spike 10 ms before a postsynaptic spike produces lasting potentiation, building over 5 to 10 minutes; the reverse order, 10 ms after, produces depression of similar size; a 100 ms separation in either order leaves nothing lasting. The data support a window roughly ±50 ms wide, sign flipping at zero, magnitude peaking at separations of only a few milliseconds.

{{fig:8.2|EPSP change as a function of post-minus-pre spike timing: potentiation for pre-before-post, depression for the reverse, decaying to no effect by about 100 ms}}

## 8.3 Unsupervised learning

**Eigenvectors of Q.** Write w(t) in the basis of Q's own eigenvectors e_μ, with Qe_μ = λ_μe_μ, λ_1 ≥ λ_2 ≥ .... Each coefficient evolves independently as c_μ(t) = c_μ(0)exp(λ_μt/τ_w), so w(t) = Σ_μ exp(λ_μt/τ_w)(w(0) · e_μ)e_μ. Every term grows, but the one with the largest eigenvalue grows fastest and, for large t, swamps the rest: w becomes parallel to e_1, the principal eigenvector, almost regardless of starting point. The output after training, v = w · u, is then proportional to e_1 · u. Saturation complicates this: in a two-input example with correlation off-diagonal -0.4, small initial weights let e_1 win and the trajectory lands at a corner like (w_max, 0), while larger initial weights can drive it instead to (w_max, w_max) before the exponential picks a direction. The Oja rule avoids that: |w|² relaxes to 1/α while the direction still converges to e_1, landing at e_1/√α exactly, no saturation needed.

{{fig:8.4|Hebbian weight vectors aligning with the direction of maximum variance of the input cloud — correlation-based for zero-mean inputs, covariance-based once the mean is nonzero}}

That direction is not incidental. The input cloud's variance is largest along e_1 by definition of a covariance matrix's eigenvectors, and for Gaussian statistics entropy grows with log σ — so projecting onto e_1 maximizes both the variance of v and the information it carries about u. Nothing in the Hebb or Oja rule was told to maximize variance; local, activity-only updates produce it as a consequence. This is principal component analysis, extracted by a rule that never computes a covariance matrix and never performs an eigen-decomposition — it only sees pre- and postsynaptic activity at one synapse.

The same argument gives ocular dominance. With right- and left-eye inputs of equal statistics, the 2×2 correlation matrix has eigenvectors e_1 = (1,1)/√2, e_2 = (1,−1)/√2, with eigenvalues λ_1 = q_S + q_D and λ_2 = q_S − q_D, q_D being the correlation between the eyes. Since q_D > 0 after eye opening, λ_1 > λ_2, so the basic Hebb rule drives w toward e_1 — equal innervation, not what develops. Subtractive normalization suppresses growth along e_1 (proportional here to the all-ones vector n) while leaving e_2 untouched, forcing w toward (1,−1): one eye saturates at w_max, the other at zero. Across an array of cortical units coupled by fixed lateral connections, this produces the alternating stripes seen experimentally.

## 8.4 Supervised learning

Everything above used only u and v, generated by the network itself. Supervised learning also imposes a desired output during training. A Hebbian rule can still be used — τ_w dw/dt = ⟨vu⟩ − αw, v now the imposed target rather than w · u — and since the right side no longer depends on w, growth is linear rather than exponential, so a plain decay term suffices for stability. Applied to a binary perceptron with ±1 inputs and outputs, this gives w = (1/N_u)Σv^m u^m, and the fraction of patterns correctly classified depends on the ratio N_u/(N_S − 1): good once this ratio exceeds roughly 5, falling to chance as the number of patterns to memorize approaches the number of inputs.

{{fig:8.12|Fraction of correctly classified patterns for a Hebbian perceptron, as a function of Nu /(NS − 1)}}

Hebbian learning, supervised or not, never checks whether the network's current output is right or wrong — it only accumulates correlation. An error-correcting rule does. For a linear unit approximating a target function h(s) from tuning curves f_b(s) as basis functions, v(s) = w · f(s), define E = ½⟨(h(s) − v(s))²⟩ and descend its gradient. Doing this one sample at a time rather than averaging the whole training set gives the delta rule: w → w + ε_w(h(s^m) − v(s^m))f(s^m). The term (h − v) is the one ingredient absent from Hebb, covariance, and BCM alike — none of them has access to a target value; they update on correlation alone. The delta rule needs that error signal explicitly, which is also why it is harder to motivate biophysically: it requires comparing the network's actual output against a value the network did not itself produce.

## 8.5 Chapter summary

Every rule after the basic Hebb rule answers one of two questions: what keeps the weights from growing forever, and what keeps a neuron's synapses from all becoming identical. Sliding thresholds, subtractive normalization, and multiplicative normalization answer the first by three different mechanisms; the second emerges only once a genuine constraint is in place. The result worth carrying forward: an unsupervised, purely local rule, with no notion of error or teacher, converges on the direction of maximum variance in its own input — principal component analysis, performed by a synapse that only ever multiplies two numbers together.
