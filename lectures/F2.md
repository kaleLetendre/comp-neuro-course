## Why a linear model, and its limits

Chapter 1 built the spike-triggered average as a way to characterize a neuron's stimulus preference. This chapter turns that average into a predictive model: given a stimulus, estimate a firing rate. The scope is explicit from the first page — the technique works well for retinal ganglion cells, LGN relay neurons, and V1 simple cells, works less well but still usably for V1 complex cells, and breaks down for V2, area 18, and MT, where nonlinear effects the chapter does not attempt to capture take over. Keep that boundary in view; everything that follows is a model of the *early* visual pathway, not a general theory of vision.

## The optimal kernel, the best stimulus, and a nonlinearity bolted on

The estimate is a linear filter: $r_{\text{est}}(t) = r_0 + \int_0^\infty d\tau\, D(\tau) s(t-\tau)$ — equation 2.1, the same form as chapter 1's $r_{\text{approx}}$. $D(\tau)$ is formally the first term of a Volterra expansion (the functional analogue of a Taylor series, equation 2.2), rearranged by Wiener into a form where $D$ is called the linear kernel. Choosing $D$ to minimize the mean squared error between estimate and data (equation 2.3) gives a condition relating $D$ to the stimulus autocorrelation $Q_{ss}$ and the firing-rate-stimulus correlation $Q_{rs}$ (equation 2.4) — solvable in general by Fourier methods (appendix A), but solvable by inspection in one special case: white noise, where $Q_{ss}(\tau) = \sigma_s^2 \delta(\tau)$. Equation 2.4 then collapses to $D(\tau) = Q_{rs}(-\tau)/\sigma_s^2 = \langle r \rangle C(\tau)/\sigma_s^2$, where $C(\tau)$ is just the spike-triggered average. This is the whole justification for using white noise in these experiments: it is the one stimulus class for which "average the stimulus before each spike" *is* the optimal linear filter, with no further correction needed. Work through why the delta function is doing that work, and what survives of equation 2.4 when $Q_{ss}$ is not a delta function — appendix A's acausal solution (equation 2.59) is the general answer, and it needs a full deconvolution in frequency, not an average.

A companion result (appendix B) answers a different question: not what the kernel is, but what stimulus, at fixed energy, drives the strongest response. The answer is the kernel itself, which licenses reading the spike-triggered average as "the stimulus this neuron likes" even without doing the regression in equation 2.4.

The linear estimate has two obvious defects: it can go negative, and it never saturates. Section 2.2 patches both with a static nonlinearity $F$, giving $r_{\text{est}}(t) = r_0 + F(L(t))$ (equation 2.8), with $L$ the linear filter output. $F$ is read off empirically by plotting $(L(t), r(t))$ pairs and fitting a curve through the scatter (figure 2.2A); three standard choices — threshold-linear, sigmoid, rectified tanh — are compared in figure 2.2B with concrete parameters ($G=25$ Hz, $L_{1/2}=3$, $r_{\max}=100$ Hz). Bussgang's theorem (appendix C) is the reassurance that bolting $F$ on afterward does not invalidate the kernel computed in section 2.2's linear step, provided the white noise used was Gaussian.

{{fig:2.3|Linear filter, then static nonlinearity, then a Poisson spike generator — the full model chain}}

That three-stage chain — linear filter, static nonlinearity, Poisson spike generation — is the complete recipe used for every neuron in the rest of the chapter, and it has a name worth fixing now: the linear-nonlinear (or LN) cascade.

## Retina to cortex, the map, the stimuli, the sampling limit

Section 2.3 is scene-setting. Light is transduced in rods and cones, passed through bipolar and horizontal cells (graded potentials, fine over retinal distances), and converted to spikes only at the retinal ganglion cell, the first stage that needs to send a signal any distance — down the optic nerve to the LGN, then on to V1 (figures 2.4, 2.5). Two response classes recur throughout the chapter: X/P/simple cells, where spatial contributions sum linearly, and Y/M/complex cells, where they do not.

{{fig:2.9|Two different spatial frequencies sampled by the same photoreceptor spacing produce identical sample points — aliasing}}

The retinotopic map is logarithmic in eccentricity ($X = \lambda \ln(1+\epsilon/\epsilon_0)$, with $\lambda \approx 12$ mm and $\epsilon_0 \approx 1°$ in the macaque), which is why cortical magnification is so much higher at the fovea. The chapter's standard stimulus, the counterphase sinusoidal grating of equation 2.18, is worth memorizing in full — spatial frequency $K$, orientation $\Theta$, phase $\Phi$, temporal frequency $\omega$ — because every tuning curve in sections 2.4 and 2.5 is this neuron's response as one of those four parameters is swept with the others fixed at preference. Separately, the photoreceptor mosaic imposes a hard sampling limit, the Nyquist frequency $K_{\text{nyq}} = \pi/\Delta x$: at 120 cones per degree in the foveal macaque, that works out to 60 cycles per degree, and frequencies above it alias onto frequencies below it rather than vanishing.

## Space, time, and space-time receptive fields

This is the chapter's technical core. The spike-triggered average is now a function of three variables, $C(x,y,\tau)$ (equation 2.21), and the optimal kernel $D(x,y,\tau)$ is the space-time receptive field. When it factors as $D(x,y,\tau) = D_s(x,y)D_t(\tau)$ the receptive field is separable — its spatial shape stays fixed while only its sign and amplitude evolve in time.

{{fig:2.10|A cat V1 simple cell's spatial receptive field, measured, next to a Gabor function fit to it}}

The spatial part of a simple cell's receptive field is fit by a Gabor function (equation 2.27): a Gaussian envelope of width $\sigma_x, \sigma_y$ times a cosine grating of preferred spatial frequency $k$ and phase $\phi$. Realistic values cluster around $k\sigma_x$ between 1.7 and 6.9, reported as a bandwidth in octaves (0.5 to 2.5) — low bandwidth means more subregions and sharper spatial-frequency tuning. The point of fitting a Gabor rather than just plotting the measured field is that it hands you the knobs: rotate $\theta$ for orientation preference, shift $k$ for spatial frequency preference, and the overlap between ON/OFF subregions and a grating's light/dark bands (figure 2.11) tells you, by eye, why mismatched orientation or phase produces a weak response.

{{fig:2.11|A grating aligned with a receptive field's ON/OFF subregions, versus one shifted out of phase or rotated off-orientation}}

The temporal part, $D_t(\tau)$ (equation 2.29, figure 2.14), rises, crosses zero, and decays — so the spatial pattern a cell prefers at $\tau=75$ ms is sign-reversed from what it preferred at $\tau=210$ ms.

{{fig:2.13|The same spatial receptive field, shown at six different time lags, reversing sign between them}}

{{fig:2.17|A separable receptive field plotted as a single space-time (x-τ) image, with the sign reversal now visible as a diagonal structure}}

This reversal is exactly why a flashed static bar is a weak stimulus for these cells (it straddles both an ON and the later-reversed OFF phase, and the two nearly cancel) while a moving or flashed-then-removed bar is strong. A separable receptive field tuned this way responds well to motion at a preferred speed but equally to both directions — it cannot report *which* way the grating moved, because $D_s$ and $D_t$ never mix.

{{fig:2.21|A direction-selective V1 cell's space-time receptive field: a tilted structure, not separable into a space part times a time part}}

Direction selectivity needs the nonseparable case, built (equation 2.35) by rotating the $x$-$\tau$ axes against each other by an angle $\psi$ before applying the Gabor and temporal functions separately — the receptive field's spatial subregions now visibly drift sideways as $\tau$ changes, and the preferred velocity is $c\tan(\psi)$.

## Throwing away phase

Complex cells cannot be fit this way at all — their ON and OFF regions overlap, so no linear spike-triggered average separates them cleanly. What *does* survive is orientation and spatial-frequency tuning; what disappears is spatial-phase tuning, and the firing rate to a counterphase grating acquires a response component at $2\omega$ instead of $\omega$ — frequency doubling.

{{fig:2.23|A model complex cell's tuning to orientation and spatial frequency, holding up, against its tuning to spatial phase, flattening out}}

The repair is the "energy model": take two simple-cell-like linear filters with preferred phases $\phi$ and $\phi - \pi/2$ (a quadrature pair), compute $L_1$ and $L_2$ from each, and combine as $L_1^2 + L_2^2$ (equations 2.39–2.42). The trigonometric identity $\cos^2(\phi-\Phi) + \sin^2(\phi-\Phi) = 1$ is what cancels the phase dependence; work through equations 2.39 and 2.40 by hand to see exactly where $\Phi$ drops out, and what $2\omega$ in equation 2.43 has to do with the squaring. The chapter also gives a biologically fussier version, equation 2.44, built from four half-wave-rectified simple cells instead of two full-wave outputs — worth comparing against 2.41 to see that it is the same answer reached without ever squaring a negative number.

## Center, surround, and their mismatched clocks

Retinal ganglion cells and LGN relay neurons are fit by a difference of Gaussians (equation 2.45): a narrow, strong center Gaussian ($\sigma_{\text{cen}}$) minus a broad, weaker surround ($\sigma_{\text{sur}} > \sigma_{\text{cen}}$, scaled by $B$).

{{fig:2.25|An LGN cell's measured spatial receptive field, center and surround, fit by a difference of two Gaussians}}

Figure 2.25's fitted values — $\sigma_{\text{cen}} = 0.3°$, $\sigma_{\text{sur}} = 1.5°$, $B = 5$ — are real numbers to compute with, not just illustration: integrate $D_s$ over a disk the size of the center alone, then over a disk covering center and surround together, and compare. Center and surround also run on different clocks in time ($\alpha_{\text{cen}} \ne \alpha_{\text{sur}}$ in equation 2.47, with the surround's reversal lagging the center's), which is why this receptive field — unlike the V1 cells above — is nonseparable even though center and surround are each separable on their own.

## A wiring sketch, not a mechanism

Hubel and Wiesel's 1962 proposal closes the chapter: sum rows of ON-center and OFF-center LGN inputs to build an oriented simple-cell field (figure 2.27A), then sum several simple cells of matching orientation but staggered phase to build a complex cell (figure 2.27B) — an alternative to squaring that reaches the same phase-invariant result by addition instead. Both are explicitly flagged as "purely descriptive"; the chapter does not claim this is how V1 is actually wired, only that it is wiring that *would* produce the receptive fields already measured.
