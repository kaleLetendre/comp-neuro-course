## The membrane as a leaky capacitor

A neuron's cytoplasm and the extracellular fluid are both good conductors, dense with ions. What separates them is a lipid bilayer 3–4 nm thick, embedded with ion channels about 10 nm long.

{{fig:5.1|A schematic diagram of a section of the lipid bilayer with two ion channels embedded in it}}

The bilayer is almost impermeable to charge, so it behaves like a capacitor: charge of one sign accumulates on the inner face, the opposite sign on the outer face, a few nanometres apart. The channels puncture this insulator and let current leak across it — hence "leaky" membrane, not perfect capacitor.

Before working through specific currents, note the natural voltage scale. Equating the thermal energy of a mole of ions, $RT \approx 2500$ J/mol, to the electrical work of moving a mole of charge across a potential $V_T$ gives

$$V_T = \frac{RT}{F} = \frac{k_BT}{q} \approx 24\text{–}27\ \text{mV at biological temperatures.}$$

Membrane potentials span roughly $-3V_T$ to $+2V_T$ — the observed $-90$ to $+50$ mV range. Every exponential that follows in this chapter, from gating rate functions to the Nernst equation, has $V_T$ in its denominator for this reason.

## Resistance, capacitance, and the time constant

Current flowing along a dendrite or axon meets resistance from the cytoplasm. For a cylindrical segment of length $L$ and radius $a$,

$$R_L = \frac{r_L L}{\pi a^2}, \qquad r_L \approx 1\text{–}3\ \text{k}\Omega\!\cdot\!\text{mm}.$$

{{fig:5.2|The longitudinal resistance of a cylindrical segment of neuronal cable with length L and radius a}}

A segment 100 µm long and 2 µm in radius has $R_L \approx 8\ \text{M}\Omega$, a non-trivial drop for small currents — why electrotonic compactness matters once multi-compartment models appear in chapter 6. A compact neuron, one potential, no spatial structure, reduces to a capacitor in parallel with a resistor.

{{fig:5.3|The capacitance and membrane resistance of a neuron considered as a single compartment}}

The specific membrane capacitance is nearly universal, $c_m \approx 10\ \text{nF/mm}^2$ — the same number as the more commonly quoted $1\ \mu\text{F/cm}^2$. The specific membrane resistance, by contrast, is highly variable, typically $r_m \approx 1\ \text{M}\Omega\!\cdot\!\text{mm}^2$, shifting with channel state. $C_m$ and $R_m$ scale with surface area $A$ in opposite directions ($C_m = c_m A$, $R_m = r_m/A$), so their product, the membrane time constant,

$$\tau_m = R_m C_m = r_m c_m,$$

is independent of cell size, typically 10–100 ms. This is the first number worth computing by hand: given $c_m$ and a leak conductance $g_L = 1/r_m$, $\tau_m$ follows directly, and doubling $g_L$ halves $\tau_m$ — the membrane responds to current faster, but also forgets it faster. Separately, $C_m\, dV/dt$ is the current needed to *change* $V$ at a given rate (1 nA moves a 1 nF cell at 1 mV/ms); $I_eR_m$ is the current needed to *hold* $V$ away from rest — two different questions, two different circuit elements.

## Equilibrium potentials and the Nernst equation

Ions cross channels under two competing influences: the electric field set up by the membrane potential, and diffusion down a concentration gradient maintained by ion pumps. The equilibrium potential for a single ion species is the voltage at which these cancel — net flux through that channel is zero. Balancing the Boltzmann factor for electrical bias against the concentration ratio gives the Nernst equation,

$$E = \frac{V_T}{z}\ln\frac{[\text{outside}]}{[\text{inside}]}.$$

Note the direction of the ratio — outside over inside — and that $z$ carries the sign of the ion's charge. Typical values: $E_K$ between $-70$ and $-90$ mV, $E_{Na}$ at $+50$ mV or above, $E_{Ca}$ near $+150$ mV, $E_{Cl}$ around $-60$ to $-65$ mV, close to rest for many cells. A channel's reversal potential sets the direction it pushes $V$: $Na^+$ and $Ca^{2+}$ conductances depolarize, $K^+$ conductances hyperpolarize, and $Cl^-$ conductances, sitting near rest, mostly add conductance ("shunting") rather than driving $V$ anywhere. The arithmetic for a specific ion and temperature is left as an exercise — run it from scratch rather than trust the typical-value list above.

When a channel is not perfectly selective, a single Nernst potential no longer applies; the resulting mixed potential is a reversal potential rather than an equilibrium potential, estimated with the Goldman equation, which this course does not derive.

## The membrane current and single-compartment models

Each channel type $i$ contributes a current proportional to its own driving force, $i_m = \sum_i g_i(V - E_i)$, where $g_i$ is conductance per unit membrane area. Everything *not* voltage- or ligand-gated — the steady background set by ion pumps — is folded into a single leakage term $\bar g_L(V - E_L)$, with $E_L$ a free parameter rather than any one ion's true equilibrium potential.

Differentiating $Q = C_mV$ and dividing through by area turns current balance into the single governing equation for any single-compartment model:

$$c_m\frac{dV}{dt} = -i_m + \frac{I_e}{A}.$$

{{fig:5.4|The equivalent circuit for a one-compartment neuron model}}

This is Kirchhoff's current law as a differential equation: capacitive current plus ionic current equals injected current. Everything from here — a passive leaky cell, a full Hodgkin-Huxley axon — is this one equation with $i_m$ written out in more or less detail. The *leaky integrate-and-fire* simplification, reducing $i_m$ to the leak term alone and hand-coding a threshold-and-reset in place of the spike, is the subject of the next lecture. What follows here is the opposite choice: write $i_m$ out in full, so the spike falls out of the dynamics on its own.

## From one channel to a population: the open-probability formalism

Current through a single channel does not vary smoothly — it switches between a fixed open-state value and zero, stochastically.

{{fig:5.7|Recording of the current passing through a single ion channel}}

A patch of membrane, though, contains thousands to millions of channels of a given type. If they gate independently — a good approximation — the law of large numbers converts single-channel stochasticity into a smooth macroscopic conductance: $g_i = \bar g_i P_i$, where $\bar g_i$ is the maximal conductance (all channels open) and $P_i$ is the fraction open at any instant, numerically equal to one channel's open probability. A deterministic $P_i$ standing in for an average over a large population of individually stochastic channels is the hinge the whole Hodgkin-Huxley formalism turns on — worth being explicit about what is averaged over what.

Two channel architectures recur. A **persistent** conductance has one kind of gate; built from $k$ identical, independent subunits that must all open, $P = n^k$, $n$ being one subunit's open probability — the *activation variable*.

{{fig:5.8|Gating of membrane channels: a persistent conductance (A) and a transient conductance (B)}}

A **transient** conductance adds a second, independent gate that can block the pore even when the first is open: $P = m^k h$, $m$ the activation variable, $h$ the *inactivation* variable. Critically, $m$ and $h$ have opposite voltage dependence — depolarization grows $m$ but shrinks $h$ — making the conductance transient rather than merely slow.

Each gating variable obeys first-order kinetics, closed $\rightleftharpoons$ open at voltage-dependent rates $\alpha(V)$ and $\beta(V)$:

$$\frac{dn}{dt} = \alpha_n(V)(1-n) - \beta_n(V)n = \frac{n_\infty(V) - n}{\tau_n(V)}.$$

Thermodynamic arguments (an energy barrier proportional to charge moved times voltage) predict exponential forms for $\alpha$ and $\beta$, and hence a sigmoidal $n_\infty(V)$ — but Hodgkin and Huxley's own fits, obtained by voltage-clamping the squid axon and reading off $\alpha$ and $\beta$ at each holding potential, depart from the clean exponential for $\alpha_n$ while matching it for $\beta_n$.

{{fig:5.9|Generic voltage-dependent gating functions compared with Hodgkin-Huxley results for the delayed-rectifier K+ conductance}}

{{fig:5.10|The voltage-dependent functions of the Hodgkin-Huxley model: steady-state activation/inactivation and their time constants}}

One exception: some channels are gated by inactivation alone and open on *hyperpolarization* rather than depolarization — hyperpolarization-activated conductances, which "deinactivate" to turn on.

## The Hodgkin-Huxley model

Writing $i_m$ for a squid axon with a leak, a delayed-rectifier $K^+$ conductance ($n^4$, persistent), and a fast $Na^+$ conductance ($m^3h$, transient) gives the full model:

$$i_m = \bar g_L(V-E_L) + \bar g_K n^4(V-E_K) + \bar g_{Na}m^3h(V-E_{Na}),$$

with standard parameters $\bar g_L = 0.003$, $\bar g_K = 0.36$, $\bar g_{Na} = 1.2$ mS/mm², $E_L = -54.387$ mV, $E_K = -77$ mV, $E_{Na} = 50$ mV. Four coupled ODEs — $V$ plus three gating variables — reproduce the action potential with no explicit threshold rule.

{{fig:5.11|The dynamics of V, m, h, and n in the Hodgkin-Huxley model during the firing of an action potential}}

Trace the sequence against figure 5.11. A small depolarizing current nudges $V$ toward $-50$ mV. Because $m$'s kinetics are fast, it jumps toward 1 almost immediately, while $h$, starting near 0.6, has not yet had time to respond — so for a brief window both gating factors in $m^3h$ are substantial, $Na^+$ floods in, and $V$ rises toward $E_{Na}$. This rise is self-reinforcing: depolarization raises $m$, which raises $Na^+$ conductance, which depolarizes further. Two slower processes arrest it. First, the *same* depolarization that opened $m$ is, with a lag, closing the $Na^+$ channel from the other end — ask what $h$ is doing on the falling side of the rise, and what that does to $m^3h$ overall. Second, depolarization has also been driving $n$ upward throughout, and $K^+$ current grows with a delay relative to $Na^+$ current; only once it is large does $V$ get driven back down past rest.

That delay does more work than it looks. Consider why a neuron cannot simply repolarize and fire again on the heels of the first spike: as $V$ returns toward rest, $n$ is still elevated and $h$ has not yet recovered from inactivation, so the conditions needed to retrigger the positive-feedback loop — low $n$, high $h$ — are not yet met. The refractory period is not a rule imposed on the model; it is a consequence of how fast each gate recovers relative to how fast $V$ moves. The same reasoning explains "all-or-none": below some injected current, $m$'s rise is too slow or small to outrun $n$'s rise, the positive feedback never takes hold, and $V$ settles back without a spike. There is no threshold parameter anywhere in equation 5.25 — "threshold" describes where this balance of rates tips, not an ingredient fed into the model.

## Channels as discrete state machines

The gating-variable formalism is a mean-field description. The chapter also gives the microscopic picture behind it: a channel as a state diagram, carrying current $gP(V-E)$ where $P$ is 1 in the single open state and 0 elsewhere, with transitions between states happening stochastically at rates read off the diagram.

{{fig:5.12|A model of the delayed-rectifier K+ channel: state diagram and simulated single-channel, 10-channel, and 100-channel currents}}

For the $K^+$ channel, the five states correspond to 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 open subunit gates, with transition rates built from $\alpha_n$, $\beta_n$, and combinatorial factors (four ways to open the first gate, one way to close the last). Summing 1, 10, and 100 simulated channels shows the deterministic Hodgkin-Huxley prediction emerging as the ensemble grows — the law of large numbers in action, not assumed.

{{fig:5.13|A model of the fast Na+ channel, with a state-dependent rather than voltage-dependent inactivation step}}

The $Na^+$ channel model disagrees with Hodgkin-Huxley in a telling way. Its inactivation step is triggered by the channel having already reached an open *state*, at voltage-independent rates, rather than by voltage directly — matching the biological picture in figure 5.8B, where the inactivating "ball" cannot block the pore until the activation gate has swung open. Summed over many channels, this state-dependent model still reproduces the Hodgkin-Huxley current almost exactly, because $m$ relaxes to $m_\infty(V)$ so quickly that "ready to inactivate," voltage-based or state-based, is nearly indistinguishable in a real time course. Only sub-millisecond resolution exposes the difference, and the experiments able to see that timescale favor the state-dependent model over Hodgkin and Huxley's original assumption that activation and inactivation act independently.
