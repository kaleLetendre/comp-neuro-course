**Grader key (teacher AI only — verify against the physical text before scoring; do not treat the summary below as a quotation)**

Expected content, to check the learner's paraphrase against, not to hand out:
1. Entropy H(R) = -sum_r P(r) log2 P(r); bits because log base 2; one bit = the information from a binary choice with two equally likely outcomes.
2. I(R;S) = H(R) - H(R|S) (equivalently H(S) - H(S|R)); the text's own worked example is a small discrete table analogous to the one used in Piece 1 here — check the learner reports numeric values consistent with correctly applying the formula to whatever table D&A actually uses, not necessarily matching Piece 1's numbers.
3. Symmetry (I(R;S) = I(S;R)) and the fact that MI makes no assumption about how the response is decoded — it upper-bounds every possible decoder, whereas an accuracy metric depends on the decoder chosen and can understate the channel's true information content.
4. Infomax: choose the encoding/response distribution to maximize H(R) subject to constraints (fixed range or fixed number of levels); the maximizing distribution, for a fixed number of discrete equally-spaced levels, is the one with as close to uniform probability across levels as the constraint allows.
5. The entropy-maximizing input/output curve is (proportional to) the cumulative distribution function of the stimulus — i.e., the optimal nonlinearity is the stimulus's own CDF, producing a uniform output distribution (histogram equalization).
6. For natural images (spatially correlated input), infomax via redundancy reduction predicts decorrelating filters — center-surround (difference-of-Gaussians-like) receptive fields that whiten the correlated input.
7. Direct method: bin spike train into small time bins, group L consecutive bins into a "word"; compute the total response entropy rate from the word distribution over the whole recording, and the noise entropy rate from the word distribution conditioned on (repeats of) the same stimulus; information rate = total entropy rate - noise entropy rate.
8. The noise entropy rate needs repeated presentations of the identical stimulus, because it measures the residual (trial-to-trial) variability in the response given a fixed stimulus — that requires multiple trials with the same stimulus to observe the response's spread.
9. Entropy estimates from finite data are downward biased — the plug-in/naive estimate computed from empirical word frequencies underestimates the true entropy, more severely as the recording (hence sample count) shrinks, because the empirical distribution under-samples rare words; the usual fix is extrapolating estimates from progressively larger data fractions to infinite data.

## Piece 1 — Entropy and mutual information on a worked table

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** ~60 min

**Learning goals**
- Build a full joint probability table from marginal and conditional probabilities and compute H(S), H(R), H(R|S), H(S|R) from it, in bits.
- Compute I(R;S) two independent ways (via entropy differences, and via the direct joint-distribution sum) and show they agree.
- State, with this example's actual numbers, why I(R;S) = I(S;R).
- Explain what that symmetry rules out about equating "information carried" with "decoding accuracy."

**Task**

Two equally likely stimuli s1, s2 drive a neuron's response, which takes one of two values r1, r2 (e.g. "response above/below some threshold count in a fixed window"). The channel is:

P(s1) = 0.5, P(s2) = 0.5
P(r1 | s1) = 0.8, P(r2 | s1) = 0.2
P(r1 | s2) = 0.3, P(r2 | s2) = 0.7

1. Build the full joint table P(s,r) (4 entries).
2. Compute the marginals P(r1), P(r2), and H(S), H(R) in bits.
3. Compute H(R|S) as the probability-weighted average of the conditional entropy given each stimulus (you may use the binary entropy function H_b(p) = -p log2 p - (1-p) log2(1-p) as a shortcut, but show at least one H_b(p) expanded from the log2 definition by hand).
4. Compute I(R;S) = H(R) - H(R|S).
5. Recompute I(R;S) directly from the joint table using I(R;S) = sum_{s,r} P(s,r) log2 [P(s,r) / (P(s)P(r))], and confirm it matches step 4 to 3 decimal places.
6. Compute H(S|R) and I(S;R) = H(S) - H(S|R), and confirm I(S;R) = I(R;S).
7. In 3-5 sentences: why is this equality forced by the formula in step 5 (look at what happens if you swap the labels s and r in that sum), and what does it rule out about using a "decoder's percent-correct" as a stand-in for how much information the channel carries?

**Deliverable**

A worked sheet (handwritten or typed) with all four table cells, both entropy calculations, both routes to I(R;S) shown side by side, the H(S|R) check, and the written answer to step 7.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All values correct to 3 decimals, both routes to I(R;S) shown and reconciled explicitly, symmetry demonstrated numerically (not asserted), and step 7 names the specific formula symmetry and the decoder-independence consequence with a concrete failure mode for accuracy-as-information. |
| Adequate | Joint table and entropies correct; one of the two I(R;S) routes present but not both reconciled, or step 7 states the conclusion without tracing it to the swapped-label argument. |
| Not yet | Joint table has an arithmetic error propagating through the rest, or I(R;S) computed only one way, or step 7 is a restatement of "MI is symmetric" with no explanation of why or what it rules out. |

**Grader key (teacher AI only)**

Joint table: P(s1,r1)=0.40, P(s1,r2)=0.10, P(s2,r1)=0.15, P(s2,r2)=0.35.
Marginals: P(r1)=0.55, P(r2)=0.45.
H(S) = 1.000 bits (exactly, since P(s1)=P(s2)=0.5).
H(R) = -0.55·log2(0.55) - 0.45·log2(0.45) = 0.4744 + 0.5184 = 0.9928 bits.
H_b(0.2) = 0.7219 bits (this is H(R|s1)); H_b(0.3) = 0.8813 bits (this is H(R|s2)).
H(R|S) = 0.5·0.7219 + 0.5·0.8813 = 0.8016 bits.
I(R;S) = H(R) - H(R|S) = 0.9928 - 0.8016 = 0.1912 bits.
Direct sum check: terms are 0.4·log2(0.4/0.275)=+0.2162, 0.1·log2(0.1/0.225)=-0.1170, 0.15·log2(0.15/0.275)=-0.1312, 0.35·log2(0.35/0.225)=+0.2231; sum = 0.1911 bits (matches step-4 value to rounding).
H(S|R) = H(S) - I(S;R); since I(S;R)=I(R;S)=0.1912, H(S|R) = 1.000 - 0.1912 = 0.8088 bits.
Accept anything in the range 0.190-0.192 bits for I(R;S) as correct (rounding path-dependence).
Step 7 expected content: the sum in step 5 is symmetric under exchanging (s,r) labels because P(s,r)=P(r,s) and P(s)P(r)=P(r)P(s) — the formula does not privilege either variable, so I(R;S)=I(S;R) is forced by construction, not a coincidence of these numbers. Consequence: any "goodness of decoding" metric (e.g. percent of trials correctly classified by some specific decoding rule) is decoder-dependent — a poor decoder can make the same channel look uninformative, and a different decoder on the same data gives a different accuracy — so accuracy cannot be a stand-in for the channel's actual information content, which is a fixed property of the joint distribution and upper-bounds every decoder's performance including the best possible one.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: If stuck on the joint table, ask "what is P(s1) times P(r1|s1)?" — do not compute the cell yourself.
- Hint 2: If stuck on H(R|S), point to the binary entropy function shortcut and ask them to expand one term (e.g. H_b(0.2)) by hand to confirm they can derive it, not just plug into a formula.
- Hint 3: If the two I(R;S) routes disagree by more than rounding, ask them to recheck the joint table cells sum to 1 and the marginals were computed by summing the right axis.
- Never: give any of the numeric entropy, joint-table, or MI values before the learner has submitted their own attempt at that specific step.

## Piece 2 — Simulation: estimator bias and infomax in code

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** ~75-90 min

**Learning goals**
- Implement the direct method's word-binning entropy-rate estimator and observe it underestimate a known ground-truth rate.
- Connect the direction of that bias (always downward, worse with less data) to Jensen's inequality applied to the concave entropy function.
- Implement the infomax-optimal nonlinearity (CDF matching) for a non-uniform stimulus and confirm it flattens the output distribution.

**Task**

Run the script below unmodified first; then answer the questions after it.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def h_b(p):
    """Binary entropy in bits."""
    if p <= 0 or p >= 1:
        return 0.0
    return -p * np.log2(p) - (1 - p) * np.log2(1 - p)

def direct_method_rate(spikes, L, dt):
    """Total-entropy rate of a binary spike train via the direct method:
    bin into words of length L bins, estimate H(word) from empirical
    word frequencies, divide by word duration."""
    n_words = len(spikes) // L
    words = spikes[: n_words * L].reshape(n_words, L)
    codes = words.dot(1 << np.arange(L))
    counts = np.bincount(codes, minlength=2 ** L)
    probs = counts[counts > 0] / counts.sum()
    H_word = -np.sum(probs * np.log2(probs))
    return H_word / (L * dt), n_words

# --- Part A: entropy-rate estimate is downward biased as recording shrinks ---
p_spike, dt, L, n_repeats = 0.1, 0.001, 8, 40
true_rate = h_b(p_spike) / dt  # bins are i.i.d., so true rate is exact

print("Part A -- direct method vs recording length")
print(f"true rate = {true_rate:.2f} bits/s\n")
for T in [500.0, 50.0, 5.0, 0.5]:
    n_bins = int(T / dt)
    ests = [direct_method_rate((rng.random(n_bins) < p_spike).astype(int), L, dt)[0]
            for _ in range(n_repeats)]
    ests = np.array(ests)
    print(f"T={T:>6.1f}s  mean estimate={ests.mean():7.2f} bits/s  "
          f"bias={ests.mean() - true_rate:7.2f} bits/s  std={ests.std():5.2f}")

# --- Part B: infomax histogram equalization ---
def hist_entropy(x, edges):
    counts, _ = np.histogram(x, bins=edges)
    p = counts / counts.sum()
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

n, bins = 200_000, 20
s = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=n)          # non-uniform stimulus
sorted_s = np.sort(s)
r = np.searchsorted(sorted_s, s, side="right") / n   # nonlinearity = own CDF

H_in = hist_entropy(s, np.linspace(0, np.percentile(s, 99.9), bins + 1))
H_out = hist_entropy(r, np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1))
H_max = np.log2(bins)

print("\nPart B -- histogram equalization")
print(f"H_max = log2({bins}) = {H_max:.4f} bits")
print(f"H(input hist)  = {H_in:.4f} bits")
print(f"H(output hist) = {H_out:.4f} bits")
```

Then answer:

1. Report your printed numbers for Part A. Is the bias (mean estimate - true rate) ever positive? Does its magnitude grow monotonically as T shrinks?
2. The plug-in entropy estimator computed from empirical word frequencies satisfies E[H(p_hat)] <= H(E[p_hat]) = H(p_true), by Jensen's inequality, because entropy is a concave function of the probability vector. Explain in your own words why this inequality forces the *direction* of the bias you observed (always <= true, never >), and why smaller T (fewer words) makes the gap larger.
3. In Part B, why is H(output hist) close to H_max while H(input hist) is not? Which specific property of the transform `r = searchsorted(sorted_s, s) / n` makes this happen?
4. Part B used a fixed number of bins (20) for both histograms. Relate this to criterion 3's infomax constraint ("fixed number of output levels") — what would you expect to happen to H(output hist) if you doubled `bins` to 40 without changing `n`? (You don't have to run it — reason from the definition of H_max.)

**Deliverable**

Your script's console output for both parts, plus written answers to the four questions above (total ~150-250 words for the prose parts).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Ran the code, correctly reports monotonically growing bias magnitude with T (in expectation over repeats), explains the Jensen argument correctly including why concavity implies E[H_hat] <= H_true (not just "less data is noisier"), correctly identifies the CDF-transform property, and correctly reasons that H_max would rise to log2(40) but H(output hist) would still track close to it only if `n` is large enough relative to 40 bins. |
| Adequate | Ran the code and reports correct numbers; Jensen explanation gestures at "biased low" without connecting it to concavity of H; bins question answered correctly by rote (H_max=log2(40)) without noting the sample-size caveat. |
| Not yet | Did not run the code / reports numbers inconsistent with a downward-biased, shrinking-with-T pattern; Jensen question answered as "more data is more accurate" with no mention of concavity or the direction of bias. |

**Grader key (teacher AI only — reference numbers from running the exact script above with NumPy 2.2.4, seed 0; treat the qualitative pattern — monotonically growing negative bias as T shrinks, output entropy close to log2(20) — as the pass bar, not exact digit matching, since RNG stream details can differ slightly across NumPy versions)**

Part A: true rate = 469.00 bits/s (= H_b(0.1)/dt = 0.4690/0.001).
- T=500.0s: mean estimate=468.90 bits/s, bias=-0.10, std=1.28
- T=50.0s: mean estimate=464.92 bits/s, bias=-4.07, std=4.16
- T=5.0s: mean estimate=449.97 bits/s, bias=-19.03, std=14.30
- T=0.5s: mean estimate=385.96 bits/s, bias=-83.04, std=34.90

Bias is negative at every T tested and grows monotonically in magnitude as T shrinks (this requires averaging over repeats — a single run at small T can show noisy non-monotonic behavior due to high variance at n_words=62; if the learner ran without averaging and got a confusing single-run result, point them at `n_repeats`).

Part B: H_max = log2(20) = 4.3219 bits. H(input hist) = 2.9640 bits. H(output hist) = 4.3219 bits (matches H_max, i.e. exactly flat within histogram resolution).

Q2 expected content: Jensen's inequality for a concave function f gives E[f(X)] <= f(E[X]). Here f = H (concave in the probability vector) and X = the empirical distribution p_hat, whose expectation over resampling equals the true p (empirical frequencies are unbiased estimators of the true cell probabilities). So E[H(p_hat)] <= H(p) always, for any sample size — the bias can never be positive in expectation. As the word count n_words shrinks, p_hat's sampling variance around p grows, and because H is strictly concave, larger deviations of p_hat from p pull H(p_hat) down more (the concavity "curves away" from the true value faster the further you are from it) — so the expected gap H(p) - E[H(p_hat)] grows as n_words shrinks.

Q3 expected content: the transform maps each sample to its own rank/n, i.e. its empirical CDF value — by construction, the empirical CDF of any continuous sample is (up to discretization) uniformly distributed on [0,1], so binning it into equal-width bins on [0,1] gives equal counts per bin, i.e. maximum entropy for that bin count.

Q4 expected content: H_max would become log2(40)=5.3219 bits; H(output hist) would still track close to that new H_max as long as n=200,000 remains large relative to 40 bins (average ~5000 samples/bin), since the CDF-transform argument in Q3 doesn't depend on bin count — but if bins were increased far enough relative to n, undersampling would reintroduce the Part-A-style downward bias.
