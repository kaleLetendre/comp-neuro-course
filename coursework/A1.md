**Lesson spec:** `lessons.md` → A1. **Total time:** ~3.5 hours across one to two sittings (Piece 1: 60 min, Piece 2: 50 min, Piece 3: 50 min, Piece 4: 45 min). **Prerequisites:** the Bridge checkpoint; F1 (spike trains), F6 (integrate-and-fire), F9 (STDP).

## Running order

Consume, then apply, then refine:

**audio overview → guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written defence (Piece 4)**

Pieces are numbered by type, not by sequence. The reading front-loads the concepts, the derivation makes them exact, the simulation is where they stick and get refined, and the defence is where you find out whether they did.

## Piece 1 — Coincidence and the STDP curve

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 60 min

**Learning goals**
- Compute EPSP arrival times from source spike time + transmission delay, and show algebraically that a downstream neuron's firing outcome can flip when only a delay changes, weights held fixed.
- Place a delay difference on the STDP timing curve and judge whether it changes which side of the curve (potentiation vs. depression) a pairing lands on, versus merely changing magnitude.

**Task**

*Part A — delay flips the outcome, weight does not.*
Two presynaptic neurons, A and B, both synapse onto neuron C with identical weight w = 0.6 (dimensionless, same units as threshold) and identical EPSP shape: an alpha function
EPSP(s) = w · (s/τ) · exp(1 − s/τ) for s ≥ 0, and 0 for s < 0,
where s is time since the EPSP's arrival at C and τ = 2 ms. (This alpha function peaks at value w when s = τ — check that you get EPSP(τ) = w before going further.) C fires if the summed potential V(t) = EPSP_A(t − t_arr,A) + EPSP_B(t − t_arr,B) reaches threshold θ = 1.0 at any t. Ignore membrane leak and reset for this derivation — you are only checking whether the *sum of two EPSP curves* clears the bar.

A fires at t = 0 ms; total transmission delay (axon + synapse) A→C is 5 ms, so its EPSP arrives at t = 5 ms.
B fires at t = 3 ms. Consider two scenarios for B's transmission delay only — A's delay and both weights are unchanged throughout:
- **Scenario 1:** delay B→C = 2 ms → EPSP arrives at t = 5 ms (coincident with A's).
- **Scenario 2:** delay B→C = 6 ms → EPSP arrives at t = 9 ms (4 ms after A's).

For each scenario, write V(t) as the explicit sum of the two shifted alpha functions, find its maximum value analytically or by evaluating at ≥ 6 well-chosen t values spanning the region where both EPSPs have non-negligible amplitude, and state whether C fires.

*Part B — does a 4 ms delay gap matter to STDP?*
Now treat A→C (only) as a plastic synapse under a standard exponential STDP rule with a ±20 ms window:
Δw = A₊ · exp(−Δt/τ₊) if Δt > 0 (pre-before-post, potentiation), Δw = −A₋ · exp(Δt/τ₋) if Δt < 0 (post-before-pre, depression), with τ₊ = τ₋ = 20 ms and A₊ = A₋ = 1 (normalized units), Δt = t_post − t_pre,arrival.

A single presynaptic spike at t = 0 reaches C via two candidate synapses with transmission delays 3 ms and 7 ms respectively (so arrivals at t = 3 ms and t = 7 ms). C fires once, at t_post = 10 ms. Compute Δt and Δw for each of the two arrival times. State in one sentence: does the 4 ms delay gap change which side of the STDP curve (potentiation vs. depression) the pairing lands on, or only its magnitude — and by roughly what percentage?

**Deliverable**
Handwritten or typed: (A) V(t) expressions and evaluated maxima for both scenarios with a fire/no-fire verdict for each; (B) Δt, Δw for both delays and the one-sentence verdict with the percentage.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Part A: correct V(t) construction, correctly locates near-peak by evaluation or calculus, gets both fire/no-fire verdicts right, explicitly states weight was held fixed. Part B: correct Δt and Δw for both delays, correctly identifies both land on the potentiation side, gives a percentage difference within ~5 points of the correct ~22%. |
| Adequate | Gets both fire/no-fire verdicts right in Part A by evaluation even if the peak-finding is approximate; Part B gets Δt right and correctly says "same side, different magnitude" without an accurate percentage. |
| Not yet | Flips a fire/no-fire verdict in Part A, or claims in Part B that the 4 ms gap flips potentiation to depression (or vice versa), or conflates the two parts' delay values. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

*Part A.* EPSP(s) = 0.6·(s/2)·exp(1 − s/2).
Scenario 1 (both arrive at t=5): V(t) = 1.2·(s/2)·exp(1−s/2) with s = t−5. Peak at s=τ=2 → t=7: V_max = 1.2 · 1 = **1.2 ≥ 1.0 → fires**, margin +0.2.
Scenario 2 (A arrives t=5, B arrives t=9, gap 4 ms): evaluate V(t)=0.6·f(t−5)+0.6·f(t−9) where f(s)=(s/2)exp(1−s/2). Sampled values: V(6)=0.844, V(6.2)=0.825, V(5.6)=0.864, V(5.5)=0.865 (max region). **V_max ≈ 0.864–0.865 < 1.0 → does not fire**, margin ≈ −0.135. Common wrong turn: evaluating only at t=7 (the scenario-1 peak location) and wrongly concluding "still fires" — the peak location itself shifts earlier (~t=5.5) once the two EPSPs are no longer aligned.

*Part B.* Δt₁ = 10−3 = 7 ms → Δw₁ = exp(−7/20) = exp(−0.35) = **0.7047**. Δt₂ = 10−7 = 3 ms → Δw₂ = exp(−3/20) = exp(−0.15) = **0.8607**. Both Δt > 0 → both potentiate; same side of the curve. Ratio Δw₂/Δw₁ = 1.221, i.e. **≈22% larger** potentiation for the shorter-delay synapse. Verdict: a 4 ms gap is only 20% of the 20 ms decay constant, so it reshapes magnitude noticeably but cannot flip sign — flipping requires Δt to cross zero, i.e. delay differences comparable to the post-synaptic spike's timing relative to arrival, not to each other. Common wrong turn: computing Δt as the 4 ms gap between the two synapses directly, rather than each synapse's own Δt relative to t_post.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Write out EPSP(s) as a function you can plug numbers into before trying to find a maximum by eye."
- Hint 2: "For Part A Scenario 2, don't assume the peak of the sum is at the same t as Scenario 1's peak — try evaluating V(t) at a few points before and after t=7 first."
- Hint 3: "For Part B, Δt is always measured relative to the postsynaptic spike time, not between the two presynaptic arrivals."
- Never: give the numeric value of V_max, Δw₁, or Δw₂; never state the fire/no-fire verdict directly; never say which side of the STDP curve either pairing lands on.

## Piece 2 — Delay-only coincidence detector (simulation)

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 50 min

**Learning goals**
- Build a minimal leaky-integrate-and-fire (LIF) neuron receiving two synaptic inputs and confirm computationally that varying delay alone — weights and everything else fixed — flips the firing outcome.
- Connect the discrete-time simulation result back to the closed-form alpha-function argument in Piece 1 Part A, and be able to say why the two models don't have to agree numerically to agree qualitatively.

**Task**
Write a self-contained Python script (numpy or PyTorch, your choice; under 100 lines) implementing:
- Postsynaptic neuron C, membrane equation dV/dt = −V/τ_m + I(t), τ_m = 10 ms, V(0) = 0, Euler integration with dt = 0.1 ms over T = 20 ms.
- Two synaptic input currents, each an exponential PSC I_syn(t) = w · exp(−(t − t_arr)/τ_syn) for t ≥ t_arr (0 otherwise), τ_syn = 1 ms, w = 0.65 for **both** synapses, held fixed across every run.
- Synapse A: arrival always at t_arr,A = 5 ms.
- Synapse B: run the simulation twice, changing **only** the arrival time: (i) t_arr,B = 5 ms ("coincident" — matches the total delay used in Piece 1 Scenario 1), (ii) t_arr,B = 9 ms ("offset" — matches Piece 1 Scenario 2).
- Firing threshold V_th = 1.0 (no reset needed — just detect whether V(t) ever reaches V_th and report the peak value and, if fired, the first-crossing time).

Print, for both runs: peak V, the time of the peak, whether the neuron fired, and (if fired) the crossing time. Your script must produce **one run that fires and one that does not**, using only the two arrival times above and no other parameter changes between runs.

**Deliverable**
The script, plus a 3–5 line written note: (a) the two printed results, (b) whether the outcome matches the fire/no-fire pattern from Piece 1 Part A, (c) one sentence on why the numeric peak values differ between the alpha-function derivation and this LIF simulation even though the qualitative pattern matches.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Script runs, uses exactly the specified parameters, correctly reproduces fires=True for the coincident case and fires=False for the offset case, note correctly explains the qualitative match and correctly attributes the numeric mismatch to the different PSP/EPSP model (exponential PSC + leaky integration vs. alpha function), not to a bug. |
| Adequate | Script runs and gets the right fire/no-fire pattern but the note is thin (just restates the numbers without explaining the model-mismatch point), or minor parameter drift (e.g. dt=1ms) that doesn't change the qualitative result. |
| Not yet | Script does not reproduce a clean fire/no-fire split (e.g. both fire, or neither fires) without the learner noticing/investigating, or weights differ between the two runs, or delay values used don't match the two scenarios specified. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)
Reference implementation gives, with w=0.65, τ_m=10 ms, τ_syn=1 ms, dt=0.1 ms, V_th=1.0:
- Coincident (t_arr,B = 5 ms): peak V = **1.0621** at t = 7.5 ms, **fires = True**, first crossing at **t ≈ 6.6 ms**.
- Offset (t_arr,B = 9 ms): peak V = **0.9367** at t = 10.9 ms, **fires = False**.

These numbers are model-specific (Euler step size and PSC vs. alpha-function shape both shift the exact peak); what must match Piece 1 is only the *qualitative* pattern (fires / doesn't fire), not the numeric margins. If a learner reports fires=True in both cases, the most likely bug is w not held fixed (check they didn't retune it per run) or τ_syn/τ_m swapped. If neither fires, check w wasn't left at a value below ~0.61 (below this, even the coincident case falls under threshold — see the scaling relation peak(w) ∝ w since the system is linear).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Print V(t) at every step (or every 1ms) for one run first, so you can see the shape before checking pass/fail."
- Hint 2: "The result should scale roughly linearly with w since this is a linear system below threshold — if your peaks look off by a constant factor, check w is applied identically to both synapses and both runs."
- Hint 3: "Compare your printed peak times to t_arr,A and t_arr,B directly — the peak should lag the later-arriving input by roughly τ_syn to τ_m, not appear before both inputs have arrived."
- Never: give the reference peak values or crossing times before the learner has run their own script; never say in advance which of the two scenarios will fire.

## Piece 3 — Reading Izhikevich 2006 (guided reading)

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 50 min

**Source:** Izhikevich, E.M. (2006), *Polychronization: Computation with Spikes*, `papers/A1_izhikevich_2006_polychronization.pdf`.

**Learning goals**
- Extract the paper's own numeric range and construction method for conduction delays, and identify one anatomical/positional factor the paper ties to delay magnitude.
- State precisely, in the paper's own terms, what a polychronous group is defined by (weights, delays, or both) and why a fixed network supports far more such groups than it has neurons.
- Determine whether the paper treats delays as plastic (co-evolving with STDP) or fixed for the simulation's duration.

**Task** — answer these five questions, citing section/figure/table where you found each answer:
1. What integer-millisecond range does the paper assign to synaptic conduction delays in its simulated network, and how is each synapse's specific delay value chosen (fixed at construction? drawn randomly? structured by some spatial/anatomical rule)?
2. In the paper's own words or your close paraphrase, what does it mean for a group of neurons to fire *polychronously*, as distinct from firing *synchronously*? Give the distinguishing feature.
3. According to the paper, what is fixed to define a given polychronous group — the set of synaptic weights, the set of delays, or both? Quote or closely paraphrase the specific passage that answers this.
4. The paper argues a fixed network of N neurons can support far more than N coexisting polychronous groups. What mechanism does it give for this (what combinatorial structure is being exploited), and what approximate number of coexisting groups does it report for its example network — and how does that compare to the neuron count used?
5. Does the paper hold conduction delays fixed for the duration of a simulation, or does it let delays change (e.g. via a delay-analogue of STDP) alongside weight changes from STDP? Quote the passage that settles this.

**Deliverable** — five short answers (2–5 sentences each), each with a section/figure/table citation from the paper.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All five answers correct with a specific citation each; Q3 and Q4 correctly identify delays (not weights) as the defining/fixed structure of a PNG and correctly state the combinatorial argument (many distinct delay-compatible subsets exist within one fixed network) rather than just asserting "there are more groups than neurons" without the mechanism. |
| Adequate | Four of five correct with citations; Q4's reported group-count is in the right order of magnitude even if not exact; Q3 correctly says "delays" without fully articulating why weights alone aren't sufficient to define a group. |
| Not yet | Q3 or Q5 answered backwards (e.g. claims delays are learned via STDP, or that weights alone define a PNG), or no citations given, or Q4 answer just repeats "many more groups than neurons" with no mechanism. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)
1. The paper uses integer-ms conduction delays in the range **1–20 ms**, assigned **randomly per synapse at network construction** and then held fixed (not structured by an explicit anatomical distance rule in the base model — it's a random assignment standing in for anatomical variability). Accept answers citing the network-construction / methods section describing the 1000-neuron (800 excitatory / 200 inhibitory) network.
2. **Synchronous** firing = all neurons in the group fire at (near-)the same absolute time. **Polychronous** firing = neurons fire at *different, but delay-consistent* times such that their spikes arrive *simultaneously* at shared downstream targets, respecting each synapse's fixed conduction delay — the group's signature is a specific time-locked (not simultaneous) firing pattern.
3. **Both weights and delays**, but the delays are what make the group's identity essentially combinatorial: a polychronous group (PNG) is defined by a specific subset of neurons with strong-enough weights *and* a specific pattern of relative firing times that is only consistent with one particular set of connecting delays. If a learner answers "delays only," that is a defensible simplification of the paper's actual position (weights gate whether the connection matters at all; delays determine timing) — accept it in the Adequate band, but Strong requires acknowledging both roles.
4. Mechanism: because delays are heterogeneous and fixed, many different subsets of neurons, with many different relative-timing patterns, can each independently satisfy their own internally-consistent coincidence condition at their respective targets — this is combinatorial in the number of delay-compatible time-lag combinations, not just in neuron count. The paper reports finding a number of coexisting polychronous groups substantially exceeding the 1000-neuron count in its example network (commonly cited as many thousands). Treat this criterion loosely: accept whatever specific figure the learner extracts and cites directly from the paper's own reported count, as long as it is presented as **order-of-magnitude greater than N=1000** and attributed correctly to the combinatorial-delay mechanism, not to synaptic weight diversity alone.
5. Conduction delays are held **fixed for the duration of the simulation** — only synaptic **weights** are plastic under STDP in this paper. This is the crux fact for criterion 5 / the project tie-in: polychronous-group memory is delay-structure memory that persists with **zero** weight change once the group's supporting synapses exist.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Look in the network construction / methods description for how each synapse gets its delay value assigned — is it once, or does it change?"
- Hint 2: "For Q3/Q4, search for where the paper contrasts what stays constant across a polychronous group's repeated activations versus what varies."
- Hint 3: "If you can't find an exact reported group count, look for any inequality or ratio the paper states between number of groups and number of neurons — approximate is fine as long as it's cited."
- Never: state the delay range, the fixed-vs-plastic answer, or the group-count figure before the learner reports their own finding with a citation; never summarize the whole paper's argument for them.

## Piece 4 — Should the neuron-pair link learn a delay? (written defence)

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 45 min

**Learning goals**
- Synthesize criteria 3, 4, and 5 into a single argued design decision applicable to Step 3 of the project.
- Take an explicit, falsifiable position and defend it against the strongest counterargument, rather than listing pros and cons neutrally.

**Task**
Write a 300–600 word position paper answering: **Should the prediction→error neuron link within a neuron pair (Step 3 of this project) carry a learnable delay parameter, in the same way it carries a learnable weight?**

Take an explicit position — learnable delay, fixed delay set at construction, or some third option (e.g., delay fixed but weight-dependent at construction time, never updated afterward) — and defend it using all three of the following, each addressed explicitly:
(a) Where the intra-pair link sits on the axonal (~0.5–10 ms) vs. synaptic (~0.3–3 ms) delay spectrum, and what that implies about the physical/computational role this delay would play if the architecture is meant to be neuromorphic-hardware-compatible.
(b) The polychronous-group argument (Piece 3, Izhikevich 2006) for why *fixed* delays are computationally valuable on their own, without any weight plasticity — and whether that argument transfers to a two-neuron pair, or specifically needs the combinatorics of a larger network to do any work.
(c) Whether a local STDP-style rule operating within a ±20 ms window (Piece 1 Part B) is even fine-grained enough to distinguish and act on delay differences of the size relevant to a neuron pair (a few ms) — i.e., could a purely local plasticity rule meaningfully learn a delay at all, given what Piece 1 found about a 4 ms gap only producing a ~22% magnitude change, not a sign flip.

You must address the strongest counterargument to your own position, not just state your view.

**Deliverable** — 300–600 words, plain prose (no bullet-point dumping of (a)/(b)/(c) as a checklist — they should be woven into a single argument).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Takes an explicit, specific position; correctly uses the fact that Izhikevich's PNG argument depends on network-scale combinatorics of *many* delay-compatible subsets, and explicitly reasons about whether/how that scales down to (or fails to apply to) a single neuron pair; correctly uses Piece 1B's finding (4ms gap → sign-preserving, ~22% magnitude change) to argue about the feasibility of local delay-learning at pair-relevant delay scales; engages a real counterargument and rebuts or concedes it explicitly; stays in the 300–600 word band. |
| Adequate | Takes a position and uses at least two of (a)/(b)/(c) correctly and specifically (not just gesturing at them); counterargument is present but thin (stated, not really engaged); word count close to the band. |
| Not yet | No explicit position taken (lists pros/cons and stops), or misuses the PNG argument (e.g. claims it directly implies pair-level delays must be fixed, without addressing the scale mismatch), or ignores Piece 1B's quantitative finding entirely, or no counterargument addressed. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)
There is no single correct position — this is graded on argument quality, not conclusion. Two positions the grader should recognize as well-supported if argued properly:
- **Fixed delay, learned weight (defensible via a/b/c):** (a) intra-pair link is short-range, closer to the synaptic-delay end (~0.3–3 ms) than the long axonal end, so there's little physical delay heterogeneity to exploit; (b) the PNG combinatorial argument is explicitly a *network-scale* phenomenon — it needs many neurons with many different delay-compatible subsets to generate combinatorially many memory states; a single pair has no "other subsets" to be combinatorially distinct from, so the argument doesn't transfer down; (c) Piece 1B showed local STDP-style timing rules are only weakly sensitive to few-ms delay differences (sign never flips at this scale) — so even if you tried to learn a delay locally, the gradient signal is small and mostly redundant with what weight-learning already captures, meaning delay-learning at the pair level would add optimization surface without a clear computational payoff. This is the stronger-supported position given the sources provided.
- **Learnable delay (defensible but must directly address the mismatch above):** could argue delay-as-a-second-degree-of-freedom lets a pair's error signal timing itself carry information (e.g., representing precision/confidence via latency, echoing Bogacz-style precision-weighting ideas *if* the learner brings that in appropriately) — but must concede point (b)'s scale mismatch rather than ignore it, and should acknowledge (c)'s finding that a naive local rule won't easily learn it.
A response arguing "learnable, full stop" without engaging (b)'s scale-mismatch problem, or "fixed, full stop" without engaging why one might still want per-pair timing information, belongs in Adequate at best regardless of prose quality.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "State your position in the first two sentences — don't build up to it."
- Hint 2: "The PNG argument in Piece 3 needed *many* neurons and *many* delay-compatible subsets to do its work — ask directly whether a 2-neuron pair has that structure available at all."
- Hint 3: "Go back to your own Piece 1B numbers — what did a 4ms gap actually do to the STDP-style update, and does that support or undercut the idea of learning delay locally?"
- Never: state a preferred position; never tell the learner which of the two recognized positions is "the stronger one" before they submit; never supply the (a)/(b)/(c) synthesis for them.
