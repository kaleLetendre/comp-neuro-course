## Piece 1 — Energy monotonicity in a symmetric Hopfield network

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 45-60 min

**Learning goals**
- Derive, from the Hopfield energy function, the exact algebraic identity that links one asynchronous neuron update to the resulting energy change.
- Show numerically that the identity forces ΔE ≤ 0 under the standard update rule when weights are symmetric.
- Show numerically that the guarantee breaks when weights are asymmetric — i.e. show the counterexample, not just assert the theorem.

**Task**

Binary Hopfield network, N=4 units, states s_i ∈ {−1,+1}, energy

E(s) = −½ Σ_i Σ_j w_ij s_i s_j   (i ≠ j, w_ii = 0)

Asynchronous update rule for unit i: s_i ← sign(h_i), h_i = Σ_j w_ij s_j (j≠i, sign(0) = keep current value).

**Part A (general identity).** Show that if w_ij = w_ji for all i,j, then E(s) = −s_i h_i + C, where C does not depend on s_i. Use this to derive ΔE = −(s_i' − s_i)·h_i = −Δs_i·h_i for a single-unit update, and show this quantity is always ≤ 0 given the update rule s_i' = sign(h_i). State the two conditions on W your derivation used.

**Part B (concrete numbers).** Use

```
      j=1  j=2  j=3  j=4
i=1 [  0    1   -1    1 ]
i=2 [  1    0    1   -1 ]
i=3 [ -1    1    0    1 ]
i=4 [  1   -1    1    0 ]
```

Initial state s⁽⁰⁾ = (+1, −1, +1, −1).

1. Compute E(s⁽⁰⁾) directly from the energy formula (sum over the 6 pairs i<j).
2. Compute h_1 under s⁽⁰⁾, apply the update to unit 1 only, get s⁽¹⁾. Compute ΔE two ways: (i) via the shortcut −Δs_1·h_1, (ii) by recomputing E(s⁽¹⁾) directly. They must agree.
3. Repeat for unit 3, updating from s⁽¹⁾ to s⁽²⁾. Report E(s⁽⁰⁾), E(s⁽¹⁾), E(s⁽²⁾) and both ΔE's.

**Part C (why symmetry, concretely).** Using the *same* energy formula but the asymmetric matrix w₁₂ = 1, w₂₁ = −5 (others 0), and the *row-based* update rule h_i = Σ_j w_ij s_j, start from s⁽⁰⁾ = (+1, −1). Update unit 1 only, and show E increases. Report the before/after energy and ΔE. State in one sentence which step of your Part A derivation fails when w_ij ≠ w_ji.

**Deliverable:** A written derivation (Part A), the three numeric energy values and two ΔE checks (Part B), and the asymmetric counterexample with its ΔE (Part C). Plain text/LaTeX, no code required (arithmetic only).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Part A derivation is algebraically correct and identifies both conditions (symmetry, zero self-weight) explicitly; Part B numbers match the grader key exactly with both ΔE methods agreeing; Part C shows a genuine energy increase and correctly diagnoses which line of the Part A derivation breaks. |
| Adequate | Part A reaches the ΔE = −Δs_i h_i identity but is vague about why symmetry is required; Part B numbers are correct or have one arithmetic slip caught by the two-method cross-check; Part C shows an increase but the explanation of *why* is hand-wavy ("weights aren't equal") rather than tied to the derivation step. |
| Not yet | Part A skipped or asserts the theorem without deriving it; Part B numbers wrong and not cross-checked; Part C either doesn't show an increase (arithmetic error) or is missing. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Part A: E(s) = −½Σ_a Σ_b w_ab s_a s_b. Isolating terms containing s_i (a=i and b=i, using w_ii=0): −½ s_i[Σ_j w_ij s_j + Σ_j w_ji s_j] = −½ s_i[h_i + g_i] where g_i = Σ_j w_ji s_j. If w_ij=w_ji then g_i=h_i, giving −s_i h_i. So E = −s_i h_i + C. ΔE = −s_i' h_i − (−s_i h_i) = −(s_i'−s_i)h_i. Since s_i' = sign(h_i), s_i' and h_i always have the same sign or s_i'=s_i (no change), so Δs_i and h_i have the same sign whenever Δs_i≠0, making −Δs_i h_i ≤ 0 (equality only when h_i=0, no flip). Conditions used: (1) w_ij=w_ji for all i,j (symmetry), (2) w_ii=0 (or at least a fixed diagonal that doesn't depend on s_i in a way that breaks the sign argument).

Part B: E(s⁽⁰⁾) = 6. h_1 = w12·s2+w13·s3+w14·s4 = 1(−1)+(−1)(1)+1(−1) = −3 → s1 flips +1→−1, Δs1=−2. ΔE = −(−2)(−3) = −6 both ways; E(s⁽¹⁾) = 0. s⁽¹⁾=(−1,−1,+1,−1). h_3 = w31·s1+w32·s2+w34·s4 = (−1)(−1)+(1)(−1)+(1)(−1) = −1 → s3 flips +1→−1, Δs3=−2. ΔE = −(−2)(−1) = −2; E(s⁽²⁾) = −2. Sequence: 6 → 0 → −2, strictly non-increasing.

Part C: E = −½[w12 s1s2 + w21 s2s1] = −½(1−5)s1s2 = 2·s1s2. s⁽⁰⁾=(1,−1): E=2(−1)=−2. h_1 = w12·s2 = 1(−1) = −1 → s1 flips to −1. New state (−1,−1): E=2(1)=2. ΔE=+4 (energy *increased*). The failing step in Part A: g_i ≠ h_i (g_1 = w21·s2 = −5(−1) = 5 ≠ h_1 = −1), so E ≠ −s_i h_i + C; the −½s_i(h_i+g_i) term includes a component (g_i) that the update rule (which only "sees" h_i) has no control over, so the sign argument for ΔE≤0 no longer holds.

Common wrong turns: forgetting sign(0)=no-change and mis-flipping a unit whose field is exactly 0; computing E over all i,j (double-counting) instead of i<j or applying the ½ inconsistently; in Part C, reusing the *symmetric* update rule (averaging w12,w21) instead of the row-based rule, which hides the effect being demonstrated.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Write out E(s) as a sum over pairs first, then ask which terms actually contain s_i." Do not name which terms drop out.
- Hint 2: "You have two independent ways to get E(s⁽¹⁾) — the direct sum and the ΔE shortcut. If they disagree, that's where to look, not where to guess." Do not supply the correct h_1 value.
- Hint 3: If they're stuck on Part C after two attempts, tell them to write out g_i separately from h_i and compare — but do not compute g_1 or h_1 for them.
- Never: state the final numeric answers (6, 0, −2, +4) before they've submitted a first attempt. Never do the arithmetic in a hint.

## Piece 2 — Hopfield capacity/spurious attractor, then the real CartPole diagnostic

**Type:** simulation (two parts) · **Time:** 3-4.5 hours (the centerpiece of this lesson)

**Learning goals**
- Reproduce, with a self-contained script, the classic Hopfield capacity curve and a spurious attractor, so "basin," "capacity," and "spurious minimum" are concrete before applying them to a real network.
- Design and run a perturbation-test protocol on the actual saved CartPole network that could, in principle, distinguish genuine multi-basin structure from an unconverged slow mode.
- Practise stating a decision rule *before* seeing the result, and reporting an honest "inconclusive" when the data doesn't cleanly resolve it.

### Part (a) — Hopfield capacity and a spurious attractor

**Task.** Self-contained numpy script (no `torch`, no downloads), exact parameters:

- N = 100 units, s_i ∈ {−1,+1}.
- Generate a pool of 30 random ±1 patterns: `rng = np.random.default_rng(0)`, `pool = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(30, 100))`.
- Storage: for a given P, patterns = `pool[:P]`, weights `W = (patterns.T @ patterns) / N`, then zero the diagonal.
- Async settle: repeatedly sweep units in a random order (`np.random.default_rng(order_seed).permutation(N)` per sweep), for each unit set `s_i = sign(h_i)` with h_i = row i of W dotted with current s (keep s_i unchanged if h_i == 0), stop when a full sweep makes zero changes, cap at 20 sweeps.
- Corruption for a stored pattern p (index `idx`): flip each bit independently with probability 0.2 using `np.random.default_rng(2000+idx)`.
- **Capacity curve:** for P in {5, 8, 10, 13, 15, 18, 20, 25, 30}: store `pool[:P]`; for each of the P patterns, corrupt it (seed 2000+idx), settle (order seed 3000+idx), compute overlap m = (s_final · p)/N; count "recalled" if m ≥ 0.95; report recall fraction and mean overlap vs P.
- **Spurious attractor:** store only `pool[:3]`. Build the mixture state `mix = sign(pattern1+pattern2+pattern3)` (ties → +1). Settle starting from `mix` (order seed 11). Report the overlap of the settled state with each of the 3 stored patterns. Then run one more verification sweep (order seed 555) from the settled state and report how many units change — a true fixed point changes zero.

**Deliverable:** the script, a printed table (P, recall_fraction, mean_overlap), and the spurious-attractor overlaps + verification-sweep change count, plus 2-3 sentences: does the recall curve show a sharp knee, and roughly where relative to the textbook capacity α_c·N ≈ 0.138·100 ≈ 13.8? Is the mixture state a genuine, stable, non-recalled fixed point by the 0.95 threshold?

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Recall curve matches the grader key within noise (mean overlap trend included, not just the pass/fail fraction); correctly locates the knee near P≈13-18 and connects it to 0.138N; spurious attractor confirmed stable (0 changes) with all three overlaps correctly reported and correctly identified as below the recall threshold. |
| Adequate | Curve shape is right (recall degrades with P) but exact numbers are off because a spec detail was changed (different seed, different sweep cap) without acknowledging it; spurious attractor demonstrated but the stability check is skipped or misinterpreted. |
| Not yet | No knee / recall doesn't degrade (bug in settle or overlap sign convention); "spurious attractor" reported is actually one of the 3 stored patterns (overlap ≈1.0 with one of them) — i.e. didn't check against all three. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Exact reproduction with the seeds above gives:

| P | recall fraction | mean overlap |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 8 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 10 | 0.90 | 0.916 |
| 13 | 0.846 | 0.909 |
| 15 | 0.80 | 0.885 |
| 18 | 0.50 | 0.792 |
| 20 | 0.45 | 0.806 |
| 25 | 0.24 | 0.698 |
| 30 | 0.033 | 0.481 |

Knee between P=13 and P=20; recall fraction crosses 0.5 around P=18-20, roughly 1.3-1.5× the textbook α_c·N=13.8 — consistent with finite-N softening of the sharp large-N transition (expect the learner to note the curve is a *degradation*, not a cliff, and place it in the right ballpark, not hit these digits exactly if they used different seeds).

Spurious attractor: with `pool[:3]` and mix-state init, the settle loop makes **zero changes on the very first sweep** — the mixture of 3 stored patterns is already an exact fixed point of the Hebbian rule (a standard, well-known Hopfield fact for odd numbers of mixed patterns). Overlaps with the 3 stored patterns: **[0.52, 0.38, 0.60]** — all comfortably below the 0.95 recall threshold (and below 0.9), confirming a genuine spurious minimum rather than a mislabeled recall. Verification sweep: **0 changes**, confirming stability.

Common wrong turns: overlap sign convention flipped (get ≈ −1 instead of +1 and conclude recall always fails); forgetting to reset/reseed per-P so patterns aren't nested (breaks reproducibility); reporting the spurious attractor test using a *freshly random* s0 instead of the mixture state — this usually just re-finds one of the 3 stored patterns (overlap→1.0 with one of them) rather than demonstrating a spurious minimum, because random states are more often in a stored pattern's basin than exactly on a mixture ridge.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Print the overlap after every few sweeps for one P value before trusting the final-only number — is it monotonic?" Do not give expected overlap values.
- Hint 2: If their capacity curve is flat/wrong: "Check what a completely undisturbed pattern (0% corruption) does first — if that doesn't recall perfectly, the bug is in settle or storage, not capacity." Do not debug their code for them line by line.
- Hint 3: If they can't find a spurious attractor from random inits: "A random state is unlikely to sit exactly at a spurious minimum — try constructing the state you'd predict one to be, from the patterns you stored, rather than hoping to stumble on it." Do not name the mixture-state construction outright until they've tried at least one other init strategy.
- Never: hand them the completed capacity table or the mixture-state overlaps before they've run their own script.

### Part (b) — Perturbation-test protocol on the real CartPole network

**Context:** `steps/02b_persistent_state/campaigns/01_baseline_tuning/runs/run_01/best.pkl` is a trained 256-neuron PC network. The existing attractor test (`test_attractors.py`) settled 20 random initial activity states under one clamped observation for 100/500/2000/10000 iterations each, finding: (i) distinct fixed points at every iteration count, (ii) pairwise L2 distance between settled states shrinking from ~2.1 (100 iters) to ~1.85 (2000 iters) to ~1.7 (10000 iters) — a ~19% drop over a 100× increase in iterations, and (iii) the settled ACTION sensor spanning −0.69 to +0.67, crossing zero, for the *same* clamped observation. Nobody currently knows whether this is genuine multi-basin structure or a slow mode that hasn't finished relaxing.

**Task.** Design and run a script (loading the pickle, reusing `network.py`'s settle loop / the logic in `test_attractors.py` rather than reimplementing it, and `headless_rollout` if needed for a canonical observation) that does the following, with every parameter fixed in advance:

1. **Reference fixed points.** From 3 of the original 20 random inits (or 3 fresh ones if the originals weren't saved — document which), settle each for 10000 iterations under the same clamped observation as the original test. Call these s*_A, s*_B, s*_C. Record their settled ACTION values.
2. **Perturbation sweep.** From each s*_X, add i.i.d. Gaussian noise N(0, ε²) to every *free* (non-sensor) neuron's activity, for ε ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 1.0}, 5 repeats per ε with different noise seeds. Re-settle each perturbed state for the same iteration budgets as the original test (100/500/2000/10000), plus one new budget: **50000**.
3. **Measurements per run:** final L2 distance to its own s*_X, final L2 distance to the other two reference points, settled ACTION value, and — for at least one unperturbed run per reference point — the full L2-distance-to-s*_X trajectory logged at every budget checkpoint, to plot log(distance) vs iteration.
4. **Decision rule, stated before running anything:**
   - Supports **genuine multi-basin** (hypothesis A) if: small perturbations (ε ≤ 0.05) reliably return to the *same* s*_X (distance → below a stated small threshold, e.g. 0.05, within the iteration budget) across repeats, AND the log-distance-vs-iteration curve is close to a straight line (geometric decay) for all three points, AND the 50000-iteration run shows the pairwise distances between A/B/C plateauing rather than continuing to shrink.
   - Supports **unconverged slow mode** (hypothesis B) if: perturbed trajectories sometimes drift to a *different* reference point or a novel 4th point, or basin membership is not repeatable across noise seeds, OR the log-distance curve plateaus/flattens rather than being linear, OR the 50000-iteration pairwise distance keeps shrinking below the 1.7 seen at 10000.
   - Otherwise: **inconclusive**, and say exactly which measurement was ambiguous.

**Deliverable:** the script; a table of [reference point, ε, repeat, distance-to-own, distance-to-others, settled action]; the log-distance-vs-iteration data/plot for the 3 unperturbed runs at all 5 budgets (100/500/2000/10000/50000); one paragraph stating which hypothesis (A, B, or inconclusive) your own decision rule selects, applied honestly to your own numbers.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Protocol matches or improves on the spec (states ε values, repeat counts, iteration budgets, and the decision rule *before* running); actually executes it against the real model and reports raw numbers, not just a verbal impression; conclusion follows from the stated decision rule applied to the actual data, including "inconclusive" if that's what the data says. |
| Adequate | Protocol has the right shape (perturb, re-settle, compare) but is missing a control (e.g. no repeats, so basin-membership repeatability can't be assessed; or no 50000-iteration extension, so criterion 3's question goes unanswered); runs against the real model but the decision rule was stated *after* seeing results (post-hoc reasoning) or is qualitative rather than tied to a number. |
| Not yet | No perturbation test at all — reruns the original 20-init test unchanged; or a protocol on paper only, never run against `best.pkl`; or draws a firm "it's definitely basins" / "definitely a slow mode" conclusion with no stated falsification condition. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only) — **nobody knows the ground truth here; grade the protocol design and the reasoning applied to whatever numbers come out, not whether they land on the "correct" hypothesis.**

What a strong protocol looks like, concretely: it perturbs magnitude-graded (not just one ε), it re-settles rather than reading off an instantaneous state, it checks *repeatability* (multiple noise seeds per ε, not one), it extends the iteration budget beyond what's already been tried (the 50000 step is what actually answers criterion 3 — "what further datapoint settles it" — a longer run is the only real answer available with the tools on hand), and it states the decision rule before running so the conclusion isn't rationalized after the fact.

On criterion 3's specific question (is a 19% drop over 100× iterations consistent with geometric convergence to *distinct* fixed points?): true exponential convergence to a fixed point typically saturates within a handful of time constants — if the system were cleanly converged, the pairwise distance should already be flat well before 10000 iterations, not still visibly sliding. A distance that is still detectably shrinking after a 100× increase leans toward "hasn't finished relaxing," but a single such observation is not proof — a genuine multi-basin landscape with one very slow eigenmode near the fixed points (small but nonzero eigenvalue close to the stability boundary) can produce exactly this signature too. The 50000-iteration run (or, better, tracking ‖s(t+1)−s(t)‖ per step and checking whether it decays geometrically or as a slower power law) is the discriminating datapoint the criterion is asking for. If a learner's own 50000-iteration numbers still shrink at a similar rate to the 500→10000 stretch, that is evidence for "still relaxing"; if they flatten out at ~1.7, that's evidence for "reached the basins." Accept either conclusion if it's drawn from their own numbers via their own pre-stated rule.

Do not accept: a learner claiming certainty either way without running the 50000-iteration extension or without a repeatability check across noise seeds — the criterion explicitly requires "what result rules out which hypothesis," and a single unperturbed run cannot do that.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "The original test only ever varied iteration count. What's a second thing you could vary, starting *from* one of the already-settled states, that would tell you whether it's robust?" Do not say "perturbation."
- Hint 2: If they propose perturbing but with only one ε and no repeats: "If you only try one noise magnitude once, and it comes back to the same point, is that because the basin is robust, or because that particular ε and seed happened to land inside it?" Do not tell them to add repeats outright — let them arrive at it.
- Hint 3: If stuck on criterion 3's "what further datapoint": "You already have four iteration budgets. What's missing that would tell you if the curve is still moving?" Do not say "50000 iterations" or "run longer" directly — point at the gap in their existing data, not the fix.
- Never: state or imply which hypothesis (basins vs slow mode) is correct — this is not known. Never let the learner submit a conclusion phrased as certain fact; push back on "it IS a multi-basin landscape" language even if their numbers lean that way, and ask what would change their mind.

## Piece 3 — Guided reading: D&A ch. 7

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 1.5-2 hours

**Learning goals**
- Connect the eigenvalue/eigenvector treatment of linear recurrent networks to the energy argument derived by hand in Piece 1.
- Articulate concretely how a continuous attractor (bump/ring) differs from an isolated point attractor, and how a population of spiking neurons can implement either.
- Locate, in the text, exactly where a weight variable and a state variable each appear, to ground the state-vs-weights distinction before writing about it in Piece 4.

**Task.** Read D&A ch. 7, the *Recurrent Networks* sections covering selective amplification, linear/nonlinear recurrent dynamics, and continuous line / bump / ring attractors, plus the *Stochastic Networks* section on energy functions and the Hopfield network. Answer, in writing:

1. In a bump/ring attractor, what continuous variable does the bump's position encode, and why does the network have a whole continuous *family* of fixed points along that variable rather than one isolated fixed point? Describe the geometric picture (valley vs single-point minimum).
2. A single LIF neuron cannot rest at an arbitrary constant real-valued output — it either spikes or is silent. What quantity is actually held fixed at a "fixed point" of persistent activity, if not any one neuron's voltage trace? Name the coding variable and how it's constructed from spikes.
3. D&A analyse linear recurrent networks via the eigenvectors/eigenvalues of the (symmetric) weight matrix. Connect this to Piece 1: what role do eigenvalues play in whether a mode gets amplified into persistent activity vs decays, and why does W's symmetry (guaranteeing real eigenvalues) matter for getting monotone convergence rather than oscillation?
4. D&A state the same "energy never increases under asynchronous update" result you derived by hand in Piece 1. Check: do their stated conditions match yours exactly (symmetric weights, zero self-weight)? Name any condition they add that you didn't need to state explicitly in Piece 1 (hint: synchronous vs asynchronous update).
5. Find one equation in the recurrent-network sections containing both a weight term and a state/activity term. State which of the two is being held fixed while the other evolves during (a) the settling/dynamics phase and (b) the learning phase.

**Deliverable:** written answers, roughly half a page each, citing the specific mechanism (not just "the book says so").

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Every answer names the correct mechanism with correct terminology (continuum of degenerate minima / zero eigenvalue along the ring direction; population rate as the persistent variable; eigenvalue-driven amplification tied explicitly back to Piece 1's ΔE argument; correctly flags the synchronous/asynchronous condition; correctly identifies which variable is frozen in which phase). |
| Adequate | Answers are directionally correct but stay at the level of restating the text without connecting to Piece 1's derivation or the CartPole project (e.g. answers Q3 without mentioning eigenvalues, or Q2 without naming a rate/population variable). |
| Not yet | Confuses a bump attractor with a single isolated point attractor, or claims persistent activity requires every neuron to fire at literally the same instant/rate as every other, or misses the synchronous/asynchronous distinction in Q4 entirely. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

1. The bump's position encodes a continuous variable (e.g. an angle, a position along a track — a "which direction/where" analog quantity). The fixed points form a continuous line/ring because the recurrent connectivity is translation-invariant along that dimension: shifting the bump to a neighbouring position costs zero energy along that specific direction (a flat direction / zero eigenvalue of the linearized dynamics), unlike a generic isolated fixed point where any small perturbation costs energy and gets restored. Picture: a trough/valley of degenerate minima, not a single point at the bottom of a bowl.

2. The population/mean firing rate r_i(t) (spike count in a window, or the mean-field rate variable) of each neuron in the assembly — not any individual neuron's membrane potential, which keeps cycling through threshold-and-reset regardless. Persistent activity is a stable pattern of rates maintained by recurrent excitation (balanced by inhibition/adaptation); individual spike timing can vary trial to trial while the rate pattern stays fixed. This is the "state vs implementation" gap the CartPole project will hit directly once step 03 moves to spiking neurons.

3. Decomposing activity into W's eigenvectors, each mode evolves scaled by its eigenvalue over time — modes at/above the critical eigenvalue get selectively amplified into persistent activity ("selective amplification"), others decay. This is the same phenomenon as convergence into a Hopfield attractor along that eigen-direction, and Piece 1's ΔE≤0 argument implicitly relies on the same symmetry: real eigenvalues (guaranteed by the spectral theorem for symmetric W) is what makes monotone convergence to a fixed point possible at all — asymmetric W can produce complex eigenvalues, hence rotation/oscillation instead of settling, matching the ΔE>0 counterexample in Piece 1 Part C.

4. D&A's stated conditions: symmetric weights (w_ij=w_ji), and (typically) w_ii=0 or a diagonal that doesn't interfere — matching Piece 1. The condition Piece 1 didn't have to isolate explicitly but D&A add: the update must be **asynchronous** (one unit at a time), not synchronous (all units updated simultaneously from the same snapshot) — simultaneous update does not carry the same monotonicity guarantee and can produce a 2-cycle instead of converging.

5. E.g. the discrete update s_i' = sign(Σ_j w_ij s_j) or the continuous τ dr/dt = −r + F(Wr+h): during settling, W (or w_ij) is frozen and s (or r) is the variable that evolves; during learning (e.g. the Hebbian storage rule w_ij = (1/N)Σ_μ p_i^μ p_j^μ), the patterns/activity are treated as already-given data and w_ij is what's being computed — there is no "settling" happening to the weights themselves in this simple picture.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "What happens to the energy/flow if you shift the whole bump one step to the left — does it cost anything?" Do not say "zero eigenvalue" or "flat direction" outright.
- Hint 2 (Q2): "What can a spike train not do that a rate can?" Do not name "firing rate" directly until they've proposed at least one candidate variable.
- Hint 3 (Q4): "You already found the two conditions by hand in Piece 1 — read carefully for a THIRD condition about the order of updates." Do not name synchronous/asynchronous directly.
- Never: supply a paraphrase of the target D&A passage that amounts to giving the answer. Ask what they found in the text first.

## Piece 4 — Written defence: state vs weights, and judging the diagnostic

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 1-1.5 hours

**Learning goals**
- Articulate, precisely, the state/weight timescale separation and its exception in this project's own online-learning setting.
- Take a defensible, falsifiable position on the multi-basin vs slow-mode question grounded in the learner's own Piece 2(b) data, not intuition.
- Reason about whether a specific observed fact (the action sign crossing zero) is actually evidence for either hypothesis, or a red herring.

**Task.** Write 300-600 words addressing all three:

(a) What stays fixed and what changes between "the network learns" and "the network settles to an answer for one input"? Ground this in both the Hopfield formalism (Piece 1: which symbol is w_ij, which is s_i, in the update rule vs the storage rule) and the CartPole network. Explicitly address the wrinkle in this project's own online-learning setting (campaign 02), where weights *do* update during a rollout — does the clean state/weight separation still hold there, and if not, what replaces it?

(b) State your current best judgment on the multi-basin vs slow-mode question from Piece 2(b), using your own decision rule and your own numbers. If your data was ambiguous, say "inconclusive" and say why. Either way, state one concrete, checkable observation that would change your mind.

(c) The settled ACTION value ranged from −0.69 to +0.67 across different initial states, for the *identical* clamped observation. Is the fact that it crosses zero itself informative about which hypothesis (basins vs slow mode) is more likely — or is it uninformative, and the sign alone should be ignored? Justify your position.

**Deliverable:** 300-600 words, prose (not bullet points for the main argument; a short table is fine as support).

**Rubric**

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

(a) Standard content: weights (w_ij) are the slow variable, changed by learning, persisting across trials; activity/state (s_i or r_i) is the fast variable, re-initialized or reset per trial and evolving during settling while weights are frozen. The wrinkle: in the project's online-learning campaigns (campaign 02), weights update *during* a rollout using live SGD, so the clean "learning happens between trials, settling happens within a trial" split breaks down — the honest answer is that there's still a timescale separation (weight updates per step are much smaller/slower than the settling dynamics within a step), but it's a matter of degree, not the same hard boundary as the offline case. A strong answer names this explicitly since it's the project's current focus.

(b) No fixed correct answer — grade whether the stated position (i) is A, B, or "inconclusive," (ii) is drawn from their Piece 2(b) numbers and pre-stated decision rule rather than from vibes, and (iii) comes with one specific, checkable falsifier (e.g. "if the 50000-iteration pairwise distance drops below X, I'd revise to slow-mode" — not "if I ran more experiments").

(c) Correct reasoning: the sign of the settled action, on its own, does not distinguish the hypotheses. Both a genuine bistable landscape and a set of slow-converging trajectories parked at different points along a slow manifold could straddle an arbitrary threshold like zero — the CartPole controller's action output has no special reason to respect a hard zero boundary tied to basin identity. What *would* be informative is whether the sign (and the point it's near) is repeatable — i.e., does the same reference fixed point + same small perturbation always yield the same sign across independent reruns (supports either genuine structure, if repeatable) versus the sign flipping under negligible perturbation or reordering (suggests it's balanced on an unstable ridge / still moving, not settled). Reject answers that treat the zero-crossing itself as decisive evidence for two attractors.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1 (a): "In the online case, is there ANY sense in which weights are still 'slower' than activity, even though both move within one rollout?" Do not answer for them.
- Hint 2 (b): "What specific number, if it came out differently, would flip your answer?" If they can't name one, that's the actual gap — don't supply a candidate falsifier.
- Hint 3 (c): "Would the OTHER hypothesis also predict values that happen to straddle zero? If both would, what does the sign actually tell you?" Do not state the "uninformative" conclusion directly until they've tried to argue it themselves.
- Never: tell them what the "right" answer to (b) is — there isn't one. Never let a confident, unfalsifiable claim in (b) or (c) pass without a hint pushing back.
