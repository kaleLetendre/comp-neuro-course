## Piece 1 — PING loop timing and the coherence window

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 60–75 min

**Learning goals**
- Derive an E-I loop's oscillation period from its inhibitory decay and loop delay, and show excitatory decay does not enter.
- Explain mechanistically why inhibition, not excitation, paces a PING rhythm.
- Compute whether two phase-separated inputs both fall inside a receiving neuron's excitability window, and identify what that outcome depends on.

**Task**

**Part A — period from decay + delay.**
Model: an excitatory population E and inhibitory population I in a feedback loop. E fires a volley; after one-way conduction delay `d` it reaches I; I fires and releases inhibition that decays with time constant `tau_I`; once that inhibition has decayed, and after a further one-way delay `d` for the inhibitory signal to reach E, E is free to fire the next volley. Use the approximation

```
T ≈ tau_I + 2d
f = 1000 / T      (T in ms, f in Hz)
```

(the `2d` is the round-trip conduction delay: once E→I, once I→E; `tau_I` is the time inhibition takes to decay enough for E to escape).

Given parameters:
- `tau_E` = 5 ms (excitatory synaptic/membrane decay)
- `tau_I` = 10 ms (inhibitory decay)
- `d` = 2 ms (one-way loop delay)

1. Compute `T` and `f` from the formula. State whether the rhythm lands in theta (~4–8 Hz) or gamma (~30–80 Hz).
2. `tau_E` = 5 ms was given but does not appear in the formula. Explain in 2–3 sentences, in terms of the PING mechanism, why the excitatory decay constant does not set the inter-volley interval, while the inhibitory decay constant does.
3. Recompute `T` and `f` for a "long loop": `tau_I` = 100 ms, `d` = 15 ms. State the resulting band.
4. Sketch (ASCII is fine) a timing diagram of one full cycle of the case in (1): a time axis in ms from 0 to T, with marks for "E fires," "signal reaches I," "I fires / inhibits," "inhibition reaches E and starts decaying," and "E free to fire again." Every mark must have a real ms value from your computation, not a placeholder.

**Part B — the coherence window.**
A gamma rhythm has a 25 ms cycle. The receiving neuron has a 5 ms window of postsynaptic excitability once per cycle (the period right after inhibition bottoms out, when the membrane is close enough to threshold for an incoming EPSP to trigger a spike). Two excitatory inputs, A and B, are phase-locked to two different upstream assemblies; B arrives 12 ms later in phase than A on every cycle.

5. If A arrives inside the excitability window, work out whether B also arrives inside a window (same cycle or a neighbouring one). Show the arithmetic (12 ms vs. window width 5 ms vs. cycle length 25 ms).
6. State explicitly what determined your answer to (5) — is it the number of spikes, the firing rate of A and B, or something else?

**Deliverable:** one page (typed or scanned handwriting) with: Part A steps 1–4 (including the diagram), Part B steps 5–6, one line of computed numbers per step — no unexplained final answers.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Both Part A cases correctly classified (gamma, theta) with correct T/f arithmetic; explanation of why `tau_E` drops out is mechanistic (references the loop's "brake" being inhibition, not "E is ready to fire" hand-waving); timing diagram has all four marked events with correct ms values; Part B correctly concludes B is filtered out with reasoning tied to phase modulo cycle length, not rate. |
| Adequate | Correct band classification in both Part A cases but the "why `tau_E` drops out" explanation is vague or only restates the formula; diagram present but missing one mark or one value; Part B has the right numeric conclusion but the "what determines the answer" line is hand-wavy or conflates phase with rate. |
| Not yet | Either Part A case misclassified (e.g. calls the 5/10/2 case theta or the 100/15 case gamma); no mechanistic account of why `tau_E` is irrelevant; Part B concludes "both get through" or "neither gets through" without correct 12 vs. 5 vs. 25 arithmetic. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Part A:
- Case 1: T = 10 + 2(2) = 14 ms → f = 1000/14 ≈ **71.4 Hz → gamma**.
- Case 3: T = 100 + 2(15) = 130 ms → f = 1000/130 ≈ **7.7 Hz → theta**.
- Why `tau_E` drops out: in the PING picture, once inhibition releases E, E's rise to threshold is comparatively fast/immediate relative to the inhibitory decay that gated it — the rate-limiting step in the loop is "how long is E held down," which is set by `tau_I` (plus the fixed conduction delays), not by how quickly E itself decays between volleys. Accept any answer that correctly identifies inhibition as the rate-limiting ("braking") element and excitation as comparatively fast/permissive.
- Diagram (case 1, one accepted layout): t=0 E fires → t=2 ms signal reaches I → t=2 ms I fires (instantaneous is fine as a simplification) → I's IPSC decays from t=2 to t=2+10=12 ms → t=12+2=14 ms inhibition has reached back to E and decayed enough → E fires again at t=14 ms. Accept small reorderings as long as the four events appear in a causally sane order and the final mark is at t=14 ms.

Part B: cycle = 25 ms, window = 5 ms, phase offset = 12 ms. 12 ms is more than the 5 ms window and is not a multiple of the 25 ms cycle (12 mod 25 = 12, well outside [0,5]), so if A is at phase 0 (inside [0,5]), B at phase 12 lands outside every occurrence of the window. **B does not get through; only A does.** What determines the answer: **phase (arrival time modulo cycle length) relative to the window's position and width — not spike count or rate.** A and B could have identical firing rates and this would not change.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Which two of the three given time constants (5, 10, 2 ms) actually appear in the period formula? Which one is a distractor?"
- Hint 2: "The loop delay contributes twice, not once — why would a round trip matter more than a one-way trip?"
- Hint 3: If still stuck after Hint 2, give the formula `T ≈ tau_I + 2d` explicitly and ask the learner to plug in both cases themselves.
- Never: never state "gamma" or "theta" for either case before the learner has produced their own T/f numbers. Never do the Part B mod-arithmetic for them — ask them to state the phase offset and window bounds first.

## Piece 2 — E-I rate-model rhythm and its onset

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 75–100 min

**Learning goals**
- Build a minimal delayed-feedback E-I rate model and measure its emergent oscillation frequency numerically, without assuming the answer.
- Empirically confirm the direction of the tau_I → frequency relationship predicted in Piece 1.
- Observe that oscillation is not guaranteed for every parameter setting — there is an onset/offset boundary (ties to D&A ch.7's treatment of when the E-I system transitions from a stable fixed point to a sustained rhythm).

**Task**

Implement, in Python (numpy or PyTorch, no autograd needed), a two-population rate model with an explicit conduction delay, Euler-integrated:

```
dE/dt = ( -E + sigmoid( gain * (w_EE*E   - w_EI*I_delayed + I_ext - theta_E) ) ) / tau_E
dI/dt = ( -I + sigmoid( gain * (w_IE*E_delayed - w_II*I           - theta_I) ) ) / tau_I
```
where `E_delayed(t) = E(t - d)`, `I_delayed(t) = I(t - d)` (implement the delay with a ring buffer / array indexing, not an approximation), `sigmoid(x) = 1/(1+exp(-x))`.

**Exact fixed parameters** (do not change these):
`tau_E = 5 ms`, `d = 2 ms`, `w_EE = 2.0`, `w_EI = 2.0`, `w_IE = 2.0`, `w_II = 0.0`, `I_ext = 1.0`, `theta_E = 1.0`, `theta_I = 1.0`, `gain = 10.0`, `dt = 0.1 ms`, `T_total = 1000 ms`, initial condition `E[0..d] = 0.3`, `I[0..d] = 0.1`.

**Sweep:** run the model once per value of `tau_I ∈ {5, 10, 20, 30, 50, 65, 70} ms`, all other parameters fixed as above.

**Measurement:** discard the first 400 ms as transient. On the remaining `E(t)` trace, count rising zero-crossings of `E(t) - mean(E(t))` and compute the mean inter-crossing interval as the period; frequency = 1000/period (Hz). If fewer than 3 crossings are found in the retained window, report "no sustained oscillation" for that `tau_I` rather than a spurious frequency.

**Deliverable:**
1. The script (under ~100 lines).
2. A table or plot of frequency (Hz) vs. `tau_I` (ms) for all 7 sweep values.
3. 3–5 sentences: (a) does frequency increase or decrease with `tau_I`, matching or contradicting Piece 1's prediction that longer `tau_I` → lower frequency? (b) what happens at the high end of the sweep, and why is that a real result and not a bug? (c) explicitly note that the exact Hz values will NOT match Piece 1's `T ≈ tau_I + 2d` formula number-for-number — explain in one sentence why not (different model: saturating/nonlinear feedback vs. the linear round-trip approximation), while the *direction* of the trend should still agree.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Correct delay-buffer implementation (verified by delay ≠ 0 having an effect); frequency strictly decreases as `tau_I` increases across the range where oscillation exists; correctly reports "no sustained oscillation" at `tau_I = 70`ms (or wherever it empirically stops in their run) rather than reporting a fabricated near-zero frequency; the three explanatory sentences are all present and (b) correctly frames the disappearance as a stability transition (fixed point becomes stable / Hopf-type boundary), not a numerical artifact. |
| Adequate | Correct qualitative trend and correct script, but the write-up conflates "no oscillation" with "very low frequency," or the explanation in (c) is missing or superficial ("numbers are different because it's a different model" with no reason why). |
| Not yet | Frequency trend is flat, increasing, or noisy/non-monotonic (implementation bug — likely delay not applied, or measurement window too short); no acknowledgment that oscillation can disappear; script silently reports frequency = 0 or NaN without comment. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Reference results (numpy, Euler, dt = 0.1 ms, measured as specified; small variation, e.g. ±20% relative on frequency, is acceptable due to implementation differences in the zero-crossing method):

| tau_I (ms) | frequency (Hz) |
|---|---|
| 5 | ≈ 27–28 |
| 10 | ≈ 17–18 |
| 20 | ≈ 10.5–11 |
| 30 | ≈ 7.5–8.5 |
| 50 | ≈ 5–5.5 |
| 65 | ≈ 4–4.5 |
| 70 | no sustained oscillation (system relaxes to a stable fixed point) |

Trend: strictly decreasing frequency with increasing `tau_I`, consistent in *direction* with Piece 1 (longer inhibitory decay → slower rhythm), though this toy model spans roughly beta/low-gamma down to theta rather than landing cleanly in the 30–80 Hz gamma band at the short end — accept any write-up that notices and reasonably explains this is a toy model, not a fit to biological data. The disappearance of oscillation near `tau_I ≈ 65–70 ms` is a genuine Hopf-type stability boundary of this specific parameter set (the fixed point stops being unstable), not an integration failure — reject any answer that shrugs this off as "the code broke" without checking (e.g. confirming `E`, `I` settle to a constant rather than diverging or NaN-ing).

Reference implementation sketch (do not hand to the learner pre-emptively):
```python
import numpy as np

def simulate(tau_I, tau_E=5.0, delay=2.0, dt=0.1, T=1000.0,
             w_EE=2.0, w_EI=2.0, w_IE=2.0, w_II=0.0,
             I_ext=1.0, theta_E=1.0, theta_I=1.0, gain=10.0):
    n = int(T/dt); d = int(round(delay/dt))
    E = np.zeros(n); I = np.zeros(n)
    E[:d+1] = 0.3; I[:d+1] = 0.1
    f = lambda x: 1.0/(1.0+np.exp(-gain*x))
    for t in range(d, n-1):
        dE = (-E[t] + f(w_EE*E[t] - w_EI*I[t-d] + I_ext - theta_E)) * dt/tau_E
        dI = (-I[t] + f(w_IE*E[t-d] - w_II*I[t] - theta_I)) * dt/tau_I
        E[t+1] = E[t] + dE
        I[t+1] = I[t] + dI
    return E, I

def frequency(E, dt, discard_ms=400.0):
    x = E[int(discard_ms/dt):]
    x = x - x.mean()
    cr = np.where((x[:-1] < 0) & (x[1:] >= 0))[0]
    if len(cr) < 3: return None
    return 1000.0/np.diff(cr).mean()/dt if False else 1000.0/(np.diff(cr)*dt).mean()
```

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Print the raw `E(t)` trace for one `tau_I` value before trying to measure frequency — is it oscillating, decaying to a flat line, or exploding?"
- Hint 2: If the delay seems to have no effect, ask: "What does your buffer indexing do when `d = 0` vs. `d = 20` (in dt-steps) — did you actually index `t-d`, or accidentally always read `t`?"
- Hint 3: If frequency measurement is noisy/wrong, point at the transient: "Are you measuring from t=0, including the initial transient before the rhythm settles?"
- Never: never give the parameter values beyond what's already specified in the task (they are fully specified — the learner should not need more). Never tell the learner in advance that oscillation disappears at high `tau_I` — that must be their own empirical finding.

## Piece 3 — D&A ch.7 guided reading

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 45–60 min

**Learning goals**
- Ground Piece 1's period formula in D&A's own coupled excitatory-inhibitory rate equations.
- Locate, in a primary source, the actual condition D&A gives for an E-I network to transition from a stable fixed point to sustained oscillation.
- Identify explicitly what D&A does and does not cover about phase/timing, so the learner knows the boundary of the source material before Piece 4.

**Task**

Read D&A, Chapter 7, the section(s) on excitatory-inhibitory network dynamics (the part that sets up coupled rate equations for an E and an I population and analyzes their stability). Answer:

1. Write down D&A's coupled rate equations for the E and I populations (their notation, not Piece 1's). Map each of D&A's coupling terms onto Piece 1's `w_EE`, `w_EI`, `w_IE`, `w_II`.
2. Find the condition D&A states for the coupled system to move from a stable fixed point to a sustained oscillation. Write it in your own words. Does it talk about eigenvalues, a trace/determinant condition, or something else?
3. In Piece 1 Part A, `w_II = 0` yet the loop still oscillated because of the E↔I cross-coupling. Does D&A's stability condition depend on self-inhibition (`w_II`), or purely on the cross terms (`w_EI`, `w_IE`) and the time constants? State which, citing the relevant equation/expression.
4. D&A's basic E-I model has no explicit axonal conduction delay term (unlike Piece 1's `d`). Note one place where D&A comes close to discussing timing/delay between populations, or explicitly note "no such discussion found in this section" if that's the case — do not invent one.
5. Find one place where D&A connects the E-I network's dynamics to a concrete phenomenon (e.g., response to a step or ramp input, contrast-invariant tuning, or similar). State it in one sentence.

**Deliverable:** half a page, five numbered short answers, each citing the specific equation number or a clear paraphrase location (e.g. "the paragraph introducing the linearized Jacobian of the coupled rate equations") since page numbers vary by printing.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Equations transcribed correctly with correct term-by-term mapping to Piece 1's notation; stability condition stated correctly in terms of the linearized system's eigenvalues/trace-determinant (or D&A's equivalent framing) and correctly identifies that cross-coupling, not self-inhibition, is what produces the complex (oscillatory) eigenvalues; answer to (4) is honest about what is/isn't there rather than invented; (5) gives one accurate concrete example. |
| Adequate | Equations correct but mapping to Piece 1 terms is loose or partially wrong; stability condition described qualitatively correct but without the eigenvalue/trace-determinant machinery; (4) and (5) present but vague. |
| Not yet | Equations missing or wrong; stability condition is guessed rather than found in the text; (4) invents a delay discussion that isn't in the cited material; (5) is generic ("oscillations are important in the brain") rather than the specific example D&A gives. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- (1) D&A's linearized coupled rate equations take the general form `tau_E dE/dt = -E + w_EE E - w_EI I (+ input)` and `tau_I dI/dt = -I + w_IE E - w_II I (+ input)` — this is exactly Piece 1's matrix structure; term-for-term mapping should be direct (same four weights, same two time constants).
- (2) D&A's onset-of-oscillation condition is a linear-stability argument: linearize around the fixed point, form the 2×2 Jacobian, and the system oscillates (complex eigenvalues) when the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial (trace² − 4·det) is negative; it is stable (decaying oscillation or no oscillation) when the real part of the eigenvalues is negative, i.e. trace < 0. Accept any answer that correctly identifies "eigenvalues of the linearized 2×2 system; complex eigenvalues ⇒ oscillation, sign of the real part ⇒ growing vs. decaying."
- (3) The complex-eigenvalue (oscillatory) condition depends on the cross terms `w_EI·w_IE` (their product appears in the determinant/discriminant) and the time constants; `w_II` is not required to be nonzero — setting `w_II = 0` (as in Piece 1) still permits complex eigenvalues, confirming self-inhibition is not the necessary ingredient, cross-coupling is. Reject an answer that claims self-inhibition is required for oscillation.
- (4) Correct answer is that D&A's basic treatment in this section does not include an explicit conduction-delay term in the differential equations — delay-driven oscillation mechanisms (and communication-through-coherence specifically) are outside D&A and belong to the Fries/Wang literature this lesson explicitly does not have access to. Accept "no such discussion found" as a fully correct answer; do not accept a fabricated citation.
- (5) Any accurate, specific example D&A gives of the E-I network's response properties (e.g., how the network responds to a sudden step in input, or how mutual inhibition sharpens/normalizes population responses) is acceptable — the grading bar is "specific and accurate," not a particular example.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "What are the two things you'd add to a single rate equation to make it a coupled pair — what's the term that lets I affect E, and the term that lets E affect I?"
- Hint 2: "Stability of a 2×2 linear system is usually decided by two numbers computed from the matrix — which two?" (trace and determinant)
- Hint 3: If still stuck, name the concept directly ("look for where D&A linearizes and takes eigenvalues of the Jacobian") without quoting the equation.
- Never: never quote or transcribe entire paragraphs of D&A's text to the learner — point at where to look, don't do the reading for them. Never fabricate a page number if unsure.

## Piece 4 — Functional or epiphenomenal?

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 45–60 min · **300–600 words**

**Learning goals**
- State a concrete example of phase-carried information that rate alone cannot recover.
- Marshal one piece of evidence each for "oscillations are functionally causal" and "oscillations are an epiphenomenon of recurrent E-I dynamics."
- Take an explicit, falsifiable position on whether Step 3's purely-local, no-global-clock constraint can substitute for an oscillatory phase reference — and say what evidence would change the learner's mind.

**Task**

Write 300–600 words that do all of the following:

1. Give one concrete, specific example of information that is carried in the *phase* of firing relative to a population rhythm and that could not be recovered from firing rate alone (rate = spike count in a window, with phase information discarded). Use a real quantity (e.g. a phase value in degrees or ms relative to cycle, mapped to something it encodes) — not just "phase carries information."
2. State the single strongest piece of evidence you can identify (from D&A ch.7, your Piece 1 derivation, your Piece 2 simulation, or the available papers list — C1–C6, B2, B5) that oscillations are causally functional (the rhythm itself is used downstream, not just a byproduct).
3. State the single strongest piece of evidence, from the same source pool, that oscillations can be epiphenomenal (a byproduct of E-I feedback dynamics with no computational role attributed to the rhythm itself). Your own Piece 2 simulation — a toy E-I loop that oscillates purely as a consequence of feedback and delay, without any "use" being defined for the rhythm — is a legitimate example here if you argue it correctly.
4. Take a position on the project tie-in: does Step 3's hard constraint (all learning purely local — pre-synaptic activity, post-synaptic activity, local error signal; no global loss; no global clock) rule out relying on an emergent oscillatory phase reference for coordinating graph nodes, make it necessary, or leave it irrelevant? Argue for one of these three, using your Piece 1/2 results as evidence (e.g., is a shared phase reference something that could emerge locally from many small E-I loops without a global clock, or does synchrony of that kind require exactly the kind of global timing signal the constraint forbids?).
5. State one specific observation or result that would change your mind on point 4 — not "more evidence" in general, but a stated, checkable thing (e.g. "if two unconnected local E-I loops in a graph topology spontaneously phase-lock without any shared input or connection between them, that would suggest synchrony is achievable without a global clock").

**Deliverable:** 300–600 words, five clearly labeled parts matching 1–5 above (a numbered list or headers is fine — flowing prose is not required to hit all five points explicitly).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | (1) is a specific, correctly-reasoned example where rate provably loses information that phase retains; (2) and (3) each cite one specific, correctly-described piece of evidence (not a vague appeal); (4) takes one clear position with an argument that actually uses Piece 1/2 results; (5) is concrete and falsifiable, not "more data." |
| Adequate | All five points addressed but (2)/(3) are generic ("oscillations seem important" / "the sim just did it automatically") rather than a specific named source or result; (4) states a position but doesn't connect to the derivation/sim; (5) is vague ("if I saw more evidence"). |
| Not yet | Missing one or more of the five points; (1) restates "phase carries information" without a concrete example; (4) refuses to take a position ("it depends" with no argument); (5) is unfalsifiable or absent. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- (1) Acceptable concrete examples: theta phase precession (a place cell's spike phase relative to the theta cycle shifts systematically as an animal crosses its place field, encoding position within the field at a finer resolution than the spike count over the whole passage would give — this is discussed in the replay/planning literature, B5, even though B5's specific focus is replay rather than phase precession per se, so accept if the learner reasons about phase-position coding correctly and is honest about which paper covers what) OR a constructed example (e.g. two neurons firing at the identical rate of 40 Hz, one always at phase 0° and the other always at phase 180° relative to a shared 40 Hz rhythm — mean rate is identical and indistinguishable, but the two units are anti-phase and carry different downstream timing/one could be gating-relevant while the other is gated-out). Reject a purely assertive answer with no numbers.
- (2) Acceptable "functional" evidence: communication-through-coherence itself as a mechanism claim (spikes arriving in-phase drive the target, out-of-phase spikes are filtered, as computed concretely in Piece 1 Part B) — note the learner does not have Fries 2005 itself, so they should cite this as "the coherence-window calculation in Piece 1" or reason from C1/C3 (Bogacz free-energy tutorial / Feldman & Friston attention-uncertainty) if they draw a connection to precision-weighting being time/phase-dependent. The grader should accept reasoning grounded in Piece 1/available sources, not penalize for not having Fries 2005.
- (3) Acceptable "epiphenomenal" evidence: the learner's own Piece 2 result — a bare 2-population rate model with delayed feedback produces a clean, parameter-dependent rhythm with zero notion of "use" built in; the oscillation is a direct mathematical consequence of feedback + delay + saturation, which is exactly the kind of "oscillations as a side effect of recurrent circuits" position the lesson goal describes. This is the intended anchor answer, since the learner has direct, first-hand evidence of it from Piece 2.
- (4) No single correct position; grade the argument, not the conclusion. A well-argued "necessary" (some coordination signal seems needed for phase-dependent gating across distant graph nodes, and if it can't be global, it must be an emergent local-recurrence-based rhythm) is as acceptable as a well-argued "irrelevant" (purely local rules can achieve equivalent selective routing via weight/threshold structure without any shared phase reference — e.g. stress/pruning mechanisms in Step 4 already provide a topology-based alternative to phase-based gating). Reject answers that don't engage with the "no global clock" constraint at all.
- (5) Any concrete, checkable falsification condition is acceptable (example given in the task itself — spontaneous phase-locking of two otherwise-unconnected local loops — is a valid answer if the learner writes it in their own words; other valid conditions: "if disrupting phase alignment in a working system, while leaving rates intact, breaks task performance, that's evidence for functional; if it doesn't, that's evidence for epiphenomenal").

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Rate = count over a window. Can you describe a signal where two neurons have the exact same count in every window, but you'd still say they're carrying different information?"
- Hint 2: If (3) is missing or vague: "You already have a first-hand example of an oscillation that exists purely because of feedback + delay, with no defined 'use' for the rhythm — where did you just produce one?"
- Hint 3: If (4) is a non-position ("it depends" with no argument): ask them to bet — "if you had to design Step 3 today, would you add any phase/synchrony mechanism, yes or no — now justify that specific choice."
- Never: never supply the learner's position on (4) for them. Never tell them which of C1–C6/B2/B5 to cite before they've tried to find one themselves.
