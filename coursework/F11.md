## Piece 1 — EM on a two-cause linear generative model

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 75-100 min

**Learning goals**
- Write down a causal model's generative distribution over an input given discrete latent causes, and invert it with Bayes' rule to get an E-step posterior.
- Turn E-step posteriors into posterior expectations over causes.
- Carry out a gradient-based M-step update of generative weights using those expectations, and see concretely why this is "learning at fixed beliefs" rather than "inference at fixed parameters."

**Task**

Consider a causal model with two independent binary causes $s_1, s_2 \in \{0,1\}$, each with prior $P(s_i=1) = 0.5$ (so all four joint states $(s_1,s_2)$ have prior probability $0.25$). The generative model produces a scalar input

$$y = g_1 s_1 + g_2 s_2 + \varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Current parameter estimates are $g_1 = 2$, $g_2 = -1$. A single input is observed: $y = 1.5$.

1. **E-step.** For each of the four joint states $(s_1,s_2) \in \{(0,0),(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)\}$, compute the predicted mean $\mu(s_1,s_2) = g_1 s_1 + g_2 s_2$, the Gaussian likelihood $P(y \mid s_1,s_2)$ (you may drop the shared normalizing constant $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ since it cancels), and the posterior responsibility $r(s_1,s_2) = P(s_1,s_2 \mid y)$ by multiplying by the prior and renormalizing over the four states.
2. From the four responsibilities, compute the posterior expectations $E[s_1 \mid y]$ and $E[s_2\mid y]$ (sum the responsibilities of the states where that cause is on).
3. **M-step.** Using $\hat{y} = g_1 E[s_1\mid y] + g_2 E[s_2\mid y]$ as the model's expected reconstruction, compute the error $y - \hat y$, then apply the delta-rule gradient update
$$\Delta g_i = \eta \,(y - \hat y)\, E[s_i \mid y], \qquad \eta = 1$$
State the updated $g_1, g_2$.

Do this by hand, showing every intermediate number (the four $\mu$'s, the four exponents, the four unnormalized values, the normalizer, the four responsibilities, the two expectations, $\hat y$, the error, and the two updated weights).

**Deliverable**

A pen-and-paper (or typed) worked solution showing all of the above numbers in order, plus one sentence identifying which of your two steps is "E" and which is "M" and why.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All four responsibilities correct to ±0.005, both posterior expectations and both updated weights correct to ±0.01, every intermediate quantity shown, E/M labeling sentence correct. |
| Adequate | Method fully correct (joint enumeration → prior×likelihood → renormalize → expectation → delta rule) but one arithmetic slip (e.g. a squared-error term or a sign) that the response doesn't need explained back — just recomputed. |
| Not yet | Skips the prior weighting (uses raw likelihoods as responsibilities), normalizes over the wrong set of states, or updates $g_1,g_2$ directly from responsibilities without the reconstruction-error term (conflating E- and M-steps). |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Exponent terms (using $-(y-\mu)^2/2$, dropping the shared $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$):
- $(0,0)$: $\mu=0$, exponent $=-(1.5)^2/2=-1.125$, $\exp(-1.125)=0.3247$
- $(0,1)$: $\mu=-1$, exponent $=-(2.5)^2/2=-3.125$, $\exp(-3.125)=0.04394$
- $(1,0)$: $\mu=2$, exponent $=-(0.5)^2/2=-0.125$, $\exp(-0.125)=0.8825$
- $(1,1)$: $\mu=1$, exponent $=-(0.5)^2/2=-0.125$, $\exp(-0.125)=0.8825$

Unnormalized posterior (prior $0.25$ × likelihood):
- $(0,0)$: $0.25\times0.3247=0.08118$
- $(0,1)$: $0.25\times0.04394=0.01099$
- $(1,0)$: $0.25\times0.8825=0.22063$
- $(1,1)$: $0.25\times0.8825=0.22063$

Sum $=0.53342$. Responsibilities:
- $r(0,0)=0.1522$, $r(0,1)=0.0206$, $r(1,0)=0.4136$, $r(1,1)=0.4136$ (sum to $1.000$)

Posterior expectations:
- $E[s_1\mid y] = r(1,0)+r(1,1) = 0.8272$
- $E[s_2\mid y] = r(0,1)+r(1,1) = 0.4342$

Reconstruction: $\hat y = 2(0.8272) + (-1)(0.4342) = 1.6544 - 0.4342 = 1.2203$
Error: $y-\hat y = 1.5-1.2203 = 0.2797$

Updated weights ($\eta=1$):
- $\Delta g_1 = 0.2797 \times 0.8272 = 0.2314 \Rightarrow g_1^{new} = 2.2314$
- $\Delta g_2 = 0.2797 \times 0.4342 = 0.1215 \Rightarrow g_2^{new} = -0.8785$

E-step = step 1-2 (inference: beliefs about causes updated, $g_1,g_2$ held fixed). M-step = step 3 (learning: parameters updated, beliefs $E[s_i\mid y]$ held fixed). Accept answers within ±1% relative error on final numbers; accept $\eta$ choices other than 1 only if the learner states their own $\eta$ and computes consistently with it — but the task as written fixes $\eta=1$, so flag (not fail) if unstated.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Write out all four joint states $(s_1,s_2)$ and their prior probability before touching the likelihood."
- Hint 2: "The Gaussian normalizing constant $1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$ is identical across all four hypotheses being compared for the same $y$ — it cancels in the ratio, so you only need the relative (unnormalized) values before renormalizing."
- Hint 3: "The general form is $E[s_i\mid y]=\sum_{\text{states where }s_i=1} r(\text{state})$, and the M-step gradient is $\Delta g_i=\eta(y-\hat y)E[s_i\mid y]$ — write these two formulas symbolically before substituting numbers."
- Never: state any of the four responsibilities, either expectation, $\hat y$, the error, or the updated $g_1,g_2$ outright. Never do the arithmetic for the learner, including reducing a partially-simplified expression (e.g. do not evaluate $\exp(-0.125)$ for them).

## Piece 2 — Recovering a planted dictionary with sparse coding

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 90-120 min

**Learning goals**
- Implement the E-step/M-step structure of Piece 1 in its continuous, high-dimensional form: ISTA-style sparse inference of a code, alternated with a gradient update of a dictionary.
- Observe directly that a sparsity-inducing prior, applied to data that really is a sparse combination of localized oriented components, recovers those components from a random initialization.
- Understand the gap between this synthetic demonstration and the empirical natural-image result it stands in for.

**Task**

This is a **synthetic stand-in for natural images** — no image dataset is downloaded. You will generate patches as sparse combinations of a known "ground truth" dictionary of Gabor-like filters, then check whether a sparse-coding fit recovers that dictionary from a random start. This demonstrates the mechanism (sparse prior + sparse-generating data ⇒ sparse-coding learning recovers the generators) but does **not** by itself demonstrate that real photographic natural images have this structure — that empirical claim (Olshausen & Field, and the fit to V1 receptive fields covered in the reading) is about actual image statistics, which have richer structure than "exactly $n$ active Gabor atoms plus Gaussian noise."

Use exactly these parameters so your numeric result is checkable against the key:

- `numpy.random.default_rng(0)`. Patches are $16\times16$ ($d=256$ pixels).
- Planted dictionary: 8 Gabor-like atoms, orientations `theta = linspace(0, pi, 8, endpoint=False)`, each built as `envelope * carrier` with `sigma=3.0`, `freq=0.25`, `phase=0`, centered on the patch, each atom normalized to unit norm. This gives you `D_true`, shape $(256, 8)$.
- Generate $N=2000$ synthetic patches: for each, pick `n_active=2` of the 8 atoms at random (without replacement), draw a coefficient for each from `Uniform(1,3)` times a random sign, sum those weighted atoms, then add pixel noise $\sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.05^2)$.
- Initialize a *learned* dictionary `D` with 8 columns of random Gaussian noise, each normalized to unit norm.
- Alternate for 60 epochs, in minibatches of 100 patches, in random order each epoch:
  - **Inference (E-step analog):** for the current batch, infer a sparse code $A$ by 50 iterations of ISTA: repeatedly take a gradient step on the squared-error term ($A \leftarrow A - \text{step}\cdot(-D^T(X-DA))$, with `step = 1/||D||_2^2` using the spectral norm) followed by a soft threshold at $\lambda=0.2\cdot\text{step}$: $A \leftarrow \mathrm{sign}(A)\max(|A|-\lambda,0)$.
  - **Dictionary update (M-step analog):** with $A$ fixed, take one gradient step on $D$: $D \leftarrow D - \eta_D \cdot (-(X-DA)A^T/\text{batchsize})$ with $\eta_D=0.5$, then renormalize each column of $D$ back to unit norm.
- After training, compute the recovery metric: $C = |D_{true}^T D|$ (absolute value handles the sign ambiguity of each learned atom), then for each of the 8 true atoms take its best match, `best_match = C.max(axis=1)`. Report `best_match` and its mean and min.

Keep the whole script under ~100 lines (the data-generation half above is boilerplate; the alternating fit loop is the exercise).

**Deliverable**

Your script, its printed `best_match` array with mean and min, and 3-5 sentences: (a) what the mean/min tell you about recovery, (b) what would you expect if you set $\lambda=0$ (no sparsity term) instead — would you still expect oriented, localized recovered atoms? Why or why not? — and (c) one sentence on what this synthetic result does and does not demonstrate about real V1 receptive fields.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Implements ISTA inference + dictionary gradient step correctly; mean best-match cosine similarity ≥ 0.9 (from a faithful run of the given recipe, this should land near 0.99); correctly reasons that $\lambda=0$ removes the pressure toward sparse/localized solutions (recovery would degrade toward diffuse, non-Gabor-like components, closer to a PCA-type basis); states the synthetic-vs-real-image caveat accurately. |
| Adequate | Alternating algorithm works and shows clear (if partial) recovery, e.g. mean similarity roughly 0.7-0.9, possibly from small deviations in hyperparameters or iteration counts; gets the qualitative $\lambda=0$ reasoning right even if untested; states the caveat, if loosely. |
| Not yet | No sparsity term implemented (plain least-squares dictionary fit), or the dictionary update has no alternation with inference (e.g. one-shot regression), or recovery is near chance (mean similarity below ~0.5) with no diagnosis of why, or the caveat about synthetic-vs-real is omitted or reversed (i.e. claims this proves the V1 result about real images). |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Reference implementation was run exactly as specified. Output:

```
per-atom best cosine sim: [0.999 0.999 0.999 0.999 0.999 0.999 0.999 0.999]
mean: 0.9989   min: 0.9985
```

This is deterministic given `default_rng(0)` and the exact recipe above; expect the learner's run to land at or very near these numbers if implemented faithfully. A sanity check with a different seed (`default_rng(1)`, otherwise identical) gave `mean: 0.9445, min: 0.7328` (per-atom `[0.996 0.985 0.892 0.733 0.955 0.997 0.999 0.998]`) — i.e. recovery is generally strong but the *specified* seed was chosen because it gives an unambiguous, clean result; don't fail a learner whose reasonable variations (different but sane step size, iteration count, or seed) land somewhat lower — use the mean ≥ 0.9 / mean ≥ 0.7 thresholds in the rubric, not exact match to 0.9989.

On (b): with $\lambda=0$, the "inference" step becomes plain least-squares code-fitting (no soft threshold), and the alternating fit reduces to something like an alternating-least-squares factorization — it will still reduce reconstruction error, but nothing pushes individual codes toward using few atoms per patch, so the learned dictionary is free to settle on any basis spanning the same subspace (e.g. a rotated/mixed basis resembling PCA components), not specifically the sparse generators. Expect the learner to reason this qualitatively; running it is a bonus, not required.

On (c): correct caveat content is "this shows the sparse-coding *mechanism* recovers known sparse generators when the data really is built from a small sparse dictionary; it does not independently establish that real photographs have that statistical structure — that's the separate empirical finding (Olshausen & Field, and the V1 comparison in the reading) about actual image patches." Flag any answer claiming the synthetic run proves the natural-image result.

- Hint 2: "`step = 1/||D||_2^2` needs the spectral norm (`numpy.linalg.norm(D, 2)`), not the Frobenius norm (`numpy.linalg.norm(D)` default) — check which you're calling."
- Hint 3: "For the $\lambda=0$ question, ask what happens to the code $A$ inference step if you delete the soft-threshold line entirely — what optimization problem does that leave, and does it have a unique solution for the dictionary?"

## Piece 3 — Guided reading

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 45-60 min

**Learning goals**
- Locate, in the chapter's own terms, the density-estimation framing of representation learning and the causal-model/recognition-model machinery.
- Be able to state the mechanical difference between sparse coding's and ICA's constraints on the code.
- Identify the statistical property of natural images responsible for the V1-like result, and the inference/learning roles of E- and M-steps, in the chapter's own account.

**Task**

Answer the following from D&A ch. 10, citing which named section (*Introduction*, *Density Estimation*, *Causal Models for Density Estimation*, *Discussion*) each answer comes from. Answer in your own words, not as a transcription.

1. (*Introduction*) The chapter reframes what earlier chapters treated as a stimulus-response mapping problem as a different kind of problem entirely. What is that problem, and what quantity (over what variable) does a generative/causal model try to characterize instead of a conditional firing-rate model?
2. (*Density Estimation*) What does "density estimation" mean as a goal for a model of neural representation, and how does the distribution a generative model assigns differ in role from the distribution a recognition model assigns?
3. (*Causal Models for Density Estimation*) Define, in this chapter's terms, the three pieces of a causal model: the causes, the generative model, and the recognition model. What does each one map to what?
4. (*Causal Models for Density Estimation*) [AC5] The fitting procedure alternates two steps. Which step corresponds to inference and which to learning? What goes wrong — concretely, not just "it won't work" — if you repeatedly ran only one of the two steps?
5. (*Causal Models for Density Estimation*) [AC3] What penalty or constraint makes sparse coding's code sparse? How does ICA's independence constraint act differently at the mechanical level (i.e., what is it a constraint *on*, and how does enforcing it differ from penalizing activity directly)?
6. (*Causal Models for Density Estimation* / *Discussion*) [AC4] When sparse coding or ICA is fit to natural image patches, the resulting filters come out oriented and spatially localized rather than, say, Fourier-like (globally periodic). What statistical property of natural images is responsible for this, and why does a sparse/independence-seeking objective pick it up?
7. (*Discussion*) What limitation or open question does the chapter itself raise about causal models of this kind as models of cortical representation?

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — verify each of the following against the physical text before marking; this is expected content, not a quotation, and section boundaries in the learner's edition may not line up exactly with these paraphrases)

1. Reframes the problem as unsupervised: instead of learning $P(r\mid s)$ or a decoder $P(s\mid r)$ for a *given* stimulus set, the goal is to characterize $P(I)$, the distribution over inputs/images itself, so that structure in the input population is discovered rather than assumed.
2. Density estimation = fitting a model distribution to match the distribution of a data ensemble (here, input patterns), rather than mapping inputs to responses. The generative model's distribution is over *inputs given causes*, $P(I\mid u)$ (what causes produce); the recognition model's distribution is over *causes given inputs*, $Q(u\mid I)$ (inferring causes from data) — the two run in opposite directions and are matched to each other during fitting.
3. Causes $u$: latent variables not directly observed, meant to correspond to real independent features/objects in the world. Generative model: $P(I\mid u)$ (and a prior $P(u)$) describing how causes produce inputs. Recognition model: $Q(u\mid I)$, an approximate inversion used to infer plausible causes from an observed input.
4. E-step = inference (infer/update beliefs about causes $u$ given the current generative parameters, i.e. compute or approximate $Q(u\mid I)$); M-step = learning (update the generative parameters given the current beliefs about $u$). Running only the E-step repeatedly with no M-step: beliefs about causes are refined but the generative model itself never improves, so density estimation never happens (you get better inference under a bad/arbitrary model). Running only the M-step repeatedly with no E-step: parameters are updated against stale/fixed beliefs about the causes, so the model chases beliefs that no longer reflect the (updated) parameters — updates can drift or fail to improve the actual data likelihood, since the M-step's guarantee (increasing a lower bound on log-likelihood) only holds when the E-step's beliefs are current.
5. Sparse coding penalizes the code directly — a term in the objective (e.g. an L1-like or log-prior penalty on the magnitude of each coefficient) that pushes individual coefficients toward zero, so few are active per input, while coefficients can remain correlated/dependent with each other. ICA instead imposes statistical independence between the *causes' distributions* — a constraint on the joint distribution of the code elements (that it factorizes), not a direct penalty on magnitude; a code can satisfy independence without being sparse, and sparseness is a byproduct of the marginal shape (heavy-tailed) that independence-seeking on natural-image-like data tends to produce, not the constraint itself.
6. The property is that natural images have higher-order (beyond second-order/covariance) statistical structure — specifically their pixel intensities, when passed through localized oriented filters, produce sparse, heavy-tailed (kurtotic) response distributions, unlike a Gaussian process where decorrelation (e.g. PCA/Fourier) is the whole story. A second-order-only method (PCA) is satisfied by any decorrelating basis, including Fourier-like global ones; a sparse/independence-seeking objective additionally wants each basis function's response to be sparse/non-Gaussian across the image ensemble, and it turns out oriented, spatially localized filters are what produce sparse responses on real images (edges are local and oriented), so the objective selects that particular basis rather than an arbitrary decorrelating one.
7. Any reasonable, text-consistent limitation is acceptable if it is actually raised in the *Discussion* section — expected candidates include: causal models here typically assume linear generative mixing (real sensory causes and their combination in the world/retina may be nonlinear), the independence/sparse-prior assumptions are simplifications not established as biologically exact, and/or open questions about how such unsupervised fitting could be implemented by real local synaptic learning rules. Verify the specific claim raised against the text rather than accepting an answer just because it sounds plausible.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "For Q4, write out what quantity is being held fixed in each step — that tells you which one is inference and which is learning."
- Hint 2: "For Q5, ask: is the penalty a function of one coefficient at a time, or a property of the joint distribution across coefficients?"
- Hint 3: "For Q6, think about what a Fourier basis and PCA already achieve (decorrelation) versus what's left over (the shape of each filter's response histogram across many images)."
- Never: supply the section's content directly, quote or paraphrase specific sentences from the book, or invent a page number. Never confirm or deny a specific factual claim about the text without the teacher having verified it against the physical copy first.

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Expected content, condensed: (1) representation learning = fit $P(I)$ via a causal model with generative $P(I\mid u)$ and recognition $Q(u\mid I)$; EM alternates E (infer $u$ given current parameters) and M (update parameters given current beliefs about $u$) as a tractable way to fit a latent-variable density model, mirroring the Piece 1 arithmetic (compute responsibilities/expectations, then update $g_1,g_2$). (2) Sparse coding penalizes code magnitude directly (few active coefficients per input); ICA constrains the joint distribution of coefficients to factorize (independence), which doesn't itself demand sparsity, though on image-like data an independence-seeking fit tends to land on sparse/heavy-tailed marginals anyway. (3) The learner's Piece 2 dictionary, initialized as random noise, converged (mean best-match cosine similarity ≈0.999 under the specified recipe) to the oriented, localized planted Gabor atoms because the data was built from a sparse combination of exactly such atoms and the L1 penalty rewards codes that use few of them — echoing the textual claim that natural images (real ones) produce sparse responses specifically under oriented, localized filters, which is why the same objective applied to real images (not just this synthetic stand-in) lands on V1-like filters rather than Fourier-like ones. (4) Without alternation, either beliefs about causes never adapt to an improving model (stale recognition, model never actually fits the data) or parameters get updated against beliefs that no longer correspond to the current parameters (updates can stop reliably increasing the likelihood bound) — same content as Piece 3 Q4. Closing sentence should name the generative/recognition split as structurally the same shape as their PC project without expanding on precision-weighting or free energy.
