This lesson is diagnostic. No new material. Each piece is scoped small on purpose — the goal is to surface exactly where the pre-A1 foundation has decayed after the ~3-month gap, not to re-teach it. A miss on any piece is logged as a named gap to close before A1, not remediated inline.

## Running order

Consume, then apply, then refine:

**audio overview → guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written defence (Piece 4)**

Pieces are numbered by type, not by sequence. The reading front-loads the concepts, the derivation makes them exact, the simulation is where they stick and get refined, and the defence is where you find out whether they did.

## Piece 1 — STDP sign/magnitude and LIF threshold-crossing

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 20 min

**Learning goals**
- Apply the pairwise-exponential STDP rule to compute a signed weight change from raw spike timing, not just recall "pre-before-post potentiates."
- Read magnitude, not just sign, off the STDP exponential — connect Δt position in the ~20 ms window to how much the synapse actually changes.
- Solve the LIF sub-threshold trajectory in closed form and extract a threshold-crossing time in both ms and units of τ_m.
- Explain the asymptotic (never-arrives) property of the LIF trajectory from the functional form itself, not just "because it fires first."

**Task**

Part A — STDP. Use the pairwise exponential rule
Δw(Δt) = A · exp(−|Δt| / τ) , sign = + if pre leads post (Δt > 0), − if post leads pre (Δt < 0), where Δt = t_post − t_pre,
with A = 0.01, τ = 20 ms.

A pre-synaptic spike fires 8 ms before the post-synaptic spike.
(a) State Δt (with sign) for this pairing, and compute Δw.
(b) Compute Δw for Δt = +2 ms under the same rule.
(c) State the direction (potentiation/depression) for both, and say which of the two (Δt = 2 ms or Δt = 8 ms) sits closer to the window's peak vs. its tail, in terms of the resulting magnitude.

Part B — LIF. A LIF neuron has τ_m = 10 ms, resting potential V_rest = −65 mV, threshold V_th = −55 mV. A constant input current drives the neuron toward a steady-state (asymptotic) potential V_ss = −50 mV. The sub-threshold trajectory is
V(t) = V_ss + (V_rest − V_ss) · exp(−t / τ_m).
(a) Solve for t*, the time at which V(t*) = V_th. Give t* in ms and as a multiple of τ_m.
(b) Using the functional form of V(t) — not a hand-wave about refractoriness — explain why V(t) never algebraically equals V_ss for any finite t.

**Deliverable**

One page (handwritten or typed): the two Δw values with sign and 3-significant-figure magnitude, the direction/peak-vs-tail statement, the solved t* with working shown, and the asymptote explanation in 2–3 sentences.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Correct signed Δw for both Δt to ≥2 sig figs; correctly identifies both as potentiating with Δt=8ms weaker/closer to the tail; t* in [10.9, 11.1] ms (≈1.10 τ_m) with algebra shown; explains non-arrival via the exponential term itself (e^(−t/τ) > 0 for all finite t). |
| Adequate | Correct sign/direction on both Δt values even if magnitude arithmetic is off; correctly orders the two magnitudes (2 ms > 8 ms); t* in [9, 13] ms via a recognisable version of the right method; gives at least one valid reason V(t) doesn't reach V_ss, even if it's only the fire-and-reset argument. |
| Not yet | Wrong sign/direction on either STDP case; no quantitative attempt at t*; or asserts V(t) reaches V_ss in finite time. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Part A: Δt = +8 ms (pre leads post) → Δw = 0.01 · e^(−8/20) = 0.01 · e^(−0.4) = 0.01 × 0.6703 = **0.006703** (potentiation).
Δt = +2 ms → Δw = 0.01 · e^(−2/20) = 0.01 · e^(−0.1) = 0.01 × 0.9048 = **0.009048** (potentiation).
Both potentiate — pre leads post in both cases, so sign doesn't change, only magnitude. Δt=8ms gives ~74% of the Δt=2ms magnitude (0.006703/0.009048 = 0.741): Δt=2ms sits near the window's peak (Δt→0), Δt=8ms is well down toward the tail (still within the ~20ms window but meaningfully decayed).

Part B: −55 = −50 + (−65−(−50))·e^(−t/10) = −50 − 15·e^(−t/10)
⇒ −5 = −15·e^(−t/10) ⇒ e^(−t/10) = 1/3 ⇒ t = 10·ln(3) = 10 × 1.0986 = **10.99 ms ≈ 1.10 τ_m**.
Never reaches V_ss: e^(−t/τ_m) > 0 for every finite t, so V(t) = V_ss + (positive or negative gap)·(strictly positive shrinking factor) is always strictly between V_rest and V_ss; equality is only a t→∞ limit. (The reset-before-arrival argument is also true and gets partial credit, but by itself doesn't explain why the pure sub-threshold curve is asymptotic — that's the exponential's own property.)

Common wrong turns: flipping the Δt sign convention (computing t_pre − t_post instead of t_post − t_pre); comparing |Δw| without noting both are still potentiating; sign errors when isolating the exponential in the LIF algebra (yielding negative t*); citing only "refractory period" for part B(b) without the asymptotic argument.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: Which spike happened first, pre or post? What sign does that give Δt under the definition Δt = t_post − t_pre?
- Hint 2: Write out the LIF closed form exactly as given and isolate the exponential term before taking any logarithm.
- Hint 3: Compare the two Δw values as a ratio (e^(−0.4)/e^(−0.1)) rather than computing them in isolation — does the direction of that ratio match "farther from Δt=0 means weaker change"?
- Never: never state the numeric Δw values or t* outright; never perform the log-isolation step for the learner; never confirm correctness before the learner has shown their own algebra.

## Piece 2 — Synaptic scaling vs. unconstrained Hebbian/STDP drift

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 20 min

**Learning goals**
- Show, not just state, that unbounded local Hebbian/STDP-style potentiation is a positive feedback loop with no internal restoring force.
- Implement a slow, periodic multiplicative correction and observe it hold a fast local rule's runaway in check.
- Connect the required timescale separation (corrective step must be slow relative to the learning rule) to why synaptic scaling runs on hours-to-days rather than STDP's tens-of-ms.

**Task**

Implement the following in NumPy (no dataset, no torch needed, <60 lines) and run it as specified — exact recipe, not a rough sketch:

```
seed numpy RNG with 0
N = 20                      # synapses onto one postsynaptic unit
r_pre = np.random.uniform(3.0, 8.0, size=N)   # fixed presynaptic rates, drawn immediately after seeding
w = full array of 20 values, all 0.5           # initial weights
eta = 0.01
w_max = 2.0
r_target = 5.0
K = 25                       # scaling correction interval, in steps
T = 300                      # total steps

for t in range(T):
    r_post = dot(w, r_pre) / N
    dw = eta * r_pre * r_post / N
    w = clip(w + dw, 0, w_max)
    if <scaling condition is on> and (t+1) % K == 0:
        r_post_now = dot(w, r_pre) / N
        factor = clip(r_target / r_post_now, 0.5, 1.5)
        w = clip(w * factor, 0, w_max)
    record mean(w) and r_post
```

Run this twice: **Condition A** with scaling off for all 300 steps, **Condition B** with scaling on. Print mean(w) and r_post at steps 50, 150, and 300 for each condition.

Then answer in 2–3 sentences each:
(b) Why does Condition A's weight growth not self-limit — what makes this a positive feedback loop rather than a settling process?
(c) The real biological cadence for synaptic scaling is hours-to-days, far slower than STDP's tens-of-ms. Given what you observed by triggering the correction every 25 steps here (fast, for observability), what would go wrong for learning if scaling ran on the same tens-of-ms timescale as STDP itself?

**Deliverable**

The script, its printed output at steps 50/150/300 for both conditions, and the two short written answers.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Reproduces both conditions' trajectories within ~5% of the grader-key values; correctly states A saturates to w_max with r_post well above r_target and stays there, while B's r_post returns close to r_target after every correction and never saturates; (b) names the mechanism as dw ∝ r_post ∝ w — a positive-feedback loop with no restoring term; (c) explains that fast scaling would cancel/erase the very correlation-driven changes STDP needs to accumulate before they can register as a stable learned structure. |
| Adequate | Qualitatively correct pattern (A saturates and overshoots target, B stays bounded near target) even if exact numbers drift; (b) identifies "more weight → more post activity → more potentiation" without naming it as feedback explicitly; (c) states scaling-too-fast would "interfere with learning" without the mechanism of erasing correlation-driven change. |
| Not yet | A and B show no meaningful difference, or B also saturates; no attempt at (b)/(c), or answers contradict the observed simulation. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Verified by running the exact recipe above (`np.random.seed(0)`, `r_pre = np.random.uniform(3.0, 8.0, size=20)` drawn immediately after seeding):

Condition A (no scaling): step 50 → mean(w)=1.206, r_post=7.351. Step 150 → mean(w)=2.000 (already fully saturated — all 20 weights clipped at w_max), r_post=11.816. Step 300 → unchanged, mean(w)=2.000, r_post=11.816. Full saturation (all weights = 2.0) occurs by step ≈120. Final r_post is more than 2.3× r_target.

Condition B (scaling on): step 50 → mean(w)=0.820, r_post=5.000 (measured right after the step-50 correction). Step 150 → mean(w)=0.805, r_post=5.000. Step 300 → mean(w)=0.803, r_post=5.000. Over the last 100 steps, mean(w) ranges ≈0.80–1.24 and r_post ranges from 5.000 (immediately post-correction) up to ≈7.7 (just before the next correction) — bounded oscillation, never touching w_max.

Expected-result statement to check against: A's weights hit and stay at the w_max ceiling with r_post never returning toward target; B's weights never reach the ceiling and r_post is pulled back to within a few % of r_target=5.0 every K=25 steps.

(b) dw = eta·r_pre·r_post/N and r_post = dot(w,r_pre)/N, so dw is monotonically increasing in w with no term that decreases as w grows — there is no restoring force, so weight growth compounds until the external clip (w_max) — not any property of the rule — stops it. This is exactly the runaway synaptic scaling exists to correct: unconstrained Hebbian/STDP potentiation has no built-in ceiling tied to a target rate, only an arbitrary hardware bound.

(c) If the corrective rescaling ran on STDP's own tens-of-ms timescale, it would renormalize weights before any given correlation-driven potentiation/depression event could accumulate into a stable, learned change — it would suppress the very signal STDP is trying to encode (destroying learned selectivity), rather than acting only on the slow net drift/imbalance that indicates runaway. The timescale separation is what lets scaling correct the *aggregate* trend while leaving individual STDP events intact.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: Look at how dw depends on r_post, and how r_post depends on w — trace the loop.
- Hint 2: In Condition A, check at what step every one of the 20 weights first equals exactly 2.0 — what does "exactly" tell you about what's actually stopping growth?
- Hint 3: For (c), ask what a correction applied every single STDP step would do to a weight change that STDP had just made a few ms earlier.
- Never: never provide the printed step-50/150/300 values before the learner has run their own script; never fix bugs in the learner's implementation by rewriting their loop — point at the specific line/quantity that looks wrong instead.

## Piece 3 — Guided reading: rate, latency, and population coding trade-offs

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 20 min

**Learning goals**
- Ground the rate-vs-latency trade-off in the Poisson-counting-statistics argument, with real numbers, rather than a slogan.
- Name latency coding's specific fragility (dependence on a shared, low-jitter reference/onset time) rather than a vague "it's less robust."
- State how population coding changes the trade-off by substituting simultaneous samples across neurons for a single neuron's temporal average.

**Source:** Dayan & Abbott, *Theoretical Neuroscience* (2001), ch. 1 ("Spike Trains and Firing Rates" / "The Neural Code" sections) and ch. 3 ("Population Decoding").

**Task**

Answer using only the cited chapters — no outside material.

1. D&A frames a firing-rate estimate as a spike count over an averaging window T. For a neuron firing at a steady 50 Hz, compute the expected spike count and the Poisson-based relative uncertainty (SD / mean) of the rate estimate for T = 20 ms and for T = 200 ms. State in one sentence what this numeric contrast demonstrates about the rate code's window-length trade-off.
2. Latency (time-to-first-spike) coding conveys a value in a single spike's timing rather than a count. Name the one concrete property of a single-spike estimate — tied to what a rate/count code gets from averaging over a window that a single spike cannot — that makes latency coding fragile, and say specifically what kind of external timing information a decoder needs in order to read a latency code at all.
3. From ch. 3's treatment of population decoding: explain how averaging simultaneously over many noisy, individually rate-coding neurons lets a population recover a fast *and* precise estimate of a variable, without any single neuron needing a long temporal averaging window. In one sentence, state how this changes the rate-vs-latency trade-off from Q1–Q2.
4. Apply it: for a task judging two similar, sustained tactile pressures over ~200 ms, where the contact onset is soft and not sharply timed — which coding scheme is favoured, and which specific weakness of latency coding named in Q2 is the deciding factor?

**Deliverable**

Four short written answers (2–4 sentences each, Q1 must include the numeric working).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Q1 numbers correct (counts 1 and 10, relative uncertainties 100% and ~31.6%) with the "longer window trades time for precision" conclusion stated; Q2 names dependence on a shared low-jitter reference/onset time as the specific fragility, not a generic "noise" answer; Q3 correctly identifies population averaging as substituting spatial (across-neuron) samples for temporal ones; Q4 picks rate/population coding and explicitly cites the onset-jitter weakness from Q2 as the reason. |
| Adequate | Q1 has the right direction (longer window = lower relative uncertainty) with minor numeric slips; Q2 identifies some form of timing fragility even if not framed as "reference-time dependence"; Q3 gestures at "more neurons = more reliable" without the temporal-vs-spatial averaging framing; Q4 correct answer with a weaker justification. |
| Not yet | Q1 shows no attempt at the Poisson arithmetic; Q2 answer is generic ("latency coding is less accurate") with no specific mechanism; Q4 picks latency coding for this task, or gives no reason tied to Q2. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Q1: T=20ms → mean count = 50 Hz × 0.02 s = 1 spike, Poisson SD = √1 = 1, relative uncertainty = 1/1 = **100%**. T=200ms → mean count = 50 × 0.2 = 10 spikes, SD = √10 ≈ 3.16, relative uncertainty ≈ 3.16/10 = **31.6%**. Demonstrates: a 10× longer window only buys about a 3.2× reduction in relative uncertainty (√T scaling), so rate coding trades temporal resolution for precision at a diminishing, not linear, rate — and a very short window (20ms, 1 spike) is essentially uninformative on its own.

Q2: A single spike's timing has no repeated samples to average over noise the way a count over a window does, so its estimate variance is entirely exposed to trial-to-trial jitter in spike generation *and* to jitter in whatever the decoder uses as "time zero." Concretely: reading a latency code requires the decoder to have access to a precise, shared, low-jitter reference/onset time (e.g., stimulus onset) common to encoder and decoder — without that, the latency is meaningless, since latency is only defined relative to some t=0. This reference-time dependency is the specific fragility.

Q3: Many neurons, each individually noisy/rate-coding, sampled at the same instant, give the decoder N simultaneous (not sequential) draws to average over — this substitutes spatial averaging (across the population, in effect instantaneously) for the temporal averaging a lone rate-coding neuron would need, recovering speed without sacrificing the precision a long window would have bought.

Q4: Rate (or population-rate) coding is favoured. The deciding factor is Q2's onset-jitter/reference-time dependency: a soft, ill-defined contact onset corrupts the "time zero" a first-spike-latency code requires, while a spike-count/rate estimate over the full ~200ms window is insensitive to exactly when within that window the spikes occur, so it tolerates onset ambiguity that latency coding cannot.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: For Q1, what is the Poisson relationship between the mean spike count and its standard deviation? Apply that before worrying about "windows" in the abstract.
- Hint 2: For Q2, ask "relative to what does a first-spike latency get measured?" — chase that question to its answer.
- Hint 3: For Q3, contrast averaging "over time, one neuron" against averaging "over neurons, one instant" — what do both actually buy the decoder?
- Never: never supply the chapter's specific page numbers or quote passages verbatim as a substitute for the learner reading them; never give the Q1 numeric answer before the learner has attempted the Poisson computation themselves.

## Piece 4 — Written defence: does a global broadcast scalar structurally cap credit assignment?

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 25 min · 300–600 words

**Learning goals**
- Pressure-test the learner's own "misted chemical" mental model of three-factor learning against a concrete structural comparison to backprop's chain rule.
- Distinguish what a local eligibility trace varies per-synapse from what a global broadcast scalar does *not* vary per-synapse.
- Connect the answer to why this project's local error-neuron-per-neuron-pair design is a structurally different (not just "biologically plausible") mechanism from a single global scalar.

**Task**

The learner has previously described three-factor learning as: "a success/error chemical is misted over the whole network at once; each synapse changes in proportion to how brightly it's still glowing from recent activity."

Write a 300–600 word argued piece answering: **does this model — one global broadcast value combined with local per-synapse eligibility traces — structurally prevent the system from doing something backprop's chain rule can do? If so, name precisely what, and where in the mechanism the limitation bites. If not, defend why the eligibility trace is enough.**

The piece must explicitly address:
(a) What, specifically, does backprop's chain rule compute per-synapse that a single scalar broadcast to every synapse does not and cannot compute on its own?
(b) Is the limitation (if any) fixable by making the eligibility trace more sophisticated, or is it inherent to using *one* shared scalar no matter how the trace is designed? Justify the distinction.
(c) One sentence connecting the answer to why this project's design gives each neuron pair its *own* local error neuron rather than one global signal — is that choice addressing exactly this limitation, or a different problem?

**Deliverable**

300–600 words of prose (not bullet points), addressing (a), (b), (c) explicitly.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Correctly identifies that backprop computes a distinct, causally-specific signal per parameter (∂L/∂w, which differs across synapses because it depends on that synapse's specific downstream path and weights), while a global scalar is identical at every synapse and only the *trace* differs; correctly argues this is structural (no redesign of the trace alone can manufacture per-synapse information about a synapse's specific causal contribution to a downstream error, because the scalar itself carries only "how well did things go," not "how much did you specifically matter"); correctly ties (c) to per-neuron-pair local error signals being a genuinely different mechanism (structurally computed local error, not a broadcast) rather than merely "more biological." |
| Adequate | Identifies that backprop gives per-parameter differentiation and a global scalar doesn't, but explanation of *why* it's structural (vs. just "backprop is more precise") is thin; (c) makes the connection but without clearly distinguishing "different mechanism" from "same mechanism, better tuned." |
| Not yet | Treats the two mechanisms as differing only in degree of accuracy/noise rather than in what information is structurally available at the synapse; skips (a), (b), or (c) entirely; word count far outside 300–600. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Core point the essay must land on: backprop's chain rule delivers ∂L/∂w_ij, a value that is *different for every synapse* because it is computed by propagating the error back through the *specific* downstream weights and derivatives that synapse's output subsequently passes through — it encodes "how much did this exact synapse's output causally matter to the final error, given everything downstream of it." A three-factor rule's global scalar (e.g. a dopamine-like reward-prediction-error signal) is, by construction, the *same number* received at every synapse in the network at that moment; the only thing that varies synapse-to-synapse is the local eligibility trace (recent pre/post correlation "glow"). That means the system can modulate "how much to change" per synapse based on *how active/correlated it recently was*, but it cannot encode "how much this specific synapse's specific causal path contributed to *this specific* downstream error" — because that information (the downstream Jacobian/weight structure) is never computed or transmitted anywhere in the three-factor mechanism. This is structural, not a trace-design problem: no matter how rich the eligibility trace is made (longer memory, multiple timescales, nonlinear), it is still built only from local pre/post activity history, which contains no information about downstream weights or downstream errors elsewhere in the network — that information physically doesn't reach the synapse under a single global broadcast. Making the trace fancier can encode *which* recent event to credit, but not *how causally responsible this synapse was for a specific downstream outcome relative to other synapses on other paths*.

(c) tie-in: this project's design — each neuron pair carries its own error neuron, wired from that prediction neuron's own connections, rather than one global signal for the whole network — is addressing exactly this limitation: each local error is a *structurally computed*, neuron-specific signal (derived from that neuron's own local prediction error), not a broadcast modulated only by a trace. It's a genuinely different mechanism, not a more finely-tuned version of the three-factor scalar. (A learner who instead argues "it's the same idea, just localized further" should be pushed on why per-neuron-pair errors are computed locally from that pair's own inputs/predictions, not received identically at every pair — that's the structural difference.)

Common wrong turns: arguing the eligibility trace itself "solves" credit assignment (it doesn't — it only says when to apply a change, not why differently across synapses relative to a specific downstream error); treating this as a noise/precision issue rather than an information-availability issue; conflating "local" (PC's per-pair error) with "global" (three-factor's broadcast) as though they're the same category of mechanism because both avoid literal backprop.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: What single quantity does every synapse in the network receive under the three-factor model at a given moment — is it the same number everywhere, or different at each synapse?
- Hint 2: If two synapses have identical eligibility traces but sit on completely different downstream paths with different consequences for the final error, does the three-factor rule change them differently? What would have to be true for it to?
- Hint 3: Ask what information would need to physically arrive at a synapse for it to know "how much I specifically mattered to that downstream error" — where would that information come from, and does the three-factor mechanism carry it?
- Never: never write or outline the essay's paragraphs for the learner; never supply the phrase "structural, not fixable by a better trace" as a given conclusion — the learner must argue their way there; never confirm which side of (a)/(b) is "correct" mid-draft.
