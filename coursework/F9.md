## Piece 1 — Hebbian instability and the Oja fixed point

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 60-75 min

**Learning goals**
- Solve the linear ODE for an unconstrained rate-based Hebbian synapse and show the weight has no finite steady state.
- Derive the general fixed-point condition for Oja's rule and show it reduces to the eigenvector equation of the input covariance.
- Linearize Oja's dynamics around two candidate fixed points and use the sign of the resulting rates to determine which is stable.
- State, in one paragraph, why Oja's rule finds specifically the *top* eigenvector and not any eigenvector.

**Task**

*Part (a) — the instability.* A linear unit has output $y = wx$ driven by a presynaptic input with constant variance $\sigma_x^2 = 4$. It follows the basic (rate-based) Hebb rule $\dot w = \eta \langle yx \rangle = \eta \sigma_x^2 w$, with $\eta = 0.1$.
  (i) Solve this ODE for $w(t)$ given $w(0) = 0.01$.
  (ii) Evaluate $w(t)$ at $t=5$ and $t=20$ (time in whatever units make $\eta$ dimensionless-per-unit-time).
  (iii) State the limit as $t \to \infty$, and explain in one or two sentences why this is biologically impossible for a real synapse (tie it to LTP saturation).

*Part (b) — the fixed point.* Now a linear unit has a 2-D input with covariance matrix $C = \mathrm{diag}(3, 1)$ (so $e_1=(1,0)$ and $e_2=(0,1)$ are already its eigenvectors, with eigenvalues $\lambda_1=3$, $\lambda_2=1$). Oja's rule is $\dot w = \eta\left(Cw - (w^\top C w)\,w\right)$.
  (i) Show algebraically that setting $\dot w = 0$ requires $Cw^* = (w^{*\top}Cw^*)\,w^*$, and that for any *unit-norm* $w^*$ this is exactly the eigenvector equation $Cw^* = \lambda w^*$ with $\lambda = w^{*\top}Cw^*$.
  (ii) Take $\eta = 0.05$. Linearize around $w^* = (1,0)$: write $w = (1+\delta_1, \delta_2)$, substitute into $\dot w$, drop terms of order $\delta^2$ and higher, and derive the two first-order rates $\dot\delta_1 = a\,\delta_1$ and $\dot\delta_2 = b\,\delta_2$.
  (iii) Repeat the same linearization around $w^* = (0,1)$, writing $w=(\delta_1, 1+\delta_2)$, and derive the corresponding rates $c\,\delta_1$ and $d\,\delta_2$.
  (iv) Using the four signed rates from (ii)-(iii), classify each fixed point (stable node vs. saddle) and state the general rule connecting the sign of the transverse rate to $(\lambda_i - \lambda_1)$.

**Deliverable**

A written derivation (typed or scanned handwriting) covering (a)(i)-(iii) and (b)(i)-(iv), showing every algebraic step — not just final expressions — plus the four numeric rates from (b) and a one-paragraph statement of the general result: *"Among unit vectors, Oja's rule's only asymptotically stable fixed point is the eigenvector of the largest eigenvalue of $C$."*

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All sub-parts solved correctly with shown algebra; both fixed points correctly classified with correct signed rates; closing paragraph correctly generalizes beyond the diagonal example (states the result in terms of $\lambda_i - \lambda_1$, not just "it worked for this matrix"). |
| Adequate | Part (a) fully correct. Part (b)(i) correct. At least one of the two linearizations in (b)(ii)/(b)(iii) correct with correct sign; final classification of *at least* $w^*=(1,0)$ as stable is correct, even if the saddle argument is thin. |
| Not yet | Part (a) has a sign or algebra error changing the qualitative conclusion (e.g. concludes decay instead of growth), or part (b) never reaches a signed rate — only asserts "eigenvectors are fixed points" without doing the linearization. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- (a)(i): $\dot w = 0.4w \Rightarrow w(t) = 0.01\,e^{0.4t}$.
- (a)(ii): $w(5) = 0.01e^{2} \approx 0.0739$; $w(20) = 0.01e^{8} \approx 29.81$.
- (a)(iii): $w(t)\to\infty$. Biologically impossible: synaptic strength is bounded by finite receptor number / vesicle release capacity — a synapse cannot conduct infinite current. This is the standard motivation for normalization (criterion 2 in the spec).
- (b)(i): $Cw = (3(1+\delta_1),\,\delta_2)$ at $w^*=(1,0)$ (or the analogous expression at the other point); to first order $w^\top C w \approx \lambda_1 + 2\lambda_1\delta_1$ (or $\lambda_2 + 2\lambda_2\delta_2$ at the other point). Setting $\dot w=0$ at $\delta=0$ recovers $Cw^*=\lambda_1 w^*$ — the eigenvector equation. General statement: any unit eigenvector is a fixed point because $w^{*\top}Cw^*=\lambda$ exactly when $Cw^*=\lambda w^*$ and $\|w^*\|=1$.
- (b)(ii) around $(1,0)$, $\eta=0.05$: $\dot\delta_1 \approx -6\eta\,\delta_1 = -0.3\,\delta_1$; $\dot\delta_2 \approx \eta(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)\,\delta_2 = 0.05(1-3)\delta_2 = -0.1\,\delta_2$. Both negative → stable node.
- (b)(iii) around $(0,1)$, $\eta=0.05$: $\dot\delta_2 \approx -2\eta\,\delta_2 = -0.1\,\delta_2$ (stable along its own axis); $\dot\delta_1 \approx \eta(\lambda_1-\lambda_2)\,\delta_1 = 0.05(3-1)\delta_1 = +0.1\,\delta_1$ (grows transversely). One positive, one negative → saddle.
- (b)(iv): $(1,0)$ (top eigenvalue $\lambda_1=3$) is the stable node; $(0,1)$ (the other eigenvalue) is a saddle, unstable to any perturbation toward the $(1,0)$ direction. General rule: the transverse rate at eigenvector $i$ has sign of $(\lambda_j - \lambda_i)$ for the competing eigenvalue $\lambda_j$; only the top eigenvector has no eigenvalue larger than itself, so it is the unique fully-stable direction.
- Accept sign conventions or $\eta$ folded differently as long as the *ratio* of rates and the stable/saddle classification match.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: Ask what $\langle x^2\rangle$ equals for the given $\sigma_x^2$, or ask them to write $\dot w = \eta \langle y x\rangle$ with $y=wx$ explicitly before touching part (b).
- Hint 2: If they're stuck on the linearization, tell them to expand $w^\top C w$ to first order in $\delta$ *before* substituting into $\dot w$, and to drop $\delta^2$ terms only after that expansion, not before.
- Hint 3: If they still can't get a signed rate, give them the *form* of the answer ("the transverse rate is $\eta$ times a difference of two eigenvalues — which two, and in which order, at each fixed point?") without giving the number.
- Never: never supply the final rates or the stable/saddle classification directly; never solve the ODE in (a) for them; never confirm "yes that's right" before they've shown the algebra step that produces the sign.

## Piece 2 — Oja vs. Hebb vs. two normalizations, on synthetic covariance-known data

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 90-120 min

**Learning goals**
- Verify, numerically, that Oja's rule converges onto the principal eigenvector of a *known* input covariance — computed independently by hand.
- Watch plain unnormalized Hebbian learning diverge on the same data and characterize the divergence quantitatively.
- Reproduce the qualitative difference between subtractive and multiplicative normalization: saturation-prone vs. smooth weight distributions.

**Task**

Write one self-contained Python script (numpy only, no downloads, under 100 lines) with three parts. Exact parameters below are mandatory — do not substitute your own, since results are checked numerically.

*Part A — Oja's rule finds the eigenvector.*
1. By hand, *before* writing any code: for $C = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, compute both eigenvalues and the eigenvector belonging to the larger eigenvalue, normalized to unit length. Show the characteristic polynomial and the null-space computation — don't just quote a numerical eigensolver.
2. Generate data: `rng = np.random.RandomState(0)`, `Z = rng.randn(5000, 2)`, Cholesky-factor $C$ by hand as $L=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt2 & 0\\ 1/\sqrt2 & \sqrt{0.5}\end{pmatrix}$ (verify $LL^\top=C$ yourself), then `X = Z @ L.T`.
3. Implement Oja's rule as an online update over all 5000 rows in order: $y = w\cdot x$, $w \leftarrow w + \eta(yx - y^2w)$, with $\eta=0.01$, $w_0=(1,0)$.
4. Report the final $w$, $\|w\|$, and the cosine similarity between final $w$ and your hand-computed eigenvector from step 1.

*Part B — plain Hebbian diverges.*
5. On the *same* `X` from Part A (same order), run the unnormalized rule $w \leftarrow w + \eta\,y\,x$, $\eta=0.01$, $w_0=(0.05, 0)$.
6. Record $\|w\|$ after steps 100, 500, 1000, and 5000 (0-indexed: "after step $k$" = after processing $k$ rows). Report the final normalized direction $w/\|w\|$ and compare it, in one sentence, to Part A's converged direction.

*Part C — subtractive vs. multiplicative normalization.*
7. Build 10 synapses' worth of input: `rng2 = np.random.RandomState(1)`, `gains = np.linspace(0.7, 1.3, 10)`, `base = rng2.randn(8000)`, `Xc = gains[None,:]*base[:,None] + 0.3*rng2.randn(8000,10)`, then shift so the minimum entry is exactly $0.1$: `Xc = Xc - Xc.min() + 0.1`.
8. Implement **subtractive** normalization: each step, $y=w\cdot x$, $\Delta w_i = \eta\,y\,(x_i - \bar x)$ (mean-subtracted, so $\sum_i \Delta w_i = 0$ before clipping), then clip every $w_i$ to $[0, 2]$, then re-distribute any excess/deficit from clipping among the *not-yet-saturated* units so that $\sum_i w_i = 10$ exactly is restored each step. $\eta = 0.01$, $w_0 = (1,1,\dots,1)$.
9. Implement **multiplicative** normalization: each step, $w \leftarrow w + \eta\,y\,x$, then rescale $w \leftarrow w\cdot(10/\sum_i w_i)$. Same $\eta$ and $w_0$.
10. Report, for each rule: the final weight vector, how many entries are at or within $10^{-3}$ of a bound (0 or 2), and the standard deviation across the 10 weights.

**Deliverable**

The script itself, plus a short (5-10 sentence) results note reporting: the cosine similarity number from Part A and how many degrees off-axis that implies; the four $\|w\|$ values from Part B and the qualitative growth shape; and the saturation-count / std-dev pair for each rule in Part C, with a one-line statement of which normalization scheme is the "winner-take-all" one.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All three parts implemented exactly to spec (correct RNG calls, correct constants); Part A cosine similarity ≥ 0.99 reported and correctly matched against an independently-derived hand eigenvector (not copied from code output); Part B values match expected order of magnitude at steps 100/500/1000, correctly identified as exponential blow-up; Part C correctly shows more saturated entries and higher std for subtractive than multiplicative, with the right qualitative read on which is "smoother." |
| Adequate | All three parts run and produce *a* result in the right qualitative direction (Oja converges, Hebb diverges, subtractive is more saturated/spread than multiplicative), but one part has a parameter deviation (wrong seed, wrong $\eta$, wrong $w_0$) that the learner didn't catch, or the hand-eigenvector check in Part A is missing/wrong even though the code result is fine. |
| Not yet | Any part is missing, or Oja's rule fails to converge (cosine similarity well below 0.99, e.g. from an update-rule sign error), or the Part C rules are not actually distinguishable in the reported output (same std, same saturation), indicating the two update rules were implemented identically. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — verified by running a reference implementation)

- Hand eigenvalues of $C=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$: trace $=3$, det $=1$; $\lambda = \frac{3\pm\sqrt5}{2}$ → $\lambda_{\max}=2.61803$, $\lambda_{\min}=0.38197$. Eigenvector for $\lambda_{\max}$: solve $(2-\lambda_{\max})v_1+v_2=0 \Rightarrow v_2 = 0.618\,v_1$; normalized: $v_{\max} = (0.85065,\ 0.52573)$.
- Part A reference run: final $w \approx (0.8200,\ 0.5736)$, $\|w\| \approx 1.0007$, cosine similarity to $v_{\max} \approx 0.9984$ (about $3.3^\circ$ off-axis). Accept cosine similarity in $[0.99, 1.0]$; the exact digits depend on implementation details (e.g. loop vs. vectorized) but should not deviate more than ~1% from this.
- Part B reference run (same `X`): $\|w\|$ at step 100 $\approx 0.589$; step 500 $\approx 7.3\times10^3$; step 1000 $\approx 1.03\times10^9$; step 5000 $\approx 2.7\times10^{52}$. Final direction $\approx (0.8205,\ 0.5717)$ — nearly the *same direction* as Part A's converged Oja weight, despite the unbounded magnitude. **Grading note:** because this system is exponentially divergent, exact digits at step 1000/5000 are extremely sensitive to floating-point/summation-order differences between implementations. Grade steps 100 and 500 to ~10% tolerance; for steps 1000/5000 only check that the value is enormous (>10^6 and >10^40 respectively) and monotonically increasing — do not expect digit-level match.
- Part C reference run: subtractive final $w \approx [0,\ 0,\ 0,\ 0.083,\ 1.405,\ 1.459,\ 1.335,\ 2.0,\ 1.888,\ 1.830]$, sum $=10$, 4 entries at/within $10^{-3}$ of a bound, std $\approx 0.825$. Multiplicative final $w \approx [0.932,\ 0.977,\ 0.894,\ 1.017,\ 1.029,\ 0.998,\ 1.053,\ 1.084,\ 1.024,\ 0.993]$, sum $=10$, 0 entries near a bound, std $\approx 0.053$. Expect the learner's numbers to be close to these (same seeds, deterministic algorithm) but allow reasonable tolerance for redistribution-loop implementation differences in the subtractive rule; the qualitative result — subtractive saturates several synapses at 0 or the max while multiplicative stays smooth and near-uniform, std ratio roughly 10-20x — must hold regardless of exact digits.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: If Part A doesn't converge, ask them to print $y$ and $w$ every 500 steps and check whether $\|w\|$ is heading toward 1 or wandering — don't tell them what it should converge to.
- Hint 2: If the direction is wrong but norm looks fine, ask them to re-derive the sign of the $-y^2w$ term from the Oja update (is it decaying $w$ or growing it when $y$ is large?).
- Hint 3: For Part C, if subtractive and multiplicative give near-identical results, ask them to check whether their subtractive rule's mean-subtraction term $(x_i - \bar x)$ is actually being computed per-step from the *current* $x$, not the constraint math being skipped entirely.
- Never: never give the reference numbers above; never hand over a corrected copy of their script; never confirm the hand-computed eigenvector before they show their characteristic-polynomial work from Piece 1's style of derivation.

## Piece 3 — Guided reading: D&A ch. 8

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 45-60 min

**Learning goals**
- Locate, in the primary text, the biological grounding (LTP/LTD, STDP) for the rate-based rules used elsewhere in this lesson.
- Extract the exact mathematical form of each rule (Hebb, covariance, BCM, Oja) as the text presents it, well enough to state it from memory afterward.
- Identify what distinguishes the supervised rules from everything before them in the chapter.

**Task**

Read D&A ch. 8, sections *Introduction*, *Synaptic Plasticity Rules*, *Unsupervised Learning*, and *Supervised Learning*, in that order. Answer the following from the text — do not answer from memory of this course's earlier lessons or from outside sources:

1. (*Introduction*) What two directions of synaptic weight change do LTP and LTD correspond to, and roughly what timescale separates "short-term" from "long-term" potentiation as the chapter frames it?
2. (*Introduction* or *Synaptic Plasticity Rules*, wherever the chapter places it) A presynaptic spike precedes the postsynaptic spike by 10 ms. According to the STDP timing window as the text describes it, is the synapse strengthened or weakened, and what is the approximate half-width (in ms) of the window this falls inside?
3. (*Synaptic Plasticity Rules*) Write the basic Hebb rule in the rate-based form the chapter gives (in terms of presynaptic rate, postsynaptic rate, and a learning rate). What property of this equation's right-hand side is responsible for unbounded growth?
4. (*Synaptic Plasticity Rules*) How does the covariance rule change the basic Hebb rule so that it can produce LTD (weight decrease), not just LTP? What is subtracted, and from which factor (pre- or post-synaptic, or both)?
5. (*Synaptic Plasticity Rules*) What is the BCM rule's sliding threshold usually called, and how does making it depend on a running average of recent postsynaptic activity keep the rule from diverging the way the basic Hebb rule does?
6. (*Unsupervised Learning*) What quantity, defined from the input's second-order statistics, does Oja's rule converge to extract? What two developmental phenomena does the chapter offer as biological instances of this computation (one about which eye drives a cortical cell, one about spatial receptive field structure)?
7. (*Supervised Learning*) Write the delta rule's weight update for a linear unit with target output $d$ and actual output $y$. What single quantity does this rule require that the Hebb, covariance, BCM, and Oja rules never need?
8. (*Supervised Learning*) What condition on the training data does the perceptron convergence theorem require for the perceptron learning procedure to be guaranteed to converge?

**Deliverable**

Written answers to all 8 questions, each citing which named section it came from, in your own words (not copied sentences).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All 8 answered correctly and specifically (e.g. question 3 gives the actual equation, not just "it's Hebbian"); question 2's direction and window are both correct; question 7 correctly names the target/teacher signal as the missing ingredient. |
| Adequate | 6-7 of 8 correct; minor imprecision (e.g. right form of the delta rule but vague on why it needs $d$) but no fundamental misreading of a rule's equation. |
| Not yet | Fewer than 6 correct, or any rule's core equation (Hebb, covariance, BCM, Oja, delta) is stated wrong, or STDP direction (potentiation vs. depression) is backwards. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — **verify each of these against the physical text before grading; this is expected content, not a transcription of the book**)

1. LTP = strengthening, LTD = weakening; "long-term" in the chapter's usage refers to changes that persist from tens of minutes to indefinitely, contrasted with short-term facilitation/depression on the order of seconds. Confirm the exact phrasing/timescale the book uses before marking.
2. Pre-before-post by 10 ms falls in the potentiation (LTP) side of the STDP window — strengthened. Expected half-width is on the order of ~10-30 ms (commonly cited exponential time constant ~20 ms in this literature) — confirm the book's specific number/figure before grading; do not accept an answer outside a biologically sane range (single-digit ms or full seconds should be marked wrong regardless of the exact constant in the text).
3. Rate-based Hebb rule: $\dot w = \eta\, v\, u$ (postsynaptic rate times presynaptic rate, times learning rate) — accept any correct variable naming. Runaway growth: the update has no term that can turn negative when both rates are positive/co-active, so nothing opposes growth — $\dot w$ is always $\geq 0$ whenever pre and post are both active, with no decay term.
4. Covariance rule subtracts a reference/threshold term (an average or expected firing rate, e.g. $\bar v$) from the postsynaptic (or, in some forms, presynaptic) rate before multiplying, so the update can go negative when the rate is below its own average — that's what allows LTD.
5. Usually called $\theta_M$ (the BCM sliding threshold). It rises when recent postsynaptic activity has been high, which flips the sign of the plasticity term for lower activity levels going forward — this is a negative-feedback/homeostatic mechanism, unlike the fixed threshold in the plain covariance rule, so it self-stabilizes rather than needing an external bound.
6. Oja's rule extracts the principal component (top eigenvector direction) of the input covariance/correlation. Biological instances given: ocular dominance (which eye ends up dominating a given cortical cell) and receptive field formation (e.g. orientation-selective or center-surround-like structure) as developmental outcomes of correlation-driven Hebbian competition.
7. Delta rule: $\Delta w = \eta\,(d - y)\,x$ (error times input, times learning rate). Missing ingredient the unsupervised rules never need: an externally supplied target/teacher signal $d$.
8. Perceptron convergence requires the training data to be linearly separable (the classes can be separated by some hyperplane) — without that, the perceptron procedure does not converge to a fixed correct solution.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: Point to the correct named section only ("that's covered under *Synaptic Plasticity Rules*, not *Introduction*") without stating the content.
- Hint 2: For an equation question answered vaguely, ask "what are the two activity variables and the one constant that appear on the right-hand side?" without supplying them.
- Hint 3: Quote back the learner's own answer and ask them to check it against the actual page, if it reads like a guess rather than something read from the text.
- Never: never supply an equation, a section's content, or a timing number outright, even after repeated wrong attempts — redirect to the section and stop; never treat this course's own memory notes as a substitute source for what the text says.

## Piece 4 — Written explanation: from LTP/LTD to two kinds of learning

**Type:** written explanation · **Time:** 45-60 min

**Learning goals**
- Narrate the causal chain from biological timing-dependent plasticity to the rate-based rule taxonomy to the PCA-equivalence result, in one coherent account.
- State, without hedging, why unsupervised rules cannot do what the delta rule does, and vice versa.

**Task**

Write 300-600 words explaining synaptic plasticity as a foundation for learning rules, for a reader who knows the STDP timing-window question and the Hebb/Oja/delta-rule equations exist but hasn't connected them. Your explanation must:
- State the STDP direction/timing fact from acceptance criterion 1 with actual millisecond numbers.
- Explain concretely why the unnormalized Hebb rule is unstable (tie to the ODE result from Piece 1) and what subtractive vs. multiplicative normalization each do about it, referencing the saturation-vs.-smooth contrast from Piece 2's Part C numbers.
- State what Oja's rule provably converges to and connect it, with your Piece 2 Part A numbers (the cosine similarity you obtained), to why the network is "doing statistics."
- Close by contrasting this whole unsupervised family against the delta rule/perceptron: name the one input the supervised rule needs that none of the others do, and why that changes what problems each family can solve.

**Deliverable**

A single 300-600 word written passage (prose, not bullet points), citing at least one real number from your own Piece 1 derivation and at least one real number from your own Piece 2 simulation run.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All four required content points present, correct, and connected into one causal narrative (not four disconnected paragraphs); uses the learner's own actual numbers from Pieces 1 and 2, not generic placeholders; stays within 300-600 words. |
| Adequate | All four content points present and correct, but the piece reads as a list of facts rather than a connected explanation, or reuses this document's example numbers instead of the learner's own run's numbers. |
| Not yet | Missing one of the four required content points, misstates a rule's convergence target or the STDP direction, or is well outside the word range. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Expected content, in the learner's own words and own numbers:
- STDP: pre-before-post by 10 ms → potentiation (LTP); window half-width on the order of ~10-30 ms (verify against text per Piece 3's key before penalizing a specific number).
- Hebb instability: cites something equivalent to Piece 1's $w(t) = 0.01e^{0.4t}$ result (or their own correct rework) as the concrete mechanism, then correctly states subtractive normalization keeps $\sum w_i$ fixed by shifting all weights by a common amount (prone to pushing individual weights into their bounds — high variance/saturation, e.g. the ~0.825 std / 4-saturated-synapse result from Piece 2C) while multiplicative normalization rescales proportionally (smoother, low-variance outcome, e.g. ~0.053 std, none saturated).
- Oja/PCA: states convergence to the top eigenvector of the input covariance, and cites their own Piece 2A cosine-similarity number (expect near 0.998, ~3° off) as empirical confirmation of the Piece 1(b) fixed-point derivation.
- Supervised contrast: names the target/teacher signal $d$ as the needed extra input (delta rule $\Delta w=\eta(d-y)x$), and states that this is what lets supervised rules solve a specified input-output mapping (e.g. classification with a known correct label) versus unsupervised rules which can only extract statistical structure already present in the input (no notion of "correct answer" to converge toward).
