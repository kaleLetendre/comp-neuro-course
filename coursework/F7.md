## Piece 1 — Deriving the cable equation and the length constant

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** ~75 min

**Learning goals**
- Derive the passive cable equation from Kirchhoff's current law on an infinitesimal cylinder segment.
- Extract the electrotonic length constant λ from the balance of axial and membrane-leak resistance.
- Compute λ numerically from given resistivities and diameter, carrying units through.
- Apply the steady-state exponential-attenuation solution to a concrete synapse-to-soma distance.

**Task**

*Part A — derive the cable equation.* Consider a cylindrical dendrite of diameter *d*. Take a segment of length Δx and apply Kirchhoff's current law at its boundary: the axial current entering minus the axial current leaving must equal the membrane leak current plus the capacitive charging current, minus any current injected into the segment. Write the axial current via Ohm's law using an axial resistance per unit length *r_a*, and the leak/capacitive currents using a membrane resistance per unit length *r_m* and membrane capacitance per unit length *c_m*. Take Δx → 0 and show the resulting PDE.

*Part B — extract λ.* Set ∂V/∂t = 0 and the injected current to zero. Express *r_m* and *r_a* in terms of the specific membrane resistivity *R_m* (Ω·cm²), specific axial resistivity *R_a* (Ω·cm), and diameter *d*, and show that the steady-state equation reduces to ∂²V/∂x² = V/λ² with λ = √(d·R_m / (4·R_a)).

*Part C — compute.* Using R_m = 20,000 Ω·cm², R_a = 200 Ω·cm, d = 2 µm = 2×10⁻⁴ cm, compute λ in cm and convert to µm.

*Part D — attenuation.* Using the steady-state solution for a synapse on an (idealized, semi-infinite) cable, V(soma) = V(local) · e^(−x/λ), compute the fraction of a synapse's local steady-state voltage that reaches the soma when the synapse sits at x = 2λ.

*Part E — label the terms.* Rewrite the PDE from Part A and label which term is axial (longitudinal) current flow and which is transmembrane leak current.

**Deliverable**

Written pages (handwritten or typed) covering: the Kirchhoff-law setup and the Δx → 0 limit (Part A, shown explicitly, not skipped); the λ formula in terms of R_m, R_a, d (Part B); the numeric λ with units (Part C); the attenuation fraction as both an expression and a decimal (Part D); the labeled cable equation (Part E).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All five parts correct; the Δx → 0 step is shown explicitly; λ correct to ≥3 significant figures with units carried through; attenuation given as both e^(−2) and its decimal value; axial/leak terms labeled specifically (not "term 1"/"term 2") with one line each on why. |
| Adequate | Cable equation and λ formula correct; final numeric λ and attenuation fraction correct even if intermediate unit bookkeeping is sloppy; terms labeled but justification thin. |
| Not yet | A term missing or wrong sign in the PDE; λ formula missing the factor of 4, or diameter dependence inverted; numeric λ off by more than 2× or missing units; attenuation computed as a linear (not exponential) falloff, or with the wrong sign in the exponent. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- Cable equation: c_m ∂V/∂t = (1/r_a) ∂²V/∂x² − V/r_m + i_e(x,t). Derivation sketch: axial current at x is I(x) = −(1/r_a)∂V/∂x; net axial current entering a segment of length Δx is I(x) − I(x+Δx) ≈ (1/r_a)(∂²V/∂x²)Δx (first-order Taylor expansion of −∂I/∂x). This equals the capacitive current c_m Δx·∂V/∂t plus the leak current (V/r_m)Δx, minus injected current i_e Δx. Divide by Δx, let Δx → 0.
- λ = √(r_m/r_a); with r_m = R_m/(πd) and r_a = 4R_a/(πd²): λ = √[(R_m/(πd)) / (4R_a/(πd²))] = √(R_m·d / (4R_a)).
- Numeric: λ = √(2×10⁻⁴ cm × 20,000 Ω·cm² / (4 × 200 Ω·cm)) = √(4 / 800 cm²) = √0.005 cm² = 0.070711 cm = **707.1 µm** (≈0.71 mm — inside the "hundreds of µm to ~1 mm" range the lesson scope names).
- Attenuation at x = 2λ: fraction = e^(−2) = **0.1353 (13.5%)** of the local synaptic voltage reaches the soma at steady state; the remaining 86.5% is lost to membrane leak along the way.
- Term labels: (1/r_a)∂²V/∂x² is the net axial current flowing along the cable's core (it appears as a second derivative because it's the *difference* of two first-derivative axial-current terms at the two ends of the segment); −V/r_m is the transmembrane leak current flowing out across the membrane resistance; c_m∂V/∂t is the capacitive charging current; i_e is externally injected/synaptic current per unit length.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: Ask them to write Kirchhoff's current law at a single node first — what currents meet there? — before worrying about the Δx → 0 limit.
- Hint 2: If they're stuck on the limit step, ask them to write the two axial currents at x and x+Δx explicitly via Ohm's law, subtract, and Taylor-expand — what's the leading term in Δx?
- Hint 3: If λ comes out with the wrong diameter dependence, ask them to re-derive r_m and r_a separately from R_m, R_a, and d using the cylinder's surface area (πdΔx) and cross-sectional area (πd²/4) — where does the factor of 4 come from?
- Never: never supply the finished PDE, the λ formula, or the numeric λ value outright. Walk the learner to each via the current-conservation argument and the resistivity/geometry relations, never by stating the result first.

## Piece 2 — Multi-compartment cable: attenuation curve and the discretisation rule

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** ~90 min

**Learning goals**
- Translate the continuous cable equation into a discretized system of coupled compartment ODEs with sealed (no-flux) boundaries.
- Numerically reproduce the analytic exponential attenuation law from Piece 1 when compartments are small relative to λ.
- Demonstrate, by direct numerical comparison (not assertion), that violating Δx ≪ λ produces a measurably wrong attenuation curve.

**Task**

Work in normalized units: x̃ = x/λ, t̃ = t/τ_m (τ_m = R_m·C_m; assume the standard specific membrane capacitance C_m = 1 µF/cm², giving τ_m = 20,000 Ω·cm² × 1×10⁻⁶ F/cm² = 0.02 s = 20 ms). In these units the cable equation becomes

∂V/∂t̃ = ∂²V/∂x̃² − V + E(x̃, t̃)

with E the injected current in normalized units. **Model the soma as an interior compartment, not an end compartment**: extend the cable at least 6λ on either side of the soma with sealed (Neumann) boundaries at the two far ends. (If the soma sits at a sealed boundary instead, its own reflection corrupts the comparison to the semi-infinite exp(−x/λ) law — this is a deliberate design constraint, not a simplification you can skip.)

Discretize into N compartments of width h = Δx̃. For each interior compartment i:

dV_i/dt̃ = (V_{i+1} − 2V_i + V_{i-1})/h² − V_i + E_i(t̃)

with the sealed-end rule at i=0 and i=N−1 (drop the term that would come from outside the cable, i.e. reflect the missing neighbor).

*Part A (well-resolved).* Choose h = 0.1 (Δx = 0.1λ). Inject a constant unit current, one run at a time, at compartments 1λ, 2λ, and 3λ from the soma. Integrate forward in time with a stable step until the somatic voltage plateaus. Record the ratio V(soma)/V(local injection site) at each distance and compare against e^(−x/λ) from Piece 1.

*Part B (violating the rule).* Repeat with h = 1.0 (one compartment spans a full length constant) and again with h = 2.0, same physical span. Record the same ratios (where a compartment lands at that distance) and compute percent deviation from e^(−x/λ) at a fixed distance (e.g. x = 2λ) across all three values of h.

**Deliverable**

The script (under ~100 lines), the attenuation curves (fine vs. coarse vs. analytic, plotted or tabulated), and a short error-vs-h/λ table at a fixed distance.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Fine-resolution (h ≪ λ) curve matches analytic e^(−x/λ) to within ~1%; coarse cases show clearly increasing error as h/λ grows, reported as computed percentages; soma correctly placed as an interior compartment with the reason stated; explicit error-vs-h table included. |
| Adequate | Fine case matches within a few percent and at least one coarse case is shown to deviate, but error isn't quantified precisely or only one coarse case is tested. |
| Not yet | Soma placed at a cable endpoint (breaking the exp(−x/λ) comparison even in the fine case); no quantified comparison to the analytic curve; only one resolution tested, so criterion 4 isn't actually demonstrated; unstable integration (blown-up/NaN voltages) not diagnosed. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Reference results, λ = 707.11 µm (Piece 1), physical span ≈ ±6λ, explicit Euler with dt = min(0.2, 0.2·h²), integrated to t̃ = 20 (well past steady state):

Fine, h = 0.1λ (N = 120, soma at center index 60):
- x=1λ: ratio = 0.3680 (analytic 0.3679, err +0.03%)
- x=2λ: ratio = 0.1354 (analytic 0.1353, err +0.1%)
- x=3λ: ratio = 0.0498 (analytic 0.0498, err ~0%)

Coarse, h = 1.0λ (N = 12, soma at center index 6):
- x=1λ: ratio = 0.3820 (analytic 0.3679, err +3.8%)
- x=2λ: ratio = 0.1459 (analytic 0.1353, err +7.8%)
- x=3λ: ratio = 0.0557 (analytic 0.0498, err +11.9%)

Extra-coarse, h = 2.0λ (N = 6, soma at center index 3):
- x=2λ: ratio = 0.1716 (analytic 0.1353, err +26.8%)
- x=4λ: ratio = 0.0294 (analytic 0.0183, err +60.7%)

Expected pattern: error grows both with distance and with h/λ — the same physical cylinder (same R_m, R_a, d) gives an increasingly wrong attenuation curve purely from coarser spatial discretisation. This is criterion 4 demonstrated, not asserted.

Reference implementation (teacher verification only — do not hand to the learner):
```python
import numpy as np
def run_cable(N, h, inject_idx, t_end=20.0):
    dt = min(0.2, 0.2*h**2)
    V = np.zeros(N); E = np.zeros(N); E[inject_idx] = 1.0
    for _ in range(int(t_end/dt)):
        lap = np.zeros(N)
        lap[1:-1] = V[2:] - 2*V[1:-1] + V[:-2]
        lap[0] = V[1] - V[0]
        lap[-1] = V[-2] - V[-1]
        V = V + dt*(lap/h**2 - V + E)
    return V
```

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: If the somatic voltage never plateaus or blows up, ask what stability condition explicit Euler needs relative to h — without giving the formula.
- Hint 2: If the fine-resolution curve doesn't match the analytic one even qualitatively, ask where they placed the soma in the compartment array — an end, or the interior?
- Hint 3: If they can't see the coarse case diverge, ask them to compute percent error explicitly at one fixed distance across at least two values of h, rather than eyeballing a plot.
- Never: never hand over the reference implementation or the numeric answers above. Never debug their code line by line — ask what a specific line is supposed to do and let them check it themselves.

## Piece 3 — Guided reading: D&A ch. 6

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** ~45 min

**Learning goals**
- Locate where each level of neuron modeling sits on the abstraction spectrum, and what it keeps versus discards.
- Identify what conductance-based models add beyond the Hodgkin-Huxley Na⁺/K⁺ pair.
- State the cable equation's terms and boundary conditions as the text presents them, cross-checked against Piece 1.
- Extract the text's rule of thumb for multi-compartment discretisation.

**Task**

Read D&A ch. 6, sections *Levels of Neuron Modeling*, *Conductance-Based Models*, *The Cable Equation*, and *Multi-compartment Models*. Answer in your own words (no verbatim quoting), naming the subsection each answer draws from:

2. The conductance-based section goes beyond the Hodgkin-Huxley Na⁺/K⁺ pair. Name at least one additional ionic current it discusses and its functional role.
3. In the text's derivation of the cable equation, what physical assumption lets axial current be written via a simple 1-D Ohm's law (what is assumed about intracellular vs. extracellular resistance)?
4. What boundary condition does the text use at a sealed dendritic end, and what condition applies at a branch point where two dendrites meet?
5. What rule of thumb does the text give for compartment length relative to the electrotonic length constant, and what goes wrong if it's violated?

**Deliverable**

Answers to Q1-Q5, at most one paragraph each, each tagged with the subsection it was drawn from.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All five answered accurately with the correct subsection cited; levels of modeling distinguished by what state each one tracks; sealed-end and branch-point conditions stated distinctly and correctly. |
| Adequate | Correct in substance, citations vague, or one answer thin. |
| Not yet | Conflates levels of modeling; misses that conductance-based models include currents beyond Na⁺/K⁺; can't state the compartment-length rule, or states it relative to neuron size instead of λ. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — verify each answer against the physical text before grading; never invent a page number or quotation)

- Q1 expected content: point/integrate-and-fire style models — capture spike-timing statistics via a threshold, discard spatial structure entirely; conductance-based point (Hodgkin-Huxley) models — capture spike-generation biophysics via ionic currents, discard spatial extent; multi-compartment passive/active models — capture dendritic filtering and location-dependent attenuation, discard fine 3-D branching detail unless morphologically reconstructed; full morphological reconstructions — capture actual traced dendritic geometry, computationally heavy, still discard molecular/channel-noise detail. Confirm the text's own list and terms before grading rigidly — this may not be an exact match to the text's phrasing.
- Q2 expected: a calcium current (Ca²⁺ — contributes to calcium spikes, triggers synaptic vesicle release, and/or activates calcium-dependent K⁺ channels), a calcium-activated or otherwise slow potassium current (contributes to spike-frequency adaptation/afterhyperpolarization), and/or a hyperpolarization-activated current I_h (contributes to pacemaking/resonance). One correctly described current with its role is sufficient — verify against the text's actual list.
- Q3 expected: the extracellular medium is treated as a good conductor (effectively isopotential / negligible resistance), so essentially all axial resistance to current flow is attributed to the intracellular cytoplasm, letting axial current be written as a simple 1-D Ohm's-law term along x.
- Q4 expected: sealed end — zero axial current flux (∂V/∂x = 0 at the tip; no current flows past a capped end); branch point — voltage continuity across the branch plus Kirchhoff's current law (current entering the parent branch equals the sum of currents into the daughter branches), which for impedance-matched cable branching ties daughter diameters to the parent via the 3/2-power rule.
- Q5 expected: compartment length should be small relative to λ (commonly a small fraction such as ~0.1λ or less), never chosen from the neuron's physical size. Violating it means the discretized second-difference approximation to ∂²V/∂x² no longer represents the continuous cable — compartments behave as weakly-coupled, near-isolated RC segments rather than a continuum, and the computed voltage profile deviates from the true exponential attenuation, worsening as compartment length approaches or exceeds λ (matches the Piece 2 simulation's own error trend).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: Point to the relevant subsection heading if the learner can't find where a concept is discussed — never state the content of that subsection.
- Hint 2: If an answer conflates two levels of modeling, ask what state variable each level tracks (individual channel gating variables? spike/no-spike threshold? traced 3-D geometry?).
- Hint 3: If they can't state the compartment-length rule, ask them to compare it to their own λ from Piece 1 — what did they compute, and how does that compare to "a typical compartment"?
- Never: never provide answer text or infer answers from general knowledge in place of the physical text. Verify against the book before confirming or rejecting an answer; if the exact wording is uncertain, tell the learner to re-check the cited subsection rather than asserting a page number or quotation.

## Piece 4 — Written explanation: location as an implicit weight

**Type:** written explanation · **Time:** ~45 min, 300-600 words

**Learning goals**
- Synthesize the derivation and simulation results into a coherent account of why dendritic location acts as an implicit synaptic weight.
- Explain, in the learner's own words and with their own numbers, why compartment size must track λ rather than physical neuron size.
- Situate the passive-cable result within the levels-of-modeling spectrum and conductance-based extensions.

**Task**

In 300-600 words, explain to a reader who knows Hodgkin-Huxley point neurons but has not seen cable theory:

1. Why a synapse's distance from the soma, measured in units of λ, determines how much of its current survives to influence somatic voltage — use your computed λ and attenuation fraction from Piece 1 as a concrete example.
2. Why, when simulating this numerically, compartment length must be chosen relative to λ and not the neuron's physical size — reference your Piece 2 fine-vs-coarse comparison and at least one of its error numbers.
3. How this passive-cable picture fits into the broader levels-of-modeling spectrum (Piece 3, Q1), and what a conductance-based extension (e.g. adding a calcium current, Piece 3 Q2) would let the model do that a passive cable structurally cannot.

**Deliverable**

300-600 words of prose covering all three points, citing specific numbers from Pieces 1 and 2 (not just symbols).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Explains attenuation as exponential decay in units of λ with a concrete number (e.g. "at 2λ, only ~13.5% of the local voltage survives to the soma"); explains the discretisation failure mechanistically (why the finite-difference second derivative needs small Δx/λ) with a concrete error number from their own simulation; correctly places the passive cable in the levels spectrum and names a specific, correct consequence of adding an active current; word count in range. |
| Adequate | Covers all three points with generally correct reasoning but vaguer mechanism (e.g. asserts the discretisation rule without explaining why) or omits concrete numbers. |
| Not yet | Treats synaptic location as a fixed/additive weight without connecting it to distance and exponential decay; says compartment size should just be "small" with no reference to λ; conflates active/passive cable properties or misstates the calcium current's role; well outside word count. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Expected core claims: (a) V(soma)/V(local) = e^(−x/λ); for the derivation's cylinder (R_m=20,000 Ω·cm², R_a=200 Ω·cm, d=2 µm), λ≈707 µm, so a synapse at 2λ≈1.41 mm delivers only e^(−2)≈13.5% of its local voltage to the soma at steady state — the rest is lost to leak along the way, making distance functionally a multiplicative weight on synaptic efficacy, independent of anything the synapse itself does. (b) The compartmental model approximates ∂²V/∂x² by (V_{i+1}−2V_i+V_{i-1})/Δx², accurate only when V changes little across one compartment, i.e. Δx ≪ λ. In the learner's own Piece 2 simulation, Δx=0.1λ reproduces exp(−x/λ) to within ~0.1%, while Δx=1.0λ (10× too coarse relative to the ~0.1λ rule of thumb) produces errors of roughly 4-12% growing with distance, and Δx=2.0λ produces errors of 27-60%+ — this ties compartment size to the tissue's electrotonic length scale, not to the neuron's gross physical dimensions: a much larger neuron with the same R_m/R_a/d still needs Δx ≪ 707 µm regardless of total dendrite length. (c) Passive cable theory sits above the point-neuron level (it adds spatial/dendritic filtering) but below full conductance-based/active-dendrite models; adding an active current such as I_Ca would let a dendritic segment actively boost rather than passively attenuate distal input (e.g. local calcium spikes, supralinear summation) — explicitly out of scope for this lesson's passive treatment.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: If the attenuation explanation is vague, ask them to restate it using their own Part D number from Piece 1.
- Hint 2: If the discretisation explanation lacks mechanism, ask what the finite-difference second-derivative formula assumes about how V behaves within one compartment.
- Hint 3: If the third point is generic ("more realistic"), ask them to name one specific thing an active current lets a dendrite do that a passive cable structurally cannot.
- Never: never write or rewrite sentences of the explanation for them, and never supply word-count-fitting phrasing. Point at what's missing — a claim, a number, a mechanism — and let the learner write it.
