# B4 Coursework — Developmental pruning: how brains choose their own size

**Lesson spec:** `lessons.md` → B4. **Total time:** ~2.5–3 hours (one sitting). **Prerequisites:** B2; homeostasis (synaptic scaling, threshold adaptation).

## Running order

Consume, then apply, then refine:

**audio overview → guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written defence (Piece 4)**

Pieces are numbered by type, not by sequence. The reading front-loads the concepts, the derivation makes them exact, the simulation is where they stick and get refined, and the defence is where you find out whether they did.

## Piece 1 — Overproduction/pruning rate arithmetic

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** ~40 min

**Learning goals**
- Convert developmental synapse-count data into per-unit-time growth and pruning rates.
- Show quantitatively that the overproduction phase is much faster than the elimination phase.
- Translate a biological rate ratio into a concrete per-epoch schedule for a stated network size.

**Task**

Given (background, stated directly — not from an assigned reading; these are standard, widely-replicated developmental synaptic-density figures for human cortex, order-of-magnitude accurate):
- At birth (age 0): ~2,500 synapses per cortical neuron.
- At peak density (age ≈ 2.5 years): ~15,000 synapses per neuron.
- At adult, stable density (age ≈ 18 years): ~7,500 synapses per neuron — i.e. ~50% of the peak count has been eliminated by adulthood.

Compute, showing units at every step:
1. The growth rate during the overproduction phase (birth → peak), in synapses/neuron/year.
2. The pruning rate during the elimination phase (peak → adult), in synapses/neuron/year.
3. The ratio of (1) to (2).
4. Using that ratio: for a Step 4 network with a target steady-state size of Nss = 100 neuron pairs, choose a seed size N0 and peak size Npeak that mirror the birth:peak:adult ratios above (2,500 : 15,000 : 7,500), and split a total training budget of 200 epochs into a growth-phase epoch count and a pruning-phase epoch count in proportion to the rate ratio from (3).

**Deliverable**

A short worked sheet: the four numbered computations above, each showing the arithmetic (not just the answer), plus one sentence stating the final policy as `N0 = _, Npeak = _, Nss = _, epochs_growth = _, epochs_prune = _`.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All four values correct with units shown; the epoch split in (4) is derived from the rate ratio in (3), not guessed; states in one sentence why the two phases must differ in duration (fast bulk growth vs. slow selective removal). |
| Adequate | Rates and ratio in (1)–(3) correct; (4) uses a reasonable but not clearly rate-derived epoch split. |
| Not yet | Arithmetic errors in (1)–(3), or (4) ignores the ratio entirely (e.g., 50/50 split). |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

1. Growth rate = (15,000 − 2,500) / 2.5 yr = 12,500 / 2.5 = **5,000 synapses/neuron/year**.
2. Pruning rate = (15,000 − 7,500) / (18 − 2.5) yr = 7,500 / 15.5 ≈ **484 synapses/neuron/year**.
3. Ratio = 5,000 / 484 ≈ **10.3 : 1** (growth phase is ~10× faster than pruning phase).
4. Mirroring 2,500 : 15,000 : 7,500 (≈ 0.33 : 2 : 1 relative to adult) onto Nss = 100: N0 ≈ 33, Npeak = 200, Nss = 100. Epoch split from the 10.3:1 ratio over 200 epochs: epochs_growth = 200/(1+10.3) ≈ 17.5 → **18 epochs**; epochs_prune = 200 − 18 = **182 epochs**. Accept anything in the range 15–20 growth epochs given rounding; reject splits that don't derive from the 10.3:1 ratio (e.g., unjustified 50/50 or 100/100).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Check your time windows — the growth phase and pruning phase don't span the same number of years. What are the two intervals?"
- Hint 2: "You have a rate for each phase now. A ratio of rates should become a ratio of epoch counts, not of network sizes."
- Hint 3: "Ratio ≈ 10:1. Out of 200 epochs, what split has that ratio?"
- Never: give the numeric answers 5,000 / 484 / 10.3 / 18 / 182 before you has attempted the arithmetic at least once. Never accept an epoch split that isn't shown to derive from the rate ratio.

## Piece 2 — Overproduce-then-prune vs. grow-on-demand

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** ~55 min

**Learning goals**
- Implement two competing capacity-control policies on identical synthetic data and a shared training budget.
- Measure the size/performance tradeoff empirically rather than assuming one policy dominates.
- Connect the empirical result back to Step 4's current design (stress-triggered growth only, no scheduled overproduction phase).

**Task**

Write a self-contained PyTorch or numpy script (<~100 lines) with a single-hidden-layer MLP regressing a fixed synthetic target, e.g. `y = sin(3x1) + 0.5*x2^2 - x1*x2 + noise` over `x in R^2`, 500 train / 200 test points, generated with a fixed seed.

Implement two training runs on the *same* data and the *same* total epoch budget (e.g. 200 epochs):

- **Policy A — overproduce-then-prune:** start with 200 hidden units. Train normally. Every 10 epochs after epoch 20, rank hidden units by `|outgoing weight| · |incoming weight|` (a cheap salience proxy) and permanently zero out (mask, don't un-allocate) the bottom 5% still active, down to a floor of 10 units. Record final active-unit count and test MSE.
- **Policy B — grow-on-demand:** start with 10 hidden units. Track an EMA of training loss (τ ≈ 20 epochs) as a stress proxy. Whenever stress has not decreased for 15 consecutive epochs, add 10 new hidden units (initialized small/random), up to a cap of 200. Record final active-unit count and test MSE.

Print a comparison table: `{policy, final_unit_count, test_MSE, epochs_to_reach_final_size}`.

**Deliverable**

The script, its printed comparison table, and 3–5 sentences interpreting the result: which policy reached a smaller final network, which reached lower test error, and whether either policy ever *removed* a unit it had grown (Policy B should not — this is the flaw the lesson is pointing at).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Both policies implemented correctly and share data/budget/seed; pruning in A is irreversible and floor-respecting; growth in B is genuinely stress-gated (not just scheduled); interpretation correctly identifies that B has no removal mechanism and so cannot recover from over-growth, while A can end up smaller than B. |
| Adequate | Both policies run and produce a table; one part (e.g. stress-gating, or the floor/cap) is stubbed or approximate but the comparison is still meaningful. |
| Not yet | Only one policy implemented, or "stress" in B is not actually derived from the loss trend (e.g. it's just a fixed schedule), collapsing B into a relabeled version of A. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Expected qualitative pattern, for sanity-checking a learner's run (exact numbers will vary with seed/architecture):
- Policy A should end at or near its floor (10–30 units) with test MSE within roughly 1.5–3× the MSE of an unpruned 200-unit baseline trained for the same budget — pruning costs some accuracy but recovers most of it because 200 units was overkill for a 2-D synthetic target.
- Policy B should end at a unit count that depends on how quickly the stress signal declines; because it only ever adds units, its final count is a non-decreasing function of training difficulty and cannot be pushed back down even if early units turn out redundant. Watch for learners reporting B's final size as *smaller* than A's *and* claiming this favors B — that is a red flag: it usually means B never triggered growth (stress threshold too loose) rather than that grow-on-demand is genuinely more efficient.
- Diagnostic to check correctness: temporarily force Policy B's stress threshold to trigger every epoch — final size should hit the 200 cap. If it doesn't, the growth-gating logic is broken.
- Grader should reject any submission where Policy A's "pruning" only masks gradients but the unit still receives forward signal (that's decay, not removal, and violates the lesson's "active removal, not passive decay" point).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Both policies need to see the exact same train/test split and epoch budget, or the comparison is meaningless — check your seeding."
- Hint 2: "For Policy B, print the stress EMA over time. Does it actually gate the growth events, or did you accidentally put growth on a fixed schedule?"
- Hint 3: "Once a unit is pruned in Policy A, does anything in your loop let it come back? It shouldn't."
- Never: write or debug you's script. Never supply the synthetic-function formula as a fix if theirs already satisfies "nontrivial, 2-D, fixed seed" — a different formula is fine.

## Piece 3 — D&A ch. 8 guided reading

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** ~30 min

**Learning goals**
- Explain why unconstrained Hebbian growth is unstable and what normalization schemes fix it.
- State the mechanism by which activity correlation, not weight magnitude alone, determines which synapse a competitive process keeps.
- Connect D&A's ocular-dominance model to the general principle "correlated-with-partner activity protects a synapse."

**Task** — read D&A ch. 8, *Unsupervised Learning*, and answer:

1. (§8.2–8.3, basic Hebb rule) Why does the plain Hebb rule `Δw = η u v` have no stable fixed point for `|w|` on its own — what happens to synaptic weight over repeated presentations, and why does this force some form of normalization?
2. (§8.3, normalization schemes) D&A presents subtractive and multiplicative (Oja's rule) normalization. State the practical difference in what kind of solution each tends toward — does one tend toward a winner-take-all outcome (most weights driven to zero, one or few dominant) and the other toward a softer, shared outcome? Which behavior looks more like "a synapse either survives or gets removed" versus "all synapses shrink a little"?
3. (§8.3, Oja's rule) Write Oja's rule and state which eigenvector of the input covariance matrix the weight vector converges to. Why does this mean a synapse driven by an input direction with *low* variance/correlation is disadvantaged relative to one driven by a high-variance, well-correlated input, independent of their starting weights?
4. (§8.4, ocular dominance) In D&A's treatment, inputs from the same eye are more correlated with each other than inputs from opposite eyes. State how this correlation asymmetry, combined with the normalization/competition rule from Q2–3, produces eye-specific (segregated) territories rather than a blended input from both eyes. This is the direct analogue of "correlated activity protects a synapse" — identify exactly where in the model that protection enters (is it in the learning rule, the normalization step, or the initial conditions?).
5. (§8.3, BCM) The BCM rule uses a sliding modification threshold rather than a fixed one. What problem with a fixed threshold does the sliding threshold solve, and why might a Step 4 pruning rule want an analogous *sliding* prune threshold (relative to a neuron's own recent activity) instead of one fixed weight cutoff for every neuron?

**Deliverable**

Five short written answers (2–5 sentences each), each ending with one explicit sentence connecting it to Step 4's stress/pruning design.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All five answers technically correct (instability, normalization contrast, Oja's eigenvector result, correlation-driven segregation, sliding-threshold rationale) and each carries a specific, non-generic Step 4 connection. |
| Adequate | Four of five technically correct; Step 4 connections present but somewhat generic ("this relates to pruning"). |
| Not yet | Confuses subtractive/multiplicative normalization outcomes, or answers Q4 without identifying where correlation enters the mechanism, or Step 4 connections are missing. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

1. Under plain Hebb, `Δw ∝ u v` and `v ∝ w·u`, so `Δw` grows with `w` itself — weights that start larger grow faster, with no restoring force; `|w|` diverges (or all weights collapse to zero if correlations are negative), so some norm-constraining term is required to get a nontrivial fixed point.
2. Subtractive normalization enforces the constraint by removing a fixed amount split evenly, which tends to be *unstable at the boundary* and pushes solutions toward saturation at bounds — extreme, near winner-take-all outcomes (most weights at min, a few at max). Multiplicative normalization (Oja) rescales proportionally to current weight, giving a smoother, stable convergence to the principal eigenvector without hard saturation for every unit — a softer, shared outcome. The winner-take-all-like subtractive case is the closer analogue of "survives or is removed."
3. Oja's rule: `Δw = η(vu − v²w)`. The weight vector converges to the eigenvector of the input covariance matrix with the *largest* eigenvalue (the principal component). A synapse aligned with a low-variance/poorly-correlated input direction is disadvantaged because that direction never becomes the dominant eigenvector regardless of the synapse's initial weight — the outcome is set by input statistics, not starting condition.
4. Within-eye inputs are strongly correlated; between-eye inputs are weakly correlated. Under Hebbian learning with normalization/competition (Q2–3), a postsynaptic cell's weights get pulled toward whichever correlated input group dominates locally, and the competitive normalization suppresses the weaker-correlated (other-eye) inputs to that same cell — producing spatially segregated ocular-dominance patches. The "protection" enters at the *learning-rule-plus-normalization* step: correlation raises the effective covariance eigenvalue for that input group, which the normalization then rewards at the expense of others — it is not built into initial conditions (those are typically near-symmetric/random).
5. A fixed threshold means the same rule pushes all synapses toward LTP or all toward LTD depending on global activity level, which is unstable (positive feedback: high activity → more LTP → even higher activity). The sliding BCM threshold rises with recent postsynaptic activity, so a cell that has been very active needs even more coincident input to strengthen a synapse further — a self-stabilizing, per-neuron reference level. Step 4 analogue: a single fixed weight-magnitude cutoff for pruning penalizes neurons with naturally low overall activity/weight scale; a per-neuron sliding threshold (e.g., relative to that neuron's own recent weight distribution or firing rate) avoids systematically pruning quiet-but-legitimate neurons.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "For Q1, write out what `Δw` depends on when you substitute `v = w·u` — does the growth term contain `w`?"
- Hint 2: "For Q4, ask specifically: if two synapses have the same weight right now, what property of their recent history makes one keep growing and the other shrink?"
- Hint 3: "For Q5, what does 'sliding' mean relative to what other quantity — a global constant, or something specific to that one neuron?"
- Never: summarize the whole ch. 8 argument for you in place of them reading it. Never provide the Step 4 connection sentence yourself — you must produce that link.

## Piece 4 — Step 4 policy defence

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** ~35 min

**Learning goals**
- Turn Piece 1's rate arithmetic and Piece 3's mechanism into a committed, numbered policy for Step 4.
- State a two-stage tag-then-remove pruning rule instead of a single instantaneous threshold.
- Separate which policy numbers claim biological grounding from which are unjustified engineering choices.

**Task**

Background, stated directly (not an assigned reading — Schafer et al. 2012 is not available in this course's sources, but this mechanism is required by the acceptance criteria, so it is given here):
- Synapse elimination in developing cortex is carried out by two distinct players: **complement proteins (C1q, then C3)** deposit a molecular tag on weak/inactive synapses; **microglia** recognize that tag and phagocytose (physically engulf and remove) the tagged synapse. Tagging and removal are separate steps with a delay between them — a synapse is not removed the instant it is tagged.
- The ocular-dominance critical period closes as inhibitory (GABAergic, particularly parvalbumin-interneuron) circuitry matures and structural brakes (e.g., perineuronal nets) form around cortical neurons. The same manipulation that shifts ocular dominance in an infant (monocular deprivation) does not reopen or shift dominance in an adult, because the circuit is no longer in the low-inhibition, structurally unconstrained state that permitted large-scale rewiring — the brake, not the input, is what changed.
- Robust adult neurogenesis is restricted to a small number of sites (the hippocampal dentate gyrus is the standard example; olfactory-bulb neurogenesis via the subventricular zone is well established in rodents, much more limited in adult humans). Outside these sites, the adult cortex does not generate new neurons.

Write a 300–600 word Step 4 policy document with exactly these four numbered decisions, each with a specific number and one sentence of justification:

1. **Initial over-provisioning factor** — how large should the network be seeded/allowed to grow relative to its intended steady-state size, and over what fraction of total training should this growth be front-loaded?
2. **Growth-trigger rule** — under what condition (and during what window of training) does the network create new neuron pairs? State whether growth is stress-gated throughout training or restricted to the front-loaded window from (1), and say why.
3. **Two-stage tag-then-remove pruning rule** — the exact condition that tags a connection, the tag duration before removal is possible, and the condition (if any) that can cancel a tag before removal.
4. **Biological-grounding ledger** — for each of the three numbers above, one line stating whether it is directly justified by a biological fact from this lesson (name the fact) or is an engineering choice with no biological backing (say so plainly).

**Deliverable**

The 300–600 word document, numbered 1–4 as above.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All four numbered decisions present with specific numbers (not "some" / "a while"); the two-stage tag/remove rule has a distinct tag condition, tag duration, and remove condition (three separate numbers, not one threshold reused); ledger in (4) correctly separates biologically-grounded claims (front-loaded overproduction ratio, tag-then-remove as two stages, growth-window restriction echoing limited adult neurogenesis) from unjustified engineering constants (exact epoch counts, exact ε/threshold values, exact tag/grace durations); word count in range. |
| Adequate | All four decisions present with numbers, but the ledger conflates one biologically-grounded item with an engineering one, or the tag-then-remove rule collapses tag and remove into a single threshold. |
| Not yet | Missing a numbered decision, no concrete numbers given, or growth described as continuous/unrestricted stress-gating throughout training with no front-loaded window (contradicts the overproduction argument this lesson exists to make). |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Reference policy (a defensible example; learners may choose different specific numbers, but must show the same structure and reasoning):

1. Over-provisioning factor: seed the network at N0 ≈ 0.3× the intended steady-state size Nss, and permit growth up to Npeak = 2× Nss, entirely within the first ~10% of total training epochs (mirrors Piece 1's 2,500:15,000:7,500 ≈ 0.33:2:1 ratio and its 18/200-epoch front-loaded window).
2. Growth-trigger rule: new neuron pairs are created only during that front-loaded window, gated by local stress exceeding a threshold sustained for several epochs — not by stress alone at any point in training. After the window closes, no new pairs are created for the rest of training, regardless of stress, mirroring the fact that robust neurogenesis outside hippocampus/olfactory bulb does not occur in adult cortex — ongoing stress should be resolved by weight adjustment or by recruiting existing under-used capacity, not by open-ended growth.
3. Two-stage tag-then-remove rule: a connection is **tagged** when `|w| < ε` continuously for `T_tag` epochs (e.g., ε = 0.01, T_tag = 20); a tagged connection is **removed** if it remains tagged for a further `T_grace` epochs (e.g., T_grace = 10) without `|w|` rising back above ε at any point in that grace window, in which case the tag is cancelled and the timer resets. This is the direct analogue of complement tagging (mark) followed by delayed microglial engulfment (remove), and explicitly rejects a single-instant "weight below threshold ⇒ deleted" rule.
4. Ledger: (a) the front-loaded overproduction ratio and its restriction to an early window — biologically grounded (peak:adult synapse density ratio ≈ 2:1, and elimination fraction ≈ 40–50% by adulthood, Piece 1); (b) growth being confined to that window rather than gated by stress throughout training — biologically grounded (adult neurogenesis is anatomically restricted, not a general-purpose response to local error); (c) the tag-then-remove *structure* (two stages with a delay) — biologically grounded (complement tag ≠ microglial removal, separated in time); (d) the *specific values* ε = 0.01, T_tag = 20, T_grace = 10, N0/Npeak/Nss ratios chosen as exact multiples, and the "10% of training" window boundary — engineering choices with no biological backing; they are picked to fit the SNN's training timescale, not measured from any cited fact, and should be tuned empirically.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "You have four numbered items required. Check you haven't merged (2) into (1) — growth-trigger rule needs to say when growth is *allowed to fire*, not just how big the network can get."
- Hint 2: "Tag-then-remove needs three separate numbers: a tag condition, a tag duration, and a removal/cancel condition. If you only have one threshold, it's not two-stage yet."
- Hint 3: "For the ledger, ask of each number: is this the same shape as a fact from Piece 1/background, or did I just pick a number that seemed reasonable? If it's the latter, say so — that's not a failure, that's the correct answer for that line."
- Never: write or supply you's four policy numbers as your own draft. Never accept a submission where every ledger line claims biological grounding — the point of (4) is that most exact values do not have it.
