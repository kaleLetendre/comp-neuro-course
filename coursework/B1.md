## Piece 1 — TD prediction error by hand

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 60 min

**Learning goals**
- Compute TD(0) prediction errors and value updates by hand across trials, not just write the equation
- See the value signal shift backward in time (reward → cue) as learning proceeds
- Derive, from the update rule itself, why omitting an expected reward produces a *negative* error rather than merely "no signal"
- Connect the arithmetic to Schultz's dopamine recordings via D&A ch. 9's account

**Task**

Two states per trial: cue state **C**, then reward state **R**, then trial ends (terminal value 0). TD(0) rule, with $\gamma=1$, $\alpha=0.5$:

$$\delta(C) = 0 + \gamma V(R) - V(C) \qquad \delta(R) = r_R + \gamma \cdot 0 - V(R)$$

$$V(s) \leftarrow V(s) + \alpha\,\delta(s)$$

$V(C) = V(R) = 0$ initially. Every trial, $r_R = 1$ (reward always delivered) — until trial 6, where $r_R = 0$ (reward omitted, but the cue still fires normally).

Compute, trial by trial for trials 1-6, in this order each trial: $\delta(C)$, then $\delta(R)$, then update both $V(C)$ and $V(R)$. Show your working for at least trials 1, 2, and 6 in full; a table is fine for 3-5.

1. What happens to $\delta(C)$ across trials 1-5? What happens to $\delta(R)$? State the pattern in one sentence and connect it to "the dopamine response migrates from reward to the earliest reliable predictor."
2. Compute $\delta(R)$ on trial 6 (omission) using the $V(R)$ value carried in from trial 5. Compare its sign and magnitude to $\delta(R)$ on trial 1.
3. In one or two sentences: why is the omission result harder to explain under "reward = fixed-brightness success chemical" than the cue and reward results are? (A fixed-brightness model can still shrug off "no glow" at omission — what can't it explain?)

**Deliverable**
A table with columns [Trial, V(C) before, V(R) before, δ(C), δ(R), V(C) after, V(R) after] for trials 1-6, plus written answers to sub-questions 1-3 (a paragraph total, not an essay).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All 6 rows arithmetically correct; correctly identifies δ(C) rising toward 1 and δ(R) falling toward 0 as the backward-shift signature; correctly computes δ(R) on trial 6 as strongly negative (not just "smaller than trial 1"); answer to Q3 names the specific gap (a magnitude-only signal has no mechanism to go *below* its resting/no-event level, so it cannot represent "worse than expected," only "how much"). |
| Adequate | Arithmetic correct for at least trials 1-3 and 6 (minor slips elsewhere tolerated); gets the backward-shift direction right but describes it vaguely; gets the omission sign right but the Q3 explanation just restates "it's negative" without saying what that breaks in the fixed-brightness model. |
| Not yet | Arithmetic errors in trial 1 or 2 (wrong sign or wrong update target); reports δ(R) on trial 6 as zero, small-positive, or "no response"; Q3 answer only repeats the lesson's framing back without a mechanism. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Exact trajectory ($\alpha=0.5$, $\gamma=1$, all fractions exact):

| Trial | V(C) before | V(R) before | δ(C) | δ(R) | V(C) after | V(R) after |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0.5 |
| 2 | 0 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.25 | 0.75 |
| 3 | 0.25 | 0.75 | 0.5 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.875 |
| 4 | 0.5 | 0.875 | 0.375 | 0.125 | 0.6875 | 0.9375 |
| 5 | 0.6875 | 0.9375 | 0.25 | 0.0625 | 0.8125 | 0.96875 |
| 6 (omit) | 0.8125 | 0.96875 | 0.15625 | **-0.96875** | 0.8125 (unaffected) | 0.484375 (if updated; probe trials are often left unupdated — either answer is acceptable if labelled) |

Q1: δ(C) climbs monotonically toward 1 (0 → 0.5 → 0.5 → 0.375 → 0.25 — note it rises then falls back down; the acceptable answer is "δ(C) becomes reliably positive/nonzero once the cue predicts reward, while δ(R) decays toward 0" — do not require monotonic increase, the exact numbers oscillate slightly before settling because both states are updated every trial from a moving target). δ(R) decays 1 → 0.5 → 0.25 → 0.125 → 0.0625, halving each trial (geometric decay with ratio $1-\alpha=0.5$) as reward becomes fully predicted.
Q2: δ(R) trial 6 = $0 - 0.96875 = -0.96875$, vs. $+1$ on trial 1 — opposite sign, comparable magnitude.
Q3: Accept any answer that identifies: a magnitude-only/one-directional signal has a floor (zero, or "no release") and cannot code a value *less than* that floor, so it cannot represent an outcome worse than the resting expectation — only TD's explicit subtraction of a learned prediction from the outcome produces a signed, negative error. Reject answers that just say "the model predicts a dip so it's wrong" without saying why a brightness-only signal structurally cannot dip below its floor.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "What is $V(R)$ right before trial 6, and what does the TD formula for δ(R) subtract it from?"
- Hint 2: "Try trial 1 and trial 6 side by side — same formula, different $r_R$. What's the only thing that changed?"
- Hint 3: "A chemical that only has a 'how much' dial, no 'above/below' dial — what outcome can it never represent?"
- Never: never supply the trial-6 δ(R) value directly; never say the word "negative" before the learner has attempted the computation; never do the arithmetic for a trial the learner hasn't shown working for.

## Piece 2 — Dopamine signatures from a TD agent

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 105 min · **This is the centrepiece of the lesson.**

**Learning goals**
- Implement TD(0) over a within-trial timeline (not just two lumped states) and watch the value function reshape itself with training
- Reproduce, from a rule the learner wrote and trained themselves, the three canonical dopamine signatures: burst at reward (naive), burst at cue / no response at reward (trained), dip at the omission time (trained, reward withheld)
- Build the intuition that "the response migrates backward in time" is not a story bolted onto TD — it falls out of the Bellman recursion for free

**Task**

Represent one trial as a fixed timeline of $T=20$ discrete steps, cue (CS) always at $t=0$, reward (US) always at $t=T_{US}=10$. Keep a tabular value function $V[0..T-1]$ (initialized to zero) plus one fixed, **never-updated** baseline value $V_{\text{ITI}} = 0$ representing the inter-trial interval (it doesn't predict anything, so nothing should ever push it away from 0 — this is what makes the response appear to originate at the cue rather than drift backward forever).

Per trial:
1. Build $r[t]$: zero everywhere except $r[T_{US}] = 1$ (or $0$ on an omission probe).
2. Compute the "response at cue" as the TD error for the *transition into* the trial: $\delta_{\text{cue}} = 0 + \gamma V[0] - V_{\text{ITI}}$ (this does not update $V_{\text{ITI}}$).
3. Step through $t = 0 \ldots T-1$ in order. At each $t$: $\delta_t = r[t] + \gamma V[t+1] - V[t]$ (using $V[T]:=0$), then update $V[t] \leftarrow V[t] + \alpha\,\delta_t$ immediately (online, not batched).
4. Record $\delta_{\text{cue}}$ and $\delta_t$ at $t=T_{US}$ for every trial you care to inspect.

Run $\geq 300$ training trials with $\gamma=0.98$, $\alpha=0.1$. Then run one **omission probe** trial (do not train on it, or train on a separate copy of $V$) with $r[T_{US}]=0$, and record $\delta_{\text{cue}}$ and $\delta_{T_{US}}$ on that probe.

Report, as printed numbers (a plot is a nice-to-have, not required): $(\delta_{\text{cue}}, \delta_{T_{US}})$ at trial 1, at roughly the halfway point, at the final trained trial, and on the omission probe.

**Deliverable**
A runnable script (numpy or plain PyTorch tensors, no autograd needed, under ~100 lines) plus the four $(\delta_{\text{cue}}, \delta_{T_{US}})$ number-pairs, plus 3-4 sentences stating which pair corresponds to which of the three textbook signatures (naive burst-at-reward; trained burst-at-cue/silent-at-reward; trained-but-omitted dip-at-reward-time) and why $V_{\text{ITI}}$ had to be held fixed for the cue response to show up at all.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Code runs and reproduces all three signatures within reasonable tolerance (trial 1: δ_cue ≈ 0, δ_reward ≈ 1; final trained: δ_cue clearly positive and δ_reward ≈ 0; omission probe: δ_reward clearly negative, δ_cue ≈ unchanged from final trained value); explanation correctly attributes the cue response's *origin point* to the transition from the fixed $V_{\text{ITI}}=0$, not to $V[0]$ updating itself; correctly notes the omission probe's cue response is unaffected because the agent can't know in advance a given trial will omit. |
| Adequate | Signatures qualitatively present (right signs, right rough trend) but code has a minor bug (e.g. $V[T]$ not treated as 0, or $V_{\text{ITI}}$ accidentally updated causing the cue response to fade); explanation gets 2 of 3 signatures right without the $V_{\text{ITI}}$ mechanism. |
| Not yet | Missing signature (e.g. no dip at omission, or cue and reward responses never separate); $V_{\text{ITI}}$ updated like any other state (this silently destroys the cue-response signature — the giveaway is that the "cue response" keeps growing across arbitrarily many trials instead of converging); no working code, or code that doesn't correspond to the description (batched update instead of online, wrong reward timestep, etc). |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only) — reference implementation and expected numbers

```python
import numpy as np

T, T_US = 20, 10
GAMMA, ALPHA = 0.98, 0.1
N_TRIALS = 400

def run_trial(V, omit=False):
    r = np.zeros(T)
    if not omit:
        r[T_US] = 1.0
    delta_cue = 0.0 + GAMMA * V[0] - 0.0     # V_ITI fixed at 0, never updated
    deltas = np.zeros(T)
    for t in range(T):
        v_next = V[t + 1] if t + 1 < T else 0.0
        d = r[t] + GAMMA * v_next - V[t]
        deltas[t] = d
        V[t] += ALPHA * d
    return delta_cue, deltas[T_US]

V = np.zeros(T)
checkpoints = {}
for trial in range(1, N_TRIALS + 1):
    dc, dr = run_trial(V)
    if trial in (1, N_TRIALS // 2, N_TRIALS):
        checkpoints[trial] = (dc, dr)

dc_omit, dr_omit = run_trial(V.copy(), omit=True)
```

Expected fixed point: $V[t] \to \gamma^{(T_{US}-t)}$ for $t \le T_{US}$ (analytically, $V[T_{US}]\to1$, $V[T_{US}-1]\to\gamma=0.98$, …, $V[0]\to\gamma^{10}\approx0.817$). Expected numbers:
- Trial 1: $\delta_{\text{cue}} \approx 0$, $\delta_{\text{reward}} = 1$ exactly (both $V$'s are still 0).
- Trial 400 (trained): $\delta_{\text{cue}} \approx \gamma \cdot 0.817 \approx 0.80$, $\delta_{\text{reward}} \approx 0$ (fully predicted).
- Omission probe: $\delta_{\text{cue}} \approx 0.80$ (unchanged — the animal doesn't know this trial omits), $\delta_{\text{reward}} \approx -1$ (predicted $\approx 1$, got 0).

Common bug to watch for: forgetting `V_ITI` is fixed and instead treating $t=-1$ as a normal trainable state — this makes $\delta_{\text{cue}}$ keep growing indefinitely across trials instead of converging near 0.80, since value now leaks backward past the trial boundary with no stopping point.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Print $V$ after trial 1, trial 20, trial 100. Where does the bump appear first, and which direction does it spread?"
- Hint 2: "If $V_{\text{ITI}}$ were allowed to update like every other state, what would stop it from also converging toward $\gamma^{11}$ or higher? What breaks if it does?"
- Hint 3: "On the omission probe, is $\delta_{\text{cue}}$ supposed to change at all? What would the agent have to know in advance for it to change?"
- Never: never hand over the reference implementation or the numeric fixed-point formula; never debug the learner's code line-by-line — point at which printed quantity looks wrong and let them isolate it; never accept "it kind of worked" without the three checkpoint number-pairs actually being reported.

## Piece 3 — Guided reading: D&A ch. 9, *Classical Conditioning and Reinforcement Learning*

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 45 min

**Learning goals**

**Task**
Read D&A ch. 9, the sections covering the Rescorla-Wagner rule, the temporal difference (TD) model of classical conditioning, and the section relating the TD model to dopamine neuron recordings. Answer:

Quote or closely paraphrase the specific claim (not your own derivation from Piece 1 — the point here is to check the book says the same thing your arithmetic produced).

**Deliverable**

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All four answers accurately reflect the chapter's content in the learner's own words (not copied verbatim); Q2 correctly identifies the bootstrapped/successive-prediction mechanism as the specific fix, not a vague "TD is better"; Q4's answer matches (in direction and reasoning) what the learner independently derived in Piece 1, and the learner explicitly notices and states that match. |
| Adequate | Three of four answers correct; Q4 correct but the learner doesn't connect it back to Piece 1 even when prompted. |
| Not yet | Answers describe three-factor/eligibility-trace framing instead of what the chapter actually says (i.e., the learner is still describing their prior mental model rather than reading the new material); Q3 or Q4 answered from general knowledge without locating it in the chapter. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)
Rescorla-Wagner: $\Delta w = \alpha(r - v)$ (or equivalent notation) applied once per trial; the error term is the discrepancy between the actual outcome and the trial's summed prediction. Limitation: it treats a trial as one lumped event and has no account of *when within a trial* things happen — it can't explain within-trial timing effects (e.g., prediction should update as soon as the cue is seen, before the outcome, and should be sensitive to the cue-outcome interval).
TD fix: replaces the single end-of-trial error with a moment-by-moment error $\delta(t) = r(t) + \gamma V(t+1) - V(t)$ that bootstraps off the *next* moment's own prediction rather than waiting for the final outcome — this is what lets the response migrate to the earliest predictor and lets partial/intermediate cues get valued.
Schultz correspondence: D&A present dopamine neuron firing rate as tracking $\delta(t)$ — elevated firing for positive $\delta$, suppressed (below baseline) firing for negative $\delta$, and note this is what makes the TD account attractive as a model of the dopamine signal, while flagging (accept any reasonable phrasing) that this is a correspondence/analogy the chapter draws, not a claim that dopamine neurons literally compute this in isolation from other signals.
Reward-omission claim: chapter states firing shifts from reward-locked to cue-locked as prediction improves, and that omission of an expected reward produces a *depression* of firing below baseline at the time reward was expected — the same sign flip the learner should have produced by hand in Piece 1.

**Teacher constraints**

- Hint 2: "Q3 wants a specific sentence about firing rate, not a summary of the whole dopamine section."
- Hint 3: "Compare your Q4 answer to your own Piece 1 trial-6 number before writing it down — do they agree?"

## Piece 4 — Written defence: does the stress variable need more than one channel?

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 60 min · **300-600 words**

**Learning goals**
- Articulate, without a textbook open, the specific ways real neuromodulation breaks the "one chemical, one direction, instant broadcast" model
- Make a scoped, falsifiable design decision for the project rather than a general essay

**Background the learner needs but that isn't in the available sources** (given here directly, not as an assigned reading, because the papers that would normally carry this — Yu & Dayan 2005 on expected/unexpected uncertainty, and standard pharmacology on D1/D2 signalling — aren't in the source list):
- Volume transmission (how dopamine, ACh, and NE actually reach targets outside direct synapses) diffuses over roughly tens to low hundreds of microns and acts over hundreds of milliseconds to seconds — orders of magnitude slower and more spatially constrained than a fast synaptic release event (sub-micron cleft, sub-millisecond), and very far from an instantaneous, uniform, whole-network mist.
- The standard division of labour: noradrenaline (locus coeruleus) tracks *unexpected* uncertainty — a sudden, unsignalled change in the rules (a cue that used to mean X now unpredictably means Y) — and tends to trigger a global interrupt/gain-reset. Acetylcholine (basal forebrain) tracks *expected* uncertainty — known, stable ambiguity or stochasticity within a model you already trust (a cue you know is only 70% reliable) — and tunes how strongly you weight new evidence against your existing belief, without necessarily discarding that belief.
- Dopamine's effect on plasticity is receptor-dependent in sign: neurons/synapses expressing D1-type receptors are typically pushed one way (associated with the direct pathway, generally facilitating potentiation) by rising dopamine, while D2-expressing neurons/synapses are typically pushed the other way — so the same concentration of the same molecule, at the same time, produces opposite plasticity effects depending on what's listening, not what's broadcasting.

**Task**
Write 300-600 words that:
1. Answer AC2-AC4 in your own words using the background above (don't just restate it — explain why each point is a problem for "one chemical, one direction, instant broadcast," specifically).
2. State one concrete, specific way your original "misted chemical" model is factually wrong about real biology (AC5) — a mechanism, not a restatement of the lesson's goal sentence.
3. Make an explicit decision for the project: does the stress variable (a single scalar per neuron tracking persistent unresolved prediction error, gating growth of new neuron pairs) need to become more than one channel before it's used to gate growth/pruning in Steps 3-4? Justify from the biology above, not from software-engineering taste. If you decide one channel is fine for now, say specifically what it would take to prove you wrong later (what a two-channel need would look like once you see it in simulation).
4. Name one concrete thing that breaks in the current single-scalar stress design if the answer to (3) turns out to be "no, one isn't enough" — e.g. a scenario where a neuron with high stress from an *unpredictable* structural change and a neuron with high stress from *known, stable* noisy input get treated identically by the same growth trigger, when biologically they'd be handled by different systems (NE-style global rewiring interrupt vs. ACh-style local reweighting).

**Deliverable**
300-600 word written defence covering all four points, addressed to a reader who knows the project but hasn't just read this lesson.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | AC2-AC4 explained with the *implication* for the broadcast model stated explicitly, not just the fact recited; AC5 names a specific mechanism (e.g. omission dip requiring signed subtraction, or receptor-dependent sign, or volume-transmission spatial/temporal scale) rather than re-stating the lesson goal; the project decision in (3) is a real yes/no with a stated falsification condition, not "it depends" or "more research is needed"; (4) gives one concrete scenario, not a generic worry. |
| Adequate | AC2-AC4 present but treated as isolated facts without stating what they break; AC5 correct but generic; (3) states a decision but the falsification condition is vague; (4) is generic ("stress might need more nuance") rather than a specific scenario. |
| Not yet | AC5 restates the lesson's goal sentence ("real neuromodulators are more specific than a misted chemical") instead of naming a mechanism — this is the explicit failure mode the lesson spec calls out; (3) ducks the decision entirely; word count far outside 300-600. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)
There is no single correct decision for (3) — grade the reasoning, not the verdict. A defensible "no, one channel is fine for now" argument: the stress variable's job (trigger local structural growth from *sustained, local* unresolved error) doesn't yet need to distinguish uncertainty *types*, only detect that error is high and persistent — that's closer to a slow local integrator than to a global broadcast, so the ACh/NE distinction (which is about *global* signals with different temporal/uncertainty profiles) may not bite until Steps 3-4 introduce something more broadcast-like (e.g. a network-wide "something changed" alarm). A defensible "yes, needs ≥2 channels" argument: if growth should behave differently under "this input is noisy but I already know that" (expected uncertainty → don't grow, just reweight) vs. "something in the graph topology just changed unpredictably" (unexpected uncertainty → grow/rewire), a single scalar cannot carry that distinction, and conflating them means the network either over-grows on ordinary noise or under-reacts to real structural change. Acceptable falsification conditions for either verdict: e.g. "if in simulation two neurons reach the same stress value for qualitatively different reasons and the single growth threshold produces the wrong action for one of them, split the channel."
Acceptable AC5 answers (any one, correctly explained): (a) omission of an expected reward produces a signed depression below baseline, which a magnitude-only signal cannot represent; (b) the same dopamine concentration has opposite plasticity effects at D1 vs D2 synapses, so "direction" isn't a property of the chemical at all, it's a property of the receptor; (c) volume transmission is spatially and temporally structured (tens-hundreds of µm, hundreds of ms-seconds) rather than an instantaneous uniform mist over the whole network; (d) at least two distinct modulators (ACh, NE) carry different kinds of uncertainty signal simultaneously, so "the" error/success chemical is already at least two chemicals doing different jobs.

- Hint 1: "Which of AC2-AC4 above is closest to something you already computed a number for in Piece 1 or Piece 2?"
- Hint 2: "For AC5, if your answer could have been written before this lesson started, it's not specific enough yet."
- Hint 3: "For the project decision — what would you actually see in a simulation log that would make you say 'I was wrong, split the channel'? If you can't name that, the decision isn't falsifiable yet."
- Never: never supply the verdict on (3) — this is the learner's design call, not the teacher's; never accept "real biology is more nuanced" as an AC5 answer under any phrasing; never let word count balloon past 600 without flagging it as a sign the learner hasn't found the specific mechanism yet (over-length written defences here are usually padding around a vague AC5).
