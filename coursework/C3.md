## Piece 1 — Balancing two error terms

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 60–90 min

**Learning goals**
- Write the precision-weighted energy for two error terms and state precision's relation to variance.
- Solve the balance-point equation for a two-term node and get exact numeric answers.
- Show algebraically, with numbers, how and why the settled value shifts when one term's precision changes.
- State what C1's flat energy (precision 1 everywhere) silently assumed.

**Task**

A single node `v` receives two predictions. Channel 1: `e1 = v - 3`, precision `π1 = 1`. Channel 2: `e2 = v - 6`, precision `π2` (varies below).

1. Write `F(v)` for general `π1, e1, π2, e2`. State how `π` relates to `σ²`. In one sentence, say what C1's choice of `π = 1` for every error channel silently assumed about the relative trustworthiness of those channels.
2. Set `π2 = 2`. Solve `dF/dv = 0` for `v`.
3. Set `π2 = 8` (`π1` still 1). Re-solve for `v`. In one sentence, state which target (3 or 6) `v` moved toward and why, in terms of precision.
4. Derive the general closed form `v* = (π1*a + π2*b) / (π1 + π2)` directly from `dF/dv = 0` (where `e1 = v-a`, `e2 = v-b`). Verify it reproduces both of your answers from steps 2 and 3.
5. Pick a third value of `π2` yourself (anything other than 2 or 8), compute `v*` by hand using your closed form, and confirm it falls between 3 and 6, on the correct side of 5.

**Deliverable:** the derivation and arithmetic for steps 1–5, shown in full (not just final numbers), including your chosen third `π2` and its result.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | `F(v)` and `π=1/σ²` correct; step 1's one-sentence answer says C1 assumed every channel equally trustworthy/equal-variance, not just "simplified"; steps 2–3 give exact `v=5` and `v≈5.667` with the shift correctly attributed to channel 2 becoming more precise/trusted; general closed form derived (not guessed) and verified against both numeric cases; step 5 numerically consistent with the trend. |
| Adequate | Correct final numbers for steps 2–3 but the closed form in step 4 is asserted rather than derived from `dF/dv=0`, or step 1's sentence is vague ("less weight" without saying why); step 5 present but arithmetic has a minor slip. |
| Not yet | Wrong sign somewhere in `dF/dv` (e.g. treating precision as multiplying `e` instead of `e²` before differentiating), `v` values don't match 5 and 5.667, or no closed form attempted. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- `F(v) = ½·(π1·e1² + π2·e2²)`. `π = 1/σ²`: precision is the inverse variance of that error channel's noise. Setting `π=1` everywhere (C1) assumes every error channel has the same variance — equally noisy, equally trustworthy — which is generally false and is exactly the assumption C3 removes.
- `dF/dv = π1·e1 + π2·e2 = π1·(v-3) + π2·(v-6)`.
- Step 2 (`π2=2`): `(v-3) + 2(v-6) = 3v - 15 = 0 → v = 5`.
- Step 3 (`π2=8`): `(v-3) + 8(v-6) = 9v - 51 = 0 → v = 51/9 ≈ 5.667`. Direction: `v` moved from 5 toward 6, because channel 2 (target 6) became more precise, so it now dominates the balance more than channel 1 does.
- Step 4: setting `dF/dv=0` for general `a,b`: `π1(v-a) + π2(v-b) = 0 → v(π1+π2) = π1 a + π2 b → v* = (π1 a + π2 b)/(π1+π2)`. Check: `π2=2 → (3+12)/3 = 5` ✓. `π2=8 → (3+48)/9 = 51/9` ✓.
- Step 5 example (any valid third point is acceptable, e.g. `π2=20`): `v* = (3 + 120)/21 = 123/21 ≈ 5.857` — closer to 6 than the `π2=8` case, consistent with the monotonic trend toward `b=6` as `π2` grows.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: if `dF/dv` is wrong, ask the learner to differentiate `π·e²` with respect to `v` one term at a time before combining.
- Hint 2: if step 4's closed form is just pattern-matched from steps 2–3 rather than derived, ask them to set `dF/dv=0` for symbolic `a, b, π1, π2` and solve for `v` from scratch.
- Hint 3: if step 3's direction sentence says "v got bigger" without saying why, ask which channel's target `v` is now closer to and which channel's precision changed.
- Never: state `v=5` or `v≈5.667` before the learner has produced them. Never supply the closed-form formula. Never discuss what happens as `π2 → ∞` (clamping) — that is C5's territory, not this lesson's.

## Piece 2 — Sweeping precision and breaking joint learning

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 90–120 min

**Learning goals**
- Build a small PC node with two error channels and settle it by iterating `dv/dt = -dF/dv`, not by jumping to the closed form.
- Empirically confirm Piece 1's balance point across a swept range of precision, and observe that the resulting weight-update magnitude does not scale the same way the settled value does.
- Demonstrate, from a naive online precision-update rule, why persistently small error does not imply a stable precision estimate — the instability that makes joint precision-and-weight learning unsafe.

**Task**

Part A (required). Same setup as Piece 1: `e1 = v-3` (`π1=1` fixed), `e2 = v-6` (`π2` swept), pre-synaptic activities `x1=x2=1`, weight-update learning rate `η=1`. Sweep `π2` over at least 20 log-spaced points from 0.1 to 50 (e.g. `np.logspace(-1, log10(50), 25)`). For each `π2`: **settle `v` by iterating the gradient update** `v ← v - α·dF/dv` (e.g. `α=0.05`, run until it stops moving, or a fixed 200+ steps) — do not shortcut to Piece 1's closed form for the settling step itself; use the closed form only to sanity-check your settled value. Record settled `v(π2)`, then compute `Δw1 = η·π1·e1·x1` and `Δw2 = η·π2·e2·x2` at that settled point. Plot settled `v` vs. `π2` (log x-axis) and `|Δw2|` vs. `π2`.

Part B (required). Hold `e` fixed at the constant `0.05` (a channel that is, by construction, always well predicted — do not let this `e` come from a settling node). Initialize `π = 1.0`, `η_π = 0.05`. Iterate the naive rule `π ← π + η_π·(π·e² − 1)` for at least 2000 steps. At each step also compute `Δw = η·π·e·x` (`η=1, x=1`). Plot `π(t)` and `Δw(t)` against iteration.

**Deliverable:** one self-contained script (<~100 lines, numpy or PyTorch, no repo imports), the two plots/tables from Parts A and B, and 4–6 sentences: (i) describe how settled `v` and `|Δw2|` behave as `π2` grows in Part A — do they scale the same way? — and connect the direction of the `v` shift back to Piece 1; (ii) describe what happens to `π(t)` and `Δw(t)` in Part B, and explain in terms of the update rule why persistently near-zero `e` does not stop it.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Part A settles `v` via iteration (matches closed form within a small tolerance) and correctly reports that `v` climbs toward 6 while `|Δw2|` grows but **saturates** rather than tracking `v`'s shift — i.e. explicitly notes these are not the same curve. Part B shows `π(t)` crossing zero and continuing to fall (or the mirror-image growth, if the learner's sign convention differs but is applied consistently) with no fixed point reached at this `e`, and correctly explains that because `π·e²` stays far below 1 for any precision value reached in a normal run, the rule keeps pushing `π` in the same direction indefinitely — a small, stable error does not brake the update. Correctly notes `|Δw(t)|` grows over the run despite `e` never changing. |
| Adequate | Part A shows the right qualitative shape but the settling loop is not clearly iterative (e.g. one big step), or the saturation of `|Δw2|` is observed but not stated as distinct from `v`'s behavior. Part B shows some divergence but the explanation of *why* `e` near zero fails to stabilize `π` is hand-wavy. |
| Not yet | Part A uses the closed form as the "settling" step with no iteration at all; Part B's rule is mis-implemented so `π` spuriously stabilizes (e.g. accidentally clipped, or `e` isn't actually held fixed), and no instability is observed or discussed. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- Part A closed form for cross-check: `v*(π2) = (3 + 6·π2)/(1+π2)`. Anchor values: `π2=0.1 → v≈3.273`; `π2=1 → v=4.5`; `π2=2 → v=5.0`; `π2=8 → v≈5.667`; `π2=50 → v≈5.941`.
- `e2 = v*-6 = -3/(1+π2)`, so `Δw2 = π2·e2·1 = -3π2/(1+π2)`. Magnitudes: `π2=0.1 → 0.273`; `π2=1 → 1.5`; `π2=2 → 2.0`; `π2=8 → 2.667`; `π2=50 → 2.941`; the limit as `π2→∞` is exactly `3` — it **saturates**, it does not grow without bound the way one might naively expect from "higher precision → bigger update." The reason: `e2` shrinks roughly as `1/π2` exactly fast enough to cap the product. This is the concrete form of "precision changes both which node moves most during settling and the magnitude of the weight update" — and the two do not scale together.
- Part B: with `e=0.05` fixed, `e²=0.0025`. `π·e²` stays under 1 for any `π` under ~400, so for the whole run the update is `Δπ ≈ η_π·(π·e² − 1) ≈ -η_π = -0.05` almost every step — an almost-constant decrease, i.e. `π(t) ≈ 1 - 0.05t`. It crosses zero near `t≈20` and keeps falling: `t=100 → π≈-4`; `t=1000 → π≈-49`; `t=5000 → π≈-249`. There is no fixed point reachable at this `e` within any realistic run, because `π·e²` would need `π` in the hundreds just to approach 1, and by then the sign of `π` itself has long gone unphysical (negative "precision," i.e. negative variance — meaningless). Correspondingly `|Δw(t)| = |π(t)|·0.05` grows roughly linearly and without bound: `t=100→≈0.2`; `t=1000→≈2.45`; `t=5000→≈12.45`. The instability: a channel whose error genuinely stays small (well-predicted, trustworthy) still has its precision estimate run away unboundedly under this rule, and that runaway estimate — not the actual error — ends up dominating the weight update. That is why learning precision jointly with weights online, via a rule like this, is unsafe without additional safeguards (bounding, clipping, much slower `η_π`, or fixing precision by design rather than learning it).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: if Part A's settling loop looks like one algebraic jump, ask to see the loop body and how many times it executes.
- Hint 2: if the learner reports `v` and `|Δw2|` "scale together," ask them to compute the ratio `Δw2/v` at `π2=2` and at `π2=50` and compare.
- Hint 3: if Part B doesn't diverge, ask them to print `π·e²` at several iterations and check whether it is close to 1 (the rule's target) or far below it.
- Never: state the saturation value (3) or the crossing point (`t≈20`) before the learner's own run has produced numbers in that neighborhood. Never hand over the reference implementation.

## Piece 3 — Guided reading: Feldman & Friston, attention as precision

**Source:** `papers/C3_feldman_friston_2010_attention_uncertainty.pdf`.

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 45–75 min

**Learning goals**
- Locate Feldman & Friston's formal treatment of precision within the free-energy framework and confirm it matches `π = 1/σ²`.
- Identify the specific empirical phenomenon they use to argue attention *is* precision-optimization, and the mechanism they propose implements it.
- Synthesize their abstract claim with the already-known PV/SST/VIP circuit roles — a step the paper itself does not make, so this is the learner's own reasoning, not something to extract from the text.

**Task**

Read Feldman & Friston (2010) with these questions in hand. For questions 1–4, answer by quoting or closely paraphrasing the specific passage, not by reconstructing from memory of PC theory in general — the point is to verify the paper says what you think it says.

1. Find where the paper formally introduces precision inside the free-energy/predictive-coding formulation. Write down their definition and confirm it matches `π = 1/σ²`.
2. Find the passage where they equate attention with something like "optimizing precision" (their exact phrasing may differ). Quote it, then paraphrase in your own words what "optimizing precision" means for a top-down prediction versus a bottom-up sensory signal, per their account.
3. The paper links precision-optimization to a specific physiological or psychophysical phenomenon (e.g. gain modulation, biased competition, contrast-gain effects, single-unit attentional effects). Identify which one they lean on as their primary illustration, and summarize the mechanism they propose for it (what changes physiologically when precision is "turned up" on a channel, in their account).
4. Does the paper name specific inhibitory interneuron subtypes (PV, SST, VIP) as implementing precision-weighting? If not — and take care to actually check rather than assume — what mechanism do they propose instead (e.g. synaptic gain, a neuromodulatory or receptor-level account)?
5. Synthesis, using what you already know rather than the paper: PV cells scale the precision of feedforward (bottom-up) error; VIP cells scale the precision of top-down predictions. Feldman & Friston frame attention specifically as a top-down, context-dependent optimization of precision. Which of PV or VIP would formally implement "attention" in their sense, and which one implements what you'd instead call "sensor confidence"? Justify from the definitions, not from a guess.

**Deliverable:** written answers to all five questions, with a specific quote or close paraphrase (with enough locating detail — section/heading — to find it in the PDF) for questions 1–4.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Questions 1–4 each cite a specific, real passage (verifiable against the PDF) rather than a generic PC-theory restatement; Q4 correctly reports whether interneuron subtypes are named (expect: no, given the paper's date) and correctly identifies what mechanism stands in for it; Q5's synthesis correctly names VIP as implementing attention in F&F's sense (top-down, uncertainty-on-predictions) and PV as the bottom-up confidence counterpart, with justification tied to both F&F's framing and the PV/VIP definitions. |
| Adequate | Q1–3 answered with passages that are in the right neighborhood but loosely quoted/paraphrased; Q4 answered correctly but without checking specifically (asserted from prior knowledge of the field rather than the text); Q5 reaches the right mapping but the justification only restates the definitions without connecting to F&F's specific "top-down" framing. |
| Not yet | Answers restate general PC/precision theory without evidence of having located anything specific in this paper; Q5 picks PV for attention, or gives no justification. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Questions 1–4 must be checked live against `papers/C3_feldman_friston_2010_attention_uncertainty.pdf` — this paper predates fixed interneuron-subtype microcircuit accounts of precision, so do not accept a fabricated claim that it names PV/SST/VIP explicitly, but also do not assume a specific answer to 1–3 without opening the PDF; verify the learner's quotes actually appear and are represented accurately, rather than grading against a canned quote here.

Question 5 has a determinable correct answer independent of the paper: attention, per the F&F framing, is the **top-down**, task/context-dependent optimization of precision on **predictions** — that is exactly VIP's role (scaling precision on top-down predictions), not PV's (which scales precision on bottom-up/feedforward error, i.e. sensor confidence, not attention in F&F's sense). A correct answer names VIP for attention and PV for sensor confidence, and justifies it by matching "top-down, predictions, context-dependent" to VIP's definition rather than by elimination alone.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: if Q1's quote is generic, ask the learner to search the PDF for where "precision" is first given a mathematical definition, not just discussed qualitatively.
- Hint 2: if Q2's quote is missing, suggest searching for phrasing like "optimizing precision" or "expected precision" near where they discuss attention.
- Hint 3: if Q5 defaults to PV, ask which of PV/VIP's inputs are top-down versus bottom-up, and which direction F&F's attention account runs.
- Never: supply a quote or paraphrase from the paper on the learner's behalf. Never confirm or deny a Q1–4 answer without having actually checked it against the PDF. Never state the Q5 answer (VIP) before the learner has attempted their own justification.

## Piece 4 — Written defence: the stress variable and a distrusted channel

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 45–60 min · **Length:** 300–600 words

**Learning goals**
- Apply precision-weighting to a concrete design question in this project: whether Step 4's stress variable should be precision-weighted.
- Reason concretely about the failure mode of an unweighted stress signal, using Piece 1's balance mechanics and Piece 2's instability finding.
- Recognize that "make stress precision-weighted" is itself vulnerable to Piece 2's joint-learning instability unless the precision it uses is either fixed by design or estimated safely.

**Task**

Write 300–600 words, as one connected argument, addressing:

1. Step 4's stress variable currently accumulates *unweighted* persistent error (roughly `stress ← stress + e²`, summed/decayed over time). Should it instead accumulate `π·e²` — the same quantity that appears inside `F`? Argue from Piece 1's energy formula: what does an unweighted accumulator implicitly assume about every error channel feeding it, and is that assumption any more defensible for stress than it was for C1's flat energy?
2. Concretely: what goes wrong in Step 4 if a neuron keeps accumulating stress from an error channel the network has good reason to distrust (i.e., a channel whose true precision is, or should be, low — a noisy sensor, an unreliable long-range connection)? Describe the specific failure this causes downstream (recall Step 4's stress → growth/pruning mechanism) — not just "it's wrong," but what the network actually does as a result.
3. Given Piece 2's finding, what would go wrong if you tried to fix this by learning `π` online with a naive rule and feeding that into the stress accumulator? State what would need to be true of the precision estimate (fixed by design, bounded, slowly updated, etc.) for a precision-weighted stress signal to actually be safe to use.

You may use the Step 2b soft desired-next-state clamp as a second, brief example (its strength relative to the sensory clamp is already, mechanically, a precision choice) if it strengthens the argument that precision decisions are already implicit in this project's design — but the required content is the stress-variable argument above.

**Deliverable:** 300–600 word written answer.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Correctly identifies that unweighted stress assumes every error channel is equally trustworthy (the same assumption C1's flat energy made, now baked into a growth/pruning signal); names the specific failure mode — a distrusted/noisy channel with legitimately large but uninformative error inflates stress and triggers spurious growth of neuron pairs (or blocks pruning) in response to noise rather than genuine unresolved structure, wasting resources or growing the network in the wrong place; correctly applies Piece 2 to say that naively *learning* the precision used for this weighting would risk the same runaway (a channel's precision estimate could crash or blow up under sustained near-zero or persistently-nonzero error respectively, corrupting the stress signal itself), and concludes precision for this purpose should be fixed by design (or bounded/slow) rather than jointly learned online, at least initially. |
| Adequate | Identifies that unweighted stress is a poor assumption and that a distrusted channel would inflate stress, but the description of the downstream consequence (growth/pruning) is generic; mentions Piece 2's instability only in passing without connecting it to *why* it matters specifically for a safe precision source. |
| Not yet | Argues for precision-weighted stress without identifying what unweighted stress currently assumes, or doesn't address the instability risk of learning the precision used for the weighting at all. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- Unweighted `stress ← stress + e²` treats every error channel as equally trustworthy, the same blanket assumption C1's `π=1` energy made (Piece 1, step 1). For stress specifically this is worse than for a settling energy: settling errors are transient and get re-evaluated every tick, while stress is explicitly designed to accumulate persistently — so an unweighted accumulator will persistently over-credit noisy channels, not just momentarily.
- Concrete failure: a channel with genuinely low precision (high variance, e.g. a noisy sensor or an unreliable long-range edge) will produce error that is large not because there is real unresolved structure to learn, but because the channel is inherently noisy. Unweighted stress cannot distinguish these two cases. Downstream, per the project's stress → growth/pruning mechanism, that neuron's stress crosses the growth threshold and the network spends resources spawning new neuron pairs to "resolve" what is actually irreducible sensor/channel noise — growth that will never pay off in reduced error, since the noise floor doesn't move. In the opposite direction, a channel that is falsely *trusted* (low error observed, but really because the network isn't looking at it hard, or precision is legitimately low so error swings are damped) could suppress stress and block genuinely-needed pruning or growth elsewhere. Precision-weighting (`π·e²`) is the fix in principle: it scales each channel's contribution to stress by how much that error is actually supposed to mean.
- Instability risk (from Piece 2): if `π` for this weighting is itself learned online with a naive rule like Piece 2's, the same runaway applies — a channel with persistently small error can have its `π` estimate run away unboundedly (Piece 2, Part B) rather than settle, meaning the "trustworthiness" scaling on the stress signal is now itself unstable and could swing the stress accumulator in either direction independent of genuine unresolved error. This defeats the purpose: stress would inherit noise from precision estimation on top of the noise it was trying to filter out. For this to be safe, `π` used in stress weighting should be fixed by design (e.g. set per channel type, analogous to how PV/VIP roles are structurally fixed, not learned per-tick) or updated with strong safeguards (heavy bounding/clipping, much slower timescale than stress itself, or a running-variance estimate rather than the naive rule) — the project should not couple stress's core signal to an unstable online estimator until precision estimation itself is shown to be safe.
- The Step 2b clamp-strength point, if used, should be framed only as supporting evidence that precision-like choices are already implicit and informal in the project (the clamp's relative strength is a precision ratio nobody currently calls one) — not as a substitute for the required stress-variable argument.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: if the answer doesn't name the "equally trustworthy" assumption, ask what unweighted `stress += e²` implicitly assumes about a very noisy channel versus a very reliable one.
- Hint 2: if the downstream failure is vague, ask specifically what Step 4 does when a neuron's stress crosses threshold, and whether that action is useful when the error being tracked is irreducible noise.
- Hint 3: if the instability risk is missing, point back (without re-explaining it) to Piece 2 Part B and ask what would happen if the `π` in `π·e²` here came from that same naive update rule.
- Never: tell the learner whether stress "should" be precision-weighted before they've made their own argument — this is a real open design question for the project, not a fact to be delivered. Never supply the specific failure-mode wording above verbatim as a hint.
