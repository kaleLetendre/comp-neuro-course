## Piece 1 — Re-indexing the energy

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 60–90 min

**Learning goals**
- Rewrite the layered PC energy as a per-node sum over an explicit neighbour set, and show the two are the same formalism under a relabeling.
- Identify exactly which layered term the graph term replaces, and why nothing about locality changes.
- Trace, by hand, how a node's update depends on *both* its own prediction error and the errors of nodes it helps predict — and see this produce non-layered dependency.

**Task**

**Part A — generalize the energy.**
Layered PC (from C1) writes, for a network with layers indexed by ell = 0..L:

  E = sum_{ell=1}^{L} || x_ell − f(W_ell x_{ell−1}) ||^2

Each layer's error term uses exactly one fixed neighbour set: "the layer below." Do the following:

1. Define, for a graph with node set V and directed edges (j → i) meaning "j predicts into i," the in-neighbour set N_in(i).
2. Write the per-node prediction  x̂_i = sum_{j in N_in(i)} w_ij f(x_j)  and per-node error  e_i = x_i − x̂_i.
3. Write the total energy F = (1/2) sum_{i in V} e_i^2.
4. State in one sentence exactly which term in the layered E this replaces, and what the replacement is (be precise: it is not "layers become nodes," it is which *index set* changes and how).
5. Write the local weight update rule dw_ij/dt = eta * e_i * f(x_j) and confirm in one line why it only needs signals available at the two endpoints of the edge (pre-synaptic activity f(x_j), post-synaptic error e_i) — this is the "virtual memristor" property from CLAUDE.md.
6. Write the inference (settling) update for a free node i:
   dx_i/dt = −eta * (e_i − sum_{k : i in N_in(k)} w_ki e_k)
   Explain in one sentence why the second term exists in the graph case but is usually invisible in a strictly feedforward layered derivation (hint: in a layered net, does a node ever appear in more than one other node's prediction?).

**Part B — worked example, by hand, with numbers given.**
Take the 3-node fully-connected reciprocal graph A—B—C (every pair has an edge in both directions: A→B, B→A, B→C, C→B, A→C, C→A). Use identity f (no nonlinearity), all weights w = 0.5, integration step eta = 0.1. Clamp x_A = 1.0 for all steps. Initialize x_B = 0, x_C = 0.5 (free).

Step 1 — compute by hand: x̂_A, x̂_B, x̂_C, then e_A, e_B, e_C, then the updates dx_B and dx_C using the rule from A.6, then the new x_B, x_C. Report F = (1/2) sum e_i^2 before the update.

Step 2 — repeat once more from the new x_B, x_C. Report the new F and confirm whether it went down.

**Part C — do it yourself.**
Run **Step 3** by hand from your Step 2 outputs (same eta, same clamp on A). Report x_B, x_C, and F after Step 3. State whether F is still decreasing and whether e_A, e_B, e_C are heading toward zero or toward some nonzero fixed point — and if nonzero, say in one sentence *why* three error terms can't all be driven to zero by only two free variables (x_B, x_C) with x_A pinned away from its neighbours' joint prediction.

**Part D — answer AC2 directly.** In this same A–B–C graph, with A clamped and C free: what does B's prediction depend on? Write it out (the actual expression, not just "A and C"). Then contrast explicitly: in a layered net where layer ℓ's prediction depends only on layer ℓ−1, could a hidden unit's *value update* ever be driven by the error of a unit that is not adjacent to it in the "next layer up" sense the way B here is driven by both e_A and e_C? Name the specific structural reason (bidirectional edges / no acyclic layer order) that makes this possible on a graph and impossible by construction in the layered case.

**Deliverable**
A written derivation (LaTeX, Markdown with math, or legible handwritten scan) covering Parts A–D, with Part B/C's numeric steps shown (not just final numbers — show x̂_i, e_i, dx_i at each step).

**Rubric**

| Strong | Part A energy and update rules are correct and self-derived (not copied from this sheet's Part B mechanics); Part B/C arithmetic matches the grader key to within rounding; Part C correctly identifies convergence to a nonzero fixed point and explains why (df=2 free variables, 3 error constraints); Part D gives the explicit expression for B's prediction and names bidirectionality/no-layer-order as the structural cause. |
| Adequate | Energy and update rules correct with minor notational slips; Part B arithmetic mostly right (one arithmetic slip tolerated if the method is right); Part D correctly says B depends on both A and C but the layered contrast is vague or missing the structural "why". |
| Not yet | Energy still written as a layer-indexed sum with nodes substituted in without changing the index set; Part B skipped or only final numbers given with no e_i/x̂_i shown; Part D says B depends on A and C but gives no contrast with layered nets, or gets the direction of dependency wrong (e.g. claims B only depends on A because A is clamped "first"). |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- A.3–A.4: F = (1/2) sum_i (x_i − sum_{j in N_in(i)} w_ij f(x_j))^2. This replaces the single layer-indexed error term x_ell − f(W_ell x_{ell−1}) — the sum over layers ell becomes a sum over *all nodes* i in V, and the fixed neighbour set "layer ell−1" becomes the node-specific set N_in(i). The functional form (squared prediction error, local update) is identical; only what "neighbour" means changes.
- A.6: the second term (sum_{k: i in N_in(k)} w_ki e_k) exists because node i can be a predictor for more than one other node in a graph; in a strict layered feedforward net a unit in layer ell−1 is *only* ever used to predict layer ell, so this sum in practice has at most one nonzero contribution in the layered case and is usually folded into a single "backward pass" term — not absent in principle, just degenerate.
- Part B, Step 1: x̂_A = 0.25, e_A = 0.75; x̂_B = 0.75, e_B = −0.75; x̂_C = 0.5, e_C = 0.0. F_before = 0.5*(0.75^2 + 0.75^2 + 0^2) = 0.5625. dx_B = eta*(1.125) = 0.1125 → x_B = 0.1125. dx_C = eta*(0) = 0 → x_C = 0.5 (unchanged).
- Part B, Step 2 (from x_B=0.1125, x_C=0.5): x̂_A=0.30625, e_A=0.69375; x̂_B=0.75, e_B=−0.6375; x̂_C=0.55625, e_C=−0.05625. New F (computed on these errors, i.e. the state just before the Step-2 update) = 0.5*(0.69375^2+0.6375^2+0.05625^2) ≈ 0.4454. This is lower than 0.5625 — accept ±0.01 rounding. dx_B ≈ 0.09563 → x_B ≈ 0.20813. dx_C ≈ 0.00844 → x_C ≈ 0.50844.
- Part C, Step 3 (from x_B≈0.20813, x_C≈0.50844): e_A≈0.64172, e_B≈−0.54609, e_C≈−0.09563. dx_B≈0.08188 → x_B≈0.29001. dx_C≈0.01434 → x_C≈0.52278. Accept any answer within ±0.01 of these if the method (compute x̂, e, then dx from the Part-A update rule) is shown correctly. F is still decreasing (F3 ≈ 0.322, down from 0.445). Errors are heading toward a *nonzero* fixed point, not zero: with A clamped at 1.0 and only two free variables (x_B, x_C), the system cannot in general zero all three of e_A, e_B, e_C simultaneously — it settles where the *sum* of squared errors is minimized, not where every term is individually zero. This is the correct answer to flag; a learner who claims errors → 0 has not noticed the degrees-of-freedom mismatch and should not pass Part C.
- Part D: B's prediction is x̂_B = w_AB*x_A + w_CB*x_C — it depends on *both* A (clamped) and C (free), simultaneously and symmetrically, because both edges are live regardless of clamping status. In a layered net, a hidden unit's *value update* is driven only by the error of the unit(s) immediately above it in the fixed forward order — never by a unit's error that sits "beyond" what would be the next layer, because no such edge exists. The structural cause is exactly this: graphs allow bidirectional edges and impose no acyclic layer order, so a node can sit in the error-generating path of any of its neighbours in either direction, whereas a layer's fixed position in the stack forces influence to flow only from layer ell to ell+1 and back, never sideways or "through" to a non-adjacent layer.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: If you's Part A energy still has an "ell" anywhere, point at CLAUDE.md's neuron-pair vocabulary and ask them to name the neighbour set for a specific node instead of a layer index.
- Hint 2: If Part B arithmetic is wrong, ask them to show x̂_i for one node only and check that against the grader key before letting them continue to the next node.
- Hint 3: If Part C claims errors go to zero, ask "how many numbers can you still change, and how many equations are you trying to satisfy" — do not name degrees of freedom directly.
- Never: never supply the Part A general energy equation, the Part B numeric answers, or the Part D structural explanation directly, even partially, even after multiple wrong attempts — restate the hint ladder instead.

## Piece 2 — Settling on an adjacency matrix

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 2–3 hours

**Learning goals**
- Implement PC inference (settling) as pure local gradient descent on an arbitrary adjacency matrix, with no notion of "layer."
- Show empirically that one set of trained weights, clamped differently at test time, produces three qualitatively different behaviours — no retraining.
- Test whether settling still converges when the graph has cycles, and find a step-size regime where it visibly fails to.

**Task**

Build a self-contained PyTorch or numpy script (<~100 lines, synthetic data only, no autograd used for the weight update — the rule must be the local delta rule from Piece 1, not `loss.backward()` through the settling loop).

1. **Graph.** 6 nodes, values in R. Two triangles sharing a bridge edge: triangle {0,1,2} fully reciprocally connected, triangle {3,4,5} fully reciprocally connected, plus a reciprocal bridge edge (2,3). This graph has cycles by construction.
2. **Synthetic training patterns.** Generate 4 fixed 6-vectors (e.g. random but fixed-seed, values in [-1, 1]) — call them P0..P3.
3. **Training.** For each pattern, clamp *all* 6 nodes to the pattern's values (no free nodes), run the local weight update dw_ij += eta_w * e_i * f(x_j) for several passes over all 4 patterns (f = tanh or identity, your choice, but state which). No inference is needed during training since everything is clamped — only weight learning.
4. **Inference engine.** Write one function `settle(w, x_init, clamped_mask, clamped_vals, steps, eta_x)` that runs the Piece-1 update rule for free nodes only, holding clamped nodes fixed, and returns the trajectory of x and of F at every step.
5. **Three clamp configs from the *same* trained weights** (no retraining between them):
   - **Config 1 ("classification-like"):** clamp 5 of 6 nodes of a test pattern, leave 1 free. Check the free node settles near the true value for that pattern.
   - **Config 2 ("associative memory / pattern completion"):** clamp only 2 of 6 nodes (a small cue) matching one training pattern, leave the other 4 free. Check the settled 6-vector is close (in L2) to the *correct* full training pattern, not to the others.
   - **Config 3 ("generation"):** clamp 1 node only (treat it like a label/seed), leave the remaining 5 free. Check the generated vector is closest, by L2, to the training pattern whose corresponding node value was used as the seed.
6. **Cycles and convergence.** Plot/print F vs. iteration for at least one config. Confirm F is monotonically non-increasing for a small eta_x (e.g. 0.05–0.1). Then deliberately pick a large eta_x (e.g. eta_x >= 1.5, or scale up the weights) and show F oscillating or diverging instead of settling. Report the approximate eta_x threshold where behaviour flips from convergent to non-convergent for your specific weights.

**Deliverable**
The script, plus a short (under one page) results note: final settled vectors and L2 distances to all 4 training patterns for each config (showing the correct one wins), the F-vs-iteration behaviour for a convergent and a non-convergent eta_x, and the observed instability threshold.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All three configs correctly recover the intended pattern from one set of weights; weight update is provably local (no autograd across the settling loop, e_i and f(x_j) only); cyclic graph settles with monotonic F decrease at small eta_x; a specific eta_x (or weight-scale) is shown to break convergence with F visibly increasing/oscillating, and the note explains *why* (loop gain / step size) rather than just showing the plot. |
| Adequate | Configs 1–2 work, config 3 (generation) is weak or only partially distinguishes patterns; monotonic decrease shown at small eta_x; divergence demonstrated but not explained. |
| Not yet | Fewer than two configs distinguish the correct pattern; weight update secretly uses `loss.backward()`/autograd through multiple settling steps (violates locality); no divergence case attempted, or F-vs-iteration not reported at all. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- Expected: with tanh or identity f, eta_w ~0.01–0.05 over enough passes (tens to a few hundred, depending on pattern separation), the 4 training patterns should become distinguishable fixed points of the network. Config 1's free node should land within roughly 0.1–0.2 (absolute, values in [-1,1]) of the true value for a reasonably separated pattern set; if you's 4 random patterns happen to be too similar, weaker separation is acceptable — check they at least rank the correct pattern closest.
- Config 2 and 3: correctness is "closest by L2 to the right training pattern among the 4," not exact recovery — grade on rank-correctness, not tight numeric tolerance, since pattern separation depends on you's random seed and training length.
- Convergence: F must be non-increasing (allow floating-point noise ~1e-6) step-to-step at small eta_x on the cyclic graph — cycles do not by themselves block convergence. At large eta_x, expect either oscillation (F alternating up/down) or monotonic divergence (F growing without bound) — either is an acceptable demonstration of failure; reject a "convergence failure" that is actually just too few iterations at a small eta_x (that's under-training, not instability).
- Watch for the common bug: computing e_i using nodes' *pre-update* values for all i simultaneously (Jacobi-style) is correct and expected; if you accidentally updates x_i in place and uses the new value for x_{i+1}'s prediction in the same step (Gauss-Seidel-style without acknowledging it), that's not wrong but should be flagged as a design choice, not silently accepted as identical to the derivation in Piece 1.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: If Config 3 fails to distinguish patterns, ask what happens to a single seed node's information as it propagates two hops through tanh-squashed weights — do not suggest adding more clamped nodes (that would defeat the point of testing generation from a thin seed).
- Hint 2: If no divergence is found, ask them what the largest eigenvalue of the effective weight matrix might have to do with a fixed step size, without naming "spectral radius" outright.
- Hint 3: If they used `.backward()` on a multi-step settling trajectory, point at CLAUDE.md's "every learning rule must be purely local" constraint and ask them to point to which line computes e_i and which computes f(x_j) for the weight update.
- Never: never write or debug you's `settle()` function for them; never hand them the training-pattern separation or eta_x threshold numbers — those depend on their own random seed and must be observed, not looked up.

## Piece 4 — What the layer split was buying

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 45–60 min

**Learning goals**
- Synthesize Pieces 1–3 into a single argument: graph PC is a re-indexing of layered PC, not new maths.
- State concretely what a fixed input/output layer split *bought* (some guarantee or simplicity) and what it *cost* (flexibility this project's later steps need).
- Connect "any node clampable" to the specific mechanism it removes the need for when topology itself changes at runtime (Step 04's growth/pruning).

**Task**

Write 300–600 words defending or complicating this claim:

> "Going from layered predictive coding to predictive coding on an arbitrary graph is a re-indexing of the same energy function, not a new formalism — and the thing a fixed input/output layer split was buying was never expressiveness, it was a convergence/scheduling guarantee that graph PC has to earn some other way."

Your defence must:
- State the re-indexing explicitly (in-neighbours replace "layer below" — reference Piece 1's energy).
- Give one concrete example of what a fixed layer order guarantees "for free" that a general graph does not (draw on Piece 1 Part C or Piece 2's divergence experiment, or Piece 3's Q7 finding — something you actually derived or observed, not an abstract claim).
- Give one concrete reason "any node clampable" matters for Step 04 (stress-triggered growth/pruning): name the specific bookkeeping or mechanism (e.g. a fixed input/output layer index, or a role map that must be updated when topology changes) that this flexibility removes the need for.
- Take a position on whether the claim as stated is fully correct, or whether it understates/overstates something (e.g., is convergence really the *only* thing the layer split bought, or does it also simplify weight-update timing, credit assignment, or the C6 backprop-equivalence proof?).

**Deliverable**
300–600 words, prose (not bullet points), in your own words.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Re-indexing stated precisely and correctly (names the actual index-set change, not just "layers become nodes"); the "what layer order buys for free" example is concrete and drawn from your own derivation/simulation/reading rather than asserted; the Step 04 tie-in names a specific mechanism (e.g. no fixed input/output role map to maintain when pairs are added/pruned) rather than a generic "it's more flexible"; takes and defends a real position on the claim rather than agreeing with it wholesale. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- Correct re-indexing statement: the sum over layer index ℓ (with each ℓ's error depending only on the fixed set "layer ℓ−1") becomes a sum over node index i (with each i's error depending on N_in(i), a per-node, possibly-irregular, possibly-bidirectional set). The energy's functional form (sum of squared local prediction errors, gradient-descent inference, local Hebbian-style weight update) is unchanged.
- Acceptable "buys for free" examples: (a) a layered net's forward/settling pass has a natural evaluation order (bottom-up or top-down) with no risk of the step-size-dependent oscillation/divergence Piece 2 demonstrated on the cyclic graph — the acyclic structure gives a schedule where "converge" can mean "one clean pass" rather than "iterate until a fixed point"; (b) the layer split is what makes the C6 exact-backprop-equivalence proof clean, since equivalence proofs need a well-defined forward/backward direction; (c) credit assignment (which weight update is fixed at what point in the T-step inference loop) is trivial to define per layer, but must be argued more generally on a graph (this is what Piece 3 Q2/Q3 examined).
- Acceptable Step 04 mechanism: without free clamping, growth/pruning would require maintaining and updating a separate "role registry" (which nodes are designated input/output/hidden) every time a neuron pair is added or removed, and re-deriving or re-checking that the network's fixed input/output split still makes sense after a topology edit. Free clamping removes the need for any such registry — a newly grown or surviving pair is clampable or free purely as a per-trial choice, independent of its position in the (constantly changing) graph.
- A Strong answer should push back somewhere — e.g. arguing the claim understates the cost by not mentioning that arbitrary graphs also give up a clean stopping criterion for inference (you need a step-size/iteration-count choice, not a fixed number of forward-pass layers), which is exactly what Piece 2's divergence experiment surfaced.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: If the re-indexing statement is vague, ask them to write the two energy sums (layered, graph) side by side and point at exactly which symbol changed meaning.
- Hint 2: If the "buys for free" example is abstract, ask them which of their own Piece 1 or Piece 2 numbers would have looked different if the graph had been a strict DAG instead of containing a cycle.
- Hint 3: If the Step 04 tie-in is generic, ask "what would you have to update, in code, every time a neuron pair is added, if input/output roles were fixed by node index" — do not name "role registry" for them.
- Never: never draft or rewrite a sentence of the defence for you, even a topic sentence; never tell them whether their "position" on the claim is the "right" one to take — grade only whether the position is defended with something concrete from Pieces 1–3.
