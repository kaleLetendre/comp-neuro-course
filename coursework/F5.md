## Piece 1 — RC membrane and the Nernst equation

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** ~45–60 min

**Learning goals**
- Derive the membrane time constant from the passive RC circuit and reason about how it scales with conductance.
- Derive the Nernst equation from equal electrochemical potential, not just quote it.
- Use a computed reversal potential to reason about current direction and synaptic sign (excitatory vs. inhibitory).

**Task**

*Part A — RC circuit.* A patch of membrane has specific capacitance C_m = 1 µF/cm² and leak conductance g_L = 0.3 mS/cm² (the values used in the classic Hodgkin–Huxley squid-axon model, D&A ch. 5, *Single-Compartment Models*).
1. Write the sub-threshold (leak-only) single-compartment membrane equation per unit area: C_m dV/dt = −g_L(V − E_L) + I_e/A.
2. Rearrange it into the standard form dV/dt = −(V − V_∞)/τ_m and read off τ_m in terms of C_m and g_L.
3. Compute τ_m in ms for the numbers above.
4. State τ_m if g_L doubles (e.g., a synaptic conductance opens on top of the leak), and in one sentence explain what that does to the neuron's ability to temporally summate two inputs a few ms apart.

*Part B — Nernst equation.* At equilibrium the electrochemical potential of an ion is equal on both sides of the membrane: μ = RT ln[X] + zFV is the same inside and outside.
1. Starting from μ_in = μ_out, solve algebraically for (V_out − V_in) and show it reduces to E_X = (RT/zF) ln([X]_out/[X]_in). Show the intermediate line where the ln[X] terms are separated from the zFV terms — don't skip straight to the formula.
2. Compute E_K at human body temperature (37 °C = 310.15 K) for [K+]_in = 140 mM, [K+]_out = 5 mM, using R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), F = 96485 C/mol, z = +1.
3. Given a typical resting potential V_rest ≈ −65 mV, state whether the net K+ current at rest is inward or outward, and justify it from the sign of (V_rest − E_K).
4. A hypothetical synaptic channel has reversal potential E_syn = 0 mV. In one sentence, state the general rule (in terms of E_syn vs. V_rest) for when opening a channel is depolarizing (excitatory) vs. hyperpolarizing (inhibitory), and classify this channel.

**Deliverable**

A written (handwritten photo or typed) derivation covering both parts, with every algebraic step shown — not just final numbers — and the one-sentence answers where asked.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Both derivations show the intermediate algebra (RC rearrangement; μ_in = μ_out separated into log and voltage terms). Both numeric answers correct to within rounding. Both one-sentence reasoning answers correctly use the sign/magnitude of a voltage difference, not just a memorized rule. |
| Adequate | Final numbers correct but one derivation jumps a step (e.g. quotes τ_m = C/g without showing the ODE rearrangement, or quotes Nernst without the equal-electrochemical-potential setup). Reasoning answers correct but stated as a rule without justifying from this specific case. |
| Not yet | Numeric answer(s) wrong, unit mismatch (e.g. mixing mS/cm² and µF/cm² without cancelling units), or the excitatory/inhibitory rule is stated backwards. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

- τ_m = C_m/g_L = 1 µF/cm² ÷ 0.3 mS/cm² = 3.33 ms (exactly 10/3 ms). If g_L doubles to 0.6 mS/cm², τ_m halves to 1.67 ms — a shorter integration window, so two inputs separated by a few ms summate less (the first has decayed more by the time the second arrives).
- Nernst derivation: μ_in = RT ln[X]_in + zFV_in, μ_out = RT ln[X]_out + zFV_out. Setting equal and solving: zF(V_in − V_out) = RT ln([X]_out/[X]_in) ⇒ E_X ≡ V_in − V_out = (RT/zF) ln([X]_out/[X]_in). (Sign convention may flip depending on whether you defines E_X as V_in−V_out or V_out−V_in — accept either as long as it's used consistently and matches the numeric answer's sign.)
- RT/F at 310.15 K = 8.314 × 310.15 / 96485 = 26.73 mV. E_K = 26.73 mV × ln(5/140) = 26.73 × (−3.332) = **−89.05 mV** (accept −88 to −90 mV depending on rounding/T in °C vs K).
- V_rest (−65 mV) − E_K (−89 mV) = +24 mV > 0, so the driving force pushes K+ **outward** at rest (consistent with K+ leak current stabilizing/hyperpolarizing the resting potential).
- Rule: a channel is depolarizing (excitatory) at rest iff E_syn > V_rest, hyperpolarizing (inhibitory) iff E_syn < V_rest. Here E_syn = 0 mV > V_rest = −65 mV, so this channel is **excitatory**.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "In the leak-only equation, divide both sides by g_L and match term-by-term against dV/dt = −(V−V_∞)/τ. What plays the role of τ?"
- Hint 2: "For Nernst, write μ_in and μ_out as separate expressions before setting them equal — don't try to write the final ratio from memory."
- Hint 3: "You have τ_m and E_K as numbers now — plug V_rest and E_K into (V−E_K) and just check its sign before writing the conclusion."
- Never: state τ_m, E_K, or the excitatory/inhibitory verdict for you before they've shown their own algebra; never do the ln(5/140) arithmetic for them.

## Piece 2 — Simulation: Hodgkin–Huxley spike generator

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 90–150 min

**Learning goals**
- Implement the full conductance-based Hodgkin–Huxley model from its equations, not a library.
- Demonstrate the all-or-none property empirically via a threshold search.
- Identify, from simulated gating-variable traces, which gate ends the spike's rising phase and why.
- Measure the refractory period directly with a paired-pulse protocol, distinguishing absolute from relative refractoriness.

**Task**

Implement the standard Hodgkin–Huxley squid-axon model (D&A ch. 5, *The Hodgkin-Huxley Model*), in Python with NumPy only, no downloads, roughly 60 lines. Use exactly these parameters and rate functions (V in mV, rates in ms⁻¹, currents in µA/cm², t in ms):

```
C_m  = 1.0     µF/cm²
gNa  = 120.0   mS/cm²      E_Na = 50.0  mV
gK   = 36.0    mS/cm²      E_K  = -77.0 mV
gL   = 0.3     mS/cm²      E_L  = -54.4 mV

alpha_m(V) = 0.1*(V+40) / (1 - exp(-(V+40)/10))
beta_m(V)  = 4*exp(-(V+65)/18)
alpha_h(V) = 0.07*exp(-(V+65)/20)
beta_h(V)  = 1 / (1 + exp(-(V+35)/10))
alpha_n(V) = 0.01*(V+55) / (1 - exp(-(V+55)/10))
beta_n(V)  = 0.125*exp(-(V+65)/80)

dm/dt = alpha_m(V)*(1-m) - beta_m(V)*m      (same form for h, n)

C_m dV/dt = I_ext - gNa*m^3*h*(V-E_Na) - gK*n^4*(V-E_K) - gL*(V-E_L)
```

Initialize V = −65 mV (resting) and initialize m, h, n at their steady-state values at that voltage (x_∞ = α_x/(α_x+β_x)) — do not start them at 0. Integrate with forward Euler, dt = 0.01 ms.

Then:

(a) **Threshold and all-or-none.** Deliver a 1 ms current pulse starting at t = 5 ms. Write a bisection search over pulse amplitude (µA/cm²) that finds the current at which the trace transitions from "peak V stays below 0 mV" to "peak V shoots above 0 mV". Report the threshold current and the peak V just below vs. just above it.

(b) **Which gate ends the rise.** Run one clearly suprathreshold pulse. Record m(t), h(t), n(t) alongside V(t) through the spike. Identify, by inspecting the traces, which of the three variables is falling (not rising) at the moment V peaks and starts to repolarize, and state what that variable physically gates.

(c) **Refractory period.** Deliver a first pulse (amplitude comfortably above your threshold from (a)) at t = 5 ms, then a second, separate 1 ms pulse at t = 5+delay ms, for at least 8 delay values spanning roughly 1–20 ms. For each delay, determine (by inspecting V(t) in the window around the second pulse, not just a threshold-crossing counter) whether the second pulse evokes a genuine second spike. Do this once with the second pulse at the same amplitude as the first, and once with a much larger second-pulse amplitude (e.g. ~10× threshold). Report the shortest delay at which even the very strong pulse fails to spike (absolute refractory period) and the shortest delay at which the same-amplitude pulse succeeds (an estimate of the relative refractory period).

**Deliverable**

One self-contained `.py` file (~60 lines) that prints: the bisected threshold current and the two peak-V values bracketing it; a short printed statement naming which gating variable ends the rising phase; and a table of delay → spike/no-spike outcomes for both second-pulse amplitudes, with your stated absolute and relative refractory period in ms.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Model spikes correctly (steady-state init, correct rate functions). Threshold within ~15% of the grader key. Correctly names the inactivation gate (h) as terminating the rise, referencing its own falling value near the peak. Refractory table correctly separates the very-short-delay "spike" readings (an artifact of the window overlapping the first spike's own falling phase) from genuine second-pulse recovery, and reports an absolute refractory period in the 5–10 ms range and a longer relative-refractory recovery time for the weaker pulse. |
| Adequate | Model spikes and threshold is in a plausible range, but the refractory analysis conflates the short-delay artifact with a real second spike, or reports only one of absolute/relative refractoriness. Gate identification correct but not tied to your own trace values. |
| Not yet | Model never spikes (usually a units error, missing division by C_m, or gates initialized at 0), threshold search doesn't converge, or the wrong gating variable (m or n instead of h) is named as ending the rise. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — run against your own printed numbers, not exact-match; this was verified by executing the reference implementation)

- Bisected threshold for a 1 ms pulse at t=5 ms, dt=0.01 ms: **≈ 6.91 µA/cm²**. Peak V just below threshold ≈ **−49.6 mV** (subthreshold local response, no spike); peak V just above ≈ **+7.8 mV** and climbing to a full spike — this jump from ~−50 mV to a full ~+40 mV spike for a <0.1% change in stimulus is the all-or-none property. (Exact value depends on dt and pulse duration; accept anything in the 5–10 µA/cm² range for these parameters, as long as a sharp transition is demonstrated.)
- Suprathreshold run (amplitude 15 µA/cm²): spike peaks at **t ≈ 6.82 ms** (i.e. ~1.8 ms after pulse onset) at **V ≈ +40.3 mV**. At that peak, m ≈ 0.90 (still high — Na+ activation hasn't collapsed), **h ≈ 0.35 and still falling**, reaching its minimum ≈0.08 around t ≈ 8.7 ms. n is still rising at the peak, reaching its own maximum (≈0.77) later, around t ≈ 8.4 ms. **h (Na+ inactivation) is the variable falling at the peak and terminating the rise** — it shuts off the inward Na+ current, not m (which is still near its max) and not n (which peaks even later and drives the falling/AHP phase).
- Refractory scan, first pulse = 15 µA/cm² (safely suprathreshold alone):
  - With a very strong second pulse (60 µA/cm², ~9× threshold): no second spike evocable for delay ≤ 7 ms; first successful second spike at delay = 8 ms. → **absolute refractory period ≈ 7–8 ms** for this parameter set.
  - With a same-amplitude (15 µA/cm²) second pulse: no second spike for delays 3–12 ms; spiking resumes at delay = 13 ms. → **relative refractory period extends to ≈ 12–13 ms** for a stimulus no stronger than the one that fired the first spike.
  - Known artifact to watch for: naive threshold-crossing checks at delay = 1–2 ms will report a "spike" — this is the tail of the *first* action potential itself (which peaks ~1.8 ms after its own pulse onset) still being inside the measurement window, not a real second spike. A learner who reports delay-1 or delay-2 as genuine second spikes without this caveat has not actually verified their detector against the trace.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Compute α and β for m, h, n at V=−65 mV by hand (or print them) and initialize each gate at α/(α+β) — starting at 0 will give you a non-physiological first few ms."
- Hint 2: "If V never spikes for any amplitude you try, check that you divided the whole right-hand side by C_m, and that gNa/gK/gL are in mS/cm² while C_m is in µF/cm² — a mismatched factor of 1000 will silently kill spiking."
- Hint 3: "For the paired-pulse table, hold the first pulse amplitude fixed and well above your own threshold, and vary only the delay of the second pulse — don't co-vary amplitude and delay in the same sweep."
- Never: hand you the working numpy implementation, the bisected threshold value, or the refractory-period numbers; only confirm whether their own printed results are in a plausible range once they've run their own code, and point them at which of the three gates to re-examine if they misidentify h.

## Piece 3 — Guided reading

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** ~45 min

**Learning goals**
- Extract D&A's own definitions of the passive membrane equation, the gating-variable formalism, and the Hodgkin–Huxley current-balance equation.
- Locate, in the text, which gate D&A assigns to opening vs. closing the Na+ conductance.
- Extract D&A's account of stochastic single-channel models and what the macroscopic conductance represents.

**Task**

Read D&A ch. 5, sections *Introduction*, *Electrical Properties of Neurons*, *Single-Compartment Models*, *Voltage-Dependent Conductances*, *The Hodgkin-Huxley Model*, *Modeling Channels*. Answer in your own words (no verbatim copying), naming the subsection each answer comes from:

1. In *Electrical Properties of Neurons*, what physical quantities do the membrane capacitance and the leak conductance each represent, and what is the resulting sub-threshold equation usually called?
2. In *Single-Compartment Models*, write out the general current-balance equation D&A gives for a membrane with an arbitrary number of ionic conductances (the sum over channel types, each with its own reversal potential).
3. In *Voltage-Dependent Conductances*, what is the standard differential-equation form D&A gives for a gating variable, and how are its steady-state value and time constant expressed in terms of the α/β rate functions?
4. In *The Hodgkin-Huxley Model*, which three gating variables are introduced, and which ionic conductance (and power of the gate) does each belong to?
5. Still in that section: what specifically extinguishes the inward sodium current during a spike — and is it the same gate that opened it, or a different one?
6. In *Modeling Channels*, what is a single ion channel assumed to do, and what does the macroscopic conductance g(t) represent when there are N such channels?

**Deliverable**

Six short answers (2–5 sentences each), each tagged with the subsection it draws from.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All six answers match the expected content below, in your own words, correctly tagged to subsection. |
| Adequate | Four or five answers correct; the rest close but missing a specific detail (e.g. names m and h but not the power m³, or gives the gating ODE without the τ(V)/x_∞(V) form). |
| Not yet | Fewer than four correct, or answers copied near-verbatim rather than paraphrased, or the wrong gate is named as closing the Na+ current. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — **expected content; verify against the physical text before marking**, since exact wording/organization may vary by printing)

1. Expected: C_m represents the membrane's charge-storage capacity (µF/cm², from the lipid bilayer acting as a dielectric); g_L represents the ohmic conductance of channels open at rest (mS/cm²). The resulting equation is the passive (sub-threshold) isopotential membrane equation.
2. Expected: C_m dV/dt = −Σ_i g_i(V,t)(V − E_i) + I_e/A, summed over every ionic conductance i, each with its own (possibly voltage/time-dependent) conductance and its own reversal potential E_i.
3. Expected: dx/dt = α_x(V)(1−x) − β_x(V)x, equivalently written τ_x(V) dx/dt = x_∞(V) − x with x_∞(V) = α_x/(α_x+β_x) and τ_x(V) = 1/(α_x+β_x).
4. Expected: m and h belong to the Na+ conductance (g_Na = ḟg_Na·m³h — m³ is activation, h is inactivation); n belongs to the K+ (delayed rectifier) conductance (g_K = ḟg_K·n⁴).
5. Expected: h — the (slower) inactivation gate — closes and shuts off the Na+ current; it is a different gate from m, which is the (faster) activation gate that opened the conductance in the first place.
6. Expected: a single channel is modeled as a stochastic two- (or few-) state device switching between open and closed with voltage-dependent transition rates; the macroscopic g(t) is the ensemble/expected value — g_max times the mean fraction of the N channels open at time t, which is exactly the deterministic HH gating variable in the large-N limit.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Look for the equation that appears right after the leak-only case is generalized — it should have a summation symbol over channel types."
- Hint 2: "Compare the two equivalent forms of the gating-variable ODE — the α/β form and the x_∞/τ form — can you convert one into the other?"
- Hint 3: "Which gate's letter appears with a higher power (cubed) versus which appears linearly, and which section explicitly separates 'activation' from 'inactivation'?"
- Never: supply page numbers or quoted sentences (the source is you's physical copy, not something available to you); never answer on you's behalf — only confirm correctness after their own paraphrase, and confirm against the actual text, not memory, before marking any answer wrong.

## Piece 4 — Written explanation

**Type:** written explanation · **Time:** ~45–60 min

**Learning goals**
- Give a causal (kinetics-based) account of why an HH spike is all-or-none, without invoking "threshold" as an assumed model feature.
- Give a causal account of why a refractory period necessarily follows a spike.
- Ground both explanations in your own numbers from Piece 2.

**Task**

In 300–600 words, explain why a Hodgkin–Huxley spike is all-or-none and why a refractory period follows it, using only the kinetics of m (Na+ activation), h (Na+ inactivation), and n (K+ activation) — do not describe the neuron as having a hardcoded "threshold" that gets "reset". Cite at least two concrete numbers from your own Piece 2 run (e.g. your bisected threshold current, your spike-peak timing, or your measured refractory delays).

**Deliverable**

300–600 words of plain prose (not a bulleted list).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Explains all-or-none as the outcome of a positive feedback loop (depolarization → more m open → more Na+ current → more depolarization) racing against h's slower inactivation, so the response is binary rather than graded; explains the refractory period as the joint effect of h being slow to recover and n/K+ conductance still being elevated after the spike; cites ≥2 of your own Piece 2 numbers correctly; never falls back to "because it crosses threshold" as an unexplained primitive. |
| Adequate | Gets the qualitative mechanism right but leans on "threshold" language at least once without unpacking it in kinetic terms, or cites only one own-number, or omits one of the two feedback loops (positive Na+ or slow K+/h recovery). |
| Not yet | Explanation is circular (spikes because it crosses threshold; refractory because it's refractory) or doesn't reference your own simulation numbers at all. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Must contain, in substance:
1. All-or-none mechanism: near rest, a small depolarization opens a few Na+ channels (m rises), but this is outpaced by the restoring leak/K+ currents. Above a critical stimulus, enough Na+ channels open that their inward current depolarizes V faster than leak/K+ can restore it — a positive feedback loop (m rising → more depolarization → m rising further) that is essentially self-sustaining once triggered, which is why the response is binary rather than scaling smoothly with stimulus size. There is no separate "threshold" variable in the model — "threshold" just names the stimulus level at which this feedback loop overtakes the restoring currents, which is exactly why the bisection search in Piece 2 finds a sharp transition rather than a gradual one (their own ~6.9 µA/cm² number, and the jump from ~−50 mV to ~+40 mV, should appear here).
2. Termination of the rise: h (Na+ inactivation) closes on a slower timescale than m opens; once h has fallen enough, Na+ current shuts off even though V is still high (their own observation that h ≈0.35 and falling at the ~40 mV peak, minimum ≈0.08 shortly after, is the evidence).
3. Refractory period: two things must happen before the positive-feedback loop can re-trigger: (i) h must recover partway (it was driven toward 0, and recovers only as V returns toward rest), and (ii) n (K+ activation) is still elevated after the spike, so K+ conductance is still pulling V down/away from threshold, opposing any new depolarizing input. Together these explain both why no stimulus (however strong) can re-trigger a spike for the first several ms (h simply isn't available yet) — your own absolute-refractory number (~7–8 ms with a very strong pulse) — and why a same-strength stimulus needs longer to succeed again (~12–13 ms) because it must additionally overcome the still-elevated K+ conductance.
4. Should explicitly state that "threshold" and "refractory period" are descriptions of when these two competing conductances (fast Na+ positive feedback vs. slower Na+ inactivation / K+ negative feedback) cross over, not separate mechanisms bolted onto the model.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Walk through what m, h, V are doing together in the first millisecond after your suprathreshold pulse — is any one of them acting alone, or are they racing each other?"
- Hint 2: "If h fell exactly as fast as m rose, would you still get a spike? What does that tell you about what 'all-or-none' actually depends on?"
- Hint 3: "You have two numbers from Piece 2 that answer 'how long is refractory' — use them instead of the word 'refractory' unexplained."
- Never: write or rewrite any sentence of the explanation for you; only point out missing causal links, unexplained-primitive language ("crosses threshold"), or missing own-numbers, and let you redraft.

## Completion bar

Done when you can state, unprompted and without reaching for "threshold-and-reset" as a primitive: (1) why a spike is all-or-none — as the outcome of Na+ activation (m) racing its own slower inactivation (h), evidenced by their own bisected threshold and the sharp sub-/supra-threshold voltage gap in Piece 2; (2) why a refractory period follows — as the joint effect of h not yet recovered and K+ activation (n) still elevated, evidenced by their own absolute (~7–8 ms) and relative (~12–13 ms) refractory measurements; and can back both claims with the derived numbers from Piece 1 (τ_m, E_K) and the guided-reading answers from Piece 3 (which gate is which, and what the deterministic conductance is an average over).
