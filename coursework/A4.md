## Piece 1 — Refractory ceiling and facilitation arithmetic

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 40 min

**Learning goals**
- Derive a firing-rate ceiling directly from an absolute refractory period.
- Hand-compute a recursive facilitation update (Tsodyks–Markram-style utilization variable) across several spikes.
- State symbolically what happens to threshold-crossing overshoot under two reset conventions, and how it shifts next-spike timing.

**Task**

Work all three parts by hand (or in a text file), showing every intermediate number — no skipped steps.

1. **Refractory ceiling.** A neuron has an absolute refractory period of 2 ms: for 2 ms after every spike it cannot fire again no matter how much input arrives. What is the maximum possible firing rate, in Hz? Then, in 2–3 sentences: a *relative* refractory period (elevated threshold that decays back to baseline, rather than a hard block) doesn't give a single ceiling number — explain what it gives instead and why you can't answer "what's the max rate" with one number the way you could in the absolute case.

2. **Facilitation arithmetic.** A synapse's utilization variable starts at baseline U₀ = 0.2. Each presynaptic spike updates it as
   `u ← u + 0.2·(1 − u)`
   Three spikes arrive 10 ms apart. Treat decay back toward baseline between spikes as negligible (you'll test that assumption's cost in Piece 2). Compute `u` immediately after spike 1, after spike 2, and after spike 3. Show the arithmetic for each step — don't just state the final number. Then compute the ratio `u_after_3 / U₀` — this is roughly how much more transmitter this synapse releases per spike after 3 spikes than it did on the very first one.

3. **Reset and overshoot (symbolic).** A neuron's membrane reaches 1.2× threshold at the instant of a spike (threshold normalized to 1.0, so overshoot = 0.2). Under **reset-to-zero**, what happens to that 0.2 of overshoot? Under **subtract-threshold** (`V ← V − V_th`), what happens to it instead? If, after the reset, the membrane were then driven toward threshold at some constant rate `R` (units: threshold-fraction per ms), write the expression for how much *sooner* (in ms) the next spike occurs under subtract-threshold compared to reset-to-zero, in terms of the retained overshoot and `R`.

**Deliverable**

A single page (markdown, plain text, or a photo of handwritten work) with three clearly labeled sections (1, 2, 3), each showing the arithmetic, not just the final answer.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All three parts correct with arithmetic shown; part 1's relative-refractory explanation correctly identifies that it's a time-varying threshold, not a fixed ceiling; part 3 gives the correct symbolic expression for the timing shift (overshoot / R) with correct reasoning about which direction it goes. |
| Adequate | Parts 1 and 2 numerically correct; part 3 correctly identifies that subtract-threshold retains overshoot and reset-to-zero discards it, even if the symbolic timing expression is missing or off. |
| Not yet | Part 1 or Part 2's arithmetic is wrong, or part 3 has reset-to-zero and subtract-threshold's effects reversed or conflated. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

1. Max rate = 1 / 2 ms = 0.5 spikes/ms = **500 Hz**. Relative refractory: threshold is elevated right after a spike and decays back to baseline over some time constant, so the "ceiling" depends on how strong the input is (a very strong input can still fire during the relative-refractory window, just less easily) — there's no single Hz number, only a rate that depends on input drive, unlike the absolute case which is a hard cutoff independent of input strength.
2. u₀ = 0.2. u₁ = 0.2 + 0.2·(1−0.2) = 0.2 + 0.16 = **0.36**. u₂ = 0.36 + 0.2·(1−0.36) = 0.36 + 0.128 = **0.488**. u₃ = 0.488 + 0.2·(1−0.488) = 0.488 + 0.1024 = **0.5904**. Ratio u₃/U₀ = 0.5904/0.2 = **2.952** (≈3× baseline release).
3. Reset-to-zero: the 0.2 overshoot is discarded, V is set exactly to 0. Subtract-threshold: the 0.2 overshoot is carried forward, V is set to 0.2 (= 1.2 − 1.0). Timing shift: next spike needs to climb from 0 vs. from 0.2 to reach threshold 1.0, i.e. it needs 1.0/R ms vs. 0.8/R ms — subtract-threshold fires **0.2/R ms sooner**. Accept any algebraically equivalent form (e.g. Δt = overshoot/R).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Write out u after spike 1 before touching spike 2 — don't try to do all three in your head."
- Hint 2: "For part 3, ask what value V is set to under each rule, not what happens to the neuron's spike output."
- Hint 3: "Part 3's timing expression: if reset-to-zero has to climb the full 1.0 and subtract-threshold only has to climb 1.0 minus the overshoot, what's the difference in climb distance, and how does climb distance relate to time at rate R?"
- Never: state the numeric answers (500 Hz, 0.5904, 0.2/R) before the learner has submitted their own arithmetic. Never do the recursive update for them — point at which line of the recursion is next, not its value.

## Piece 2 — LIF under eight knob combinations

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 75 min

**Learning goals**
- Implement short-term plasticity as a transient resource variable that modulates transmission without touching a stored weight.
- Observe, empirically, how reset mode changes next-spike timing when overshoot is non-negligible.
- Observe how additive vs. saturating trace-stacking diverge under repeated spikes, and connect that to the size of a delayed three-factor weight update.

**Task**

Build a single-file simulation (PyTorch or numpy, no dataset downloads, under ~100 lines) of one LIF neuron driven by one synapse carrying a **fixed** presynaptic spike train. Use exactly these parameters (deviating changes what you're testing):

```
dt        = 1.0 ms       T = 200 ms        (200 steps)
tau_m     = 20 ms        V_th = 1.0        V starts at 0
tau_syn   = 5 ms         PSC amplitude scale A = 6.0
w         = 4.0          (fixed synaptic weight, the "memristor" value)
t_ref     = 2 ms absolute refractory (matches Piece 1 Q1's 500 Hz ceiling)

presynaptic spike train: t = 10, 20, 30, ..., 190 ms  (19 spikes, regular)

STP (Tsodyks-Markram style), used only when STP is "on":
  U = 0.2, tau_facil = 200 ms, tau_rec = 300 ms
  baseline before first spike: u = U = 0.2, x = 1.0
  at each presynaptic spike i, after decaying u toward U (tau_facil) and
  x toward 1.0 (tau_rec) over the elapsed inter-spike interval:
      u_new = u_pre + U*(1 - u_pre)          # facilitation jump
      efficacy_i = u_new * x_pre             # what actually reaches the neuron
      x_new = x_pre - u_new * x_pre          # depletion
  When STP is "off": efficacy_i = 1.0 for every spike (no dynamics at all).

Each presynaptic spike injects a current pulse of size A * w * efficacy_i
into an exponentially-decaying PSC (tau_syn), which drives the LIF membrane:
    V += dt * (-V + PSC) / tau_m
During the 2 ms refractory window after a spike, V does not integrate
(holds at its post-reset value); integration resumes the following step.
```

**Part A — reset × STP (4 conditions).** For each of {reset-to-zero, subtract-threshold} × {STP off, STP on}, run the simulation and record: total output spike count, and the first three output spike times.

**Part B — trace stacking (applied to Part A's 4 spike trains, 8 rows total).** For each of the 4 output spike trains from Part A, compute the peak value of an eligibility trace `e(t)` over the 200 ms window under two stacking rules, sampled every dt = 1 ms with decay time constant tau_e = 50 ms:
- **Additive:** each output spike does `e += 1.0` (after that step's decay is applied).
- **Saturating:** each output spike does `e = e + (1.0 − e) * 0.5` (a saturating jump toward ceiling 1.0 — the same "move a fraction of the remaining distance to a target" form as Piece 1 Q2's facilitation update).

Report peak `e` for both rules, for all 4 spike trains (8 numbers).

**Deliverable**

The script, plus a results table: 4 rows (reset mode × STP) × columns {spike count, first 3 spike times, peak_e_additive, peak_e_saturating}. Plus 3–4 sentences: (a) what STP on vs off does to spike count and why, (b) whether reset mode changed spike *timing*, spike *count*, or both, in your run, (c) whether additive stacking's peak ever looks unbounded-ish across a busier condition vs. a sparser one, and what that implies for a three-factor update that fires on whichever step the trace happens to be sampled.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All 8 numbers present and consistent with the run; STP on shows markedly fewer output spikes than STP off (mechanism correctly implemented, not just parameter-matched); explanation correctly separates "reset mode changed timing" from "STP changed count" without conflating the two knobs; notes that additive peak scales with spike count while saturating peak is bounded near 1.0 regardless of count. |
| Adequate | Simulation runs and produces a plausible 4-condition table (STP on has fewer spikes than STP off); trace-stacking part computed for at least 2 of the 4 conditions; explanation present but conflates reset-mode and STP effects somewhat. |
| Not yet | STP on/off produce near-identical spike counts (STP not actually implemented as a depleting/recovering resource), or trace stacking not attempted, or no results table. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — reference run, w=4.0, A=6.0; exact spike times are implementation-order-sensitive, accept ±1–2 ms and check the *pattern*, not bit-exact match)

| reset | STP | n spikes | first 3 spikes (ms) | peak_e additive | peak_e saturating |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| zero | off | 38 | 11, 15, 21 | 10.37 | 0.92 |
| zero | on | 4 | 13, 22, 32 | 2.68 | 0.79 |
| subtract | off | 53 | 11, 15, 21 | 14.35 | 0.94 |
| subtract | on | 4 | 13, 22, 31 | 2.95 | 0.81 |

Pattern to check for, in order of importance: (1) STP-on count ≪ STP-off count in both reset modes (≈4 vs. ≈40–55) — this is the load-bearing result, a near-equal count means STP isn't actually depleting. (2) Within STP-on, reset mode leaves the *count* the same (4 vs 4) but the *later* spike times diverge (32 vs 31, then 52 vs 44 in the reference run) — subtract-threshold's retained overshoot pulls later spikes earlier, cleanest to see here because count is controlled. (3) Within STP-off, both reset modes are near the refractory-limited firing ceiling from Piece 1 Q1, so subtract-threshold shows up as a higher *count* (53 vs 38) rather than a clean timing shift — that's expected, not a bug. (4) peak_e_additive scales with spike count (≈10–14 for the ~40–55-spike conditions vs ≈2.7–3.0 for the 4-spike conditions) while peak_e_saturating stays under 1.0 everywhere — this is the point of criterion 4.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "If STP-on and STP-off give almost the same spike count, check the order of operations in your TM update — are you using u_new (post-jump) or u_pre (pre-jump) to compute efficacy?"
- Hint 2: "Compare the STP-on row across the two reset modes first — count should match there, only timing should move. That's the cleanest place to see the reset-mode effect in isolation."
- Hint 3: "For the additive-vs-saturating comparison, look at the busiest condition (STP off) — that's where the two stacking rules should look most different."
- Never: hand over the reference table before the learner has run their own simulation and reported numbers. Never write or debug the learner's simulation code for them — point at which named parameter or update-order step to inspect. Never claim their numbers are "wrong" for being off by 1–2 ms from the key; check the pattern column, not exact digits.

## Piece 3 — D&A ch. 5 guided reading

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 45 min

**Learning goals**
- Ground the STP resource-variable formalism used in Pieces 1–2 in D&A's own treatment of release probability and short-term depression/facilitation.
- Locate D&A's account of refractoriness and reset convention in the integrate-and-fire section, and identify which convention D&A treats as the default.

**Task**

Read D&A ch. 5, the *Synaptic Conductances* section (release probability, short-term depression and facilitation) and the *Integrate-and-Fire Models* section (refractoriness, reset). Answer in writing:

1. D&A models short-term plasticity with variables for available/depleted resources and utilization (however they're notated in your edition). Write the correspondence explicitly: which of D&A's variables plays the role of this coursework's `x` (depletable resource), and which plays the role of `u` (utilization/facilitation)?
2. According to D&A, what determines whether a given synapse's spike train looks net-facilitating or net-depressing overall — what's the role of the baseline release probability in that outcome?
3. Does short-term depression, in D&A's account, modify the synaptic weight itself, or a separate resource variable? If that synapse then receives no presynaptic input for 500 ms, what happens to the resource variable — name the relevant recovery time constant and describe the direction it moves (toward what value)?
4. What refractory mechanisms does D&A describe for integrate-and-fire neurons, and how does D&A implement reset after a spike? Which reset convention does D&A treat as the default case, and does it discard or retain overshoot above threshold?
5. In 2–3 sentences: why is "what happens to overshoot at reset" a free modeling choice for an integrate-and-fire neuron, in a way it wouldn't be for a conductance-based model (e.g. Hodgkin–Huxley) where the spike shape and downswing are generated by the membrane dynamics themselves rather than imposed by a reset rule?

**Deliverable**

Five short written answers (2–5 sentences each), with a page/section reference into D&A for each.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All 5 answered with correct D&A page/section references; Q3 correctly states depression modifies a resource variable (not w) and correctly names the recovery time constant and its direction (recovers toward full/baseline availability); Q5 correctly identifies the reset rule as an approximation imposed on top of a simplified (non-conductance-based) spike mechanism. |
| Adequate | 4 of 5 answered correctly with references; Q3's core distinction (resource variable, not w) is present even if the time constant is mis-cited. |
| Not yet | Q3 says depression changes w, or fewer than 3 of 5 questions answered with a text reference. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

1. D&A's depletable-resource variable corresponds to this coursework's `x`; D&A's release-probability/facilitation variable corresponds to `u`. (Exact letters vary by edition/printing — accept any answer that correctly maps "resource that depletes and recovers" → x and "probability/utilization that jumps up per spike and decays back to baseline" → u.)
2. Net facilitating vs. depressing depends on baseline release probability: a synapse with low baseline release probability has more "room" for facilitation to dominate (each spike increases release probability multiplicatively, and depletion is limited because little was being released anyway); a synapse with high baseline release probability is already releasing most of its available resource per spike, so depletion dominates and the synapse looks net-depressing over a train.
3. Depression modifies a separate resource variable (x, the fraction of available/releasable resource), not the weight w. With no input for 500 ms, x recovers toward 1.0 (fully available) with the recovery time constant tau_rec (300 ms in this coursework's parameters) — i.e. exponential relaxation back to baseline, on the order of 1–2 tau_rec being most of the way there (500 ms ≈ 1.67·tau_rec).
4. D&A describes an absolute refractory period (brief, spikes are impossible) and, in some treatments, a relative refractory period via a time-varying threshold. Reset in the basic integrate-and-fire model is implemented by returning V to a fixed reset potential immediately after threshold crossing; D&A's default LIF treatment resets to a fixed reset value (reset-to-zero-style), discarding whatever overshoot occurred above threshold, since the model has no mechanism to represent the overshoot itself (voltage is treated as undefined during the spike).
5. In a conductance-based (Hodgkin–Huxley-style) model, the spike upswing and downswing are themselves the product of the voltage-gated conductance dynamics — nothing needs to be "reset," the equations naturally produce the falling phase and hyperpolarization. An integrate-and-fire model has no spike-generating dynamics at all; the spike is a threshold-crossing event handled entirely by an external rule, so what happens to V at that instant (discard the overshoot vs. keep it) is not derived from any underlying physical process in the model — it's a free choice the modeler makes, which is exactly why reset-to-zero and subtract-threshold are both "valid" LIF variants that make different predictions.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Check the subsection right after where D&A introduces release probability P_release — the depression/facilitation variables are usually introduced together, right there."
- Hint 2: "For Q3, look specifically for the phrase D&A uses for what the depression variable represents — is it described in units of conductance/weight, or in units of 'fraction available'?"
- Hint 3: "For Q4, D&A's basic integrate-and-fire section states the reset rule as one line right after the threshold condition — that line is your answer."
- Never: quote D&A's text verbatim at length in place of the learner's own answer. Never supply the page number before the learner has looked — confirm or correct a page number they offer, don't hand one over cold.

## Piece 4 — Written defence: does the memristor synapse need a second variable?

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 45 min

**Learning goals**
- Apply the STP-vs-w distinction from Pieces 1–3 to a concrete architectural decision in the learner's own project.
- Argue a position with reference to specific downstream consequences (step 02b's persisted per-synapse weights, step 04's stress/pruning), not in the abstract.

**Task**

Write 300–600 words answering: *Should the project's virtual-memristor synapse — which currently models one persistent weight `w` per synapse, updated only by local learning rules — be extended with a second, transient resource variable (an `x`/`u`-style pair, decaying independently of `w` over ~100s-of-ms) to implement short-term plasticity? Or should STP be left out of the design, keeping the synapse single-weight?*

Address, specifically:
- What would the second variable have to persist across (a single tick, an episode, forever) versus what `w` persists across in the current design — and whether the existing per-synapse persistence machinery (built for step 02b's online learning) generalizes to a variable with a much shorter, self-resetting timescale.
- What happens if STP is *not* modeled and its effect is left to be absorbed into `w` by the existing local learning rule instead — is that a loss of a real distinct mechanism, or a harmless simplification, and what specific behavior (if any) would the network fail to reproduce as a result?
- Step 04's stress/pruning mechanism prunes connections with "near-zero weights for sustained periods." If a synapse's *transmission* is being suppressed by short-term depression (a transient x near 0) while its *stored weight* w is fine, does pruning need to be able to tell those two apart? What breaks if it can't?

**Deliverable**

A single markdown or text file, 300–600 words, taking and defending one position (not a both-sides summary).

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Takes an explicit position; correctly distinguishes what persists across a tick/episode/lifetime for w vs. a hypothetical STP variable; engages concretely with the step 04 pruning conflict (transient depression vs. genuinely near-zero w) and states what breaks if the two aren't distinguished; within 300–600 words. |
| Adequate | Takes a position and addresses at least 2 of the 3 bullet points concretely (not just restating them); some hand-waving on the pruning interaction is acceptable. |
| Not yet | No clear position taken, or the answer never engages with step 04's pruning rule at all, or it's a generic "STP is biologically realistic so it should be included" essay with no reference to this project's specific mechanisms. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only — this is a judged position, not a single correct answer; use this as the standard of what "engaging concretely" looks like, not as a required conclusion)

- A well-argued **"add it"** position: notes that `w` persists indefinitely (across ticks, episodes, and — per step 02b — across saved checkpoints), while an STP resource variable would need to reset toward baseline within the episode/rollout timescale and should almost certainly *not* be checkpointed at all (checkpointing a transient variable would freeze a mid-decay snapshot as if it were a steady state). Notes that omitting STP means any input-history-dependent transmission scaling (e.g. a burst of presynaptic activity transiently amplifying or damping its own influence) has to be learned into `w` itself by the local rule, which conflates a fast reversible effect with a slow structural one — and that this specifically undermines step 04, because a synapse whose `w` looks fine but is transiently suppressed by STP could be misread by the stress/pruning rule as "near-zero weight, sustained" if pruning reads transmitted current rather than `w` directly. Concludes pruning must key off `w` (or an EMA of it), never off instantaneous post-STP transmission, and that this is exactly why the two variables must be kept structurally separate rather than merged into one.
- A well-argued **"leave it out for now"** position: notes CartPole (current step 02b focus) has no clear behavioral signature that requires STP — no repeated-burst input pattern in the current task where facilitation/depression would visibly change outcomes — so the added state (and added checkpoint/reset-timing complexity) isn't paying for itself yet; recommends deferring until step 03 (spiking neurons) where fast, repeated pre-synaptic bursts are structurally likely to occur and STP's effect becomes observable, and flags the step 04 pruning conflict as a reason to design the *interface* (pruning reads w, not transmitted current) now even before implementing STP itself.
- Either position is acceptable; a paper that ducks the checkpoint-timescale question or never mentions the pruning conflict has not engaged with the task regardless of which side it takes.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Start from what gets saved in a step 02b checkpoint today (best.pkl / norm_stats.pt) — would a transient STP variable belong in that file?"
- Hint 2: "Reread step 04's pruning rule as quoted in the task: it prunes on sustained near-zero *weight*. Where would a transient x-variable have to sit relative to that rule for pruning to still work correctly?"
- Hint 3: "Pick a side in your first sentence — don't open with 'there are tradeoffs on both sides.'"
- Never: write the defence's position or draft sentences for the learner. Never grade a "both sides, no conclusion" essay as Adequate or higher even if every bullet point is technically touched on.
