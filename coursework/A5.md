# A5 Coursework — State of the field: why SNNs, where they stand vs ANNs

**Lesson spec:** `lessons.md` → A5. **Total time:** ~4-5 hours (two sittings). **Prerequisites:** A1-A4 (delays, attractors, oscillations, short-term plasticity), and F4 for the information-per-spike argument.

## Running order

Consume, then apply, then refine:

**audio overview → guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written defence (Piece 4)**

Pieces are numbered by type, not by sequence. The reading front-loads the concepts, the derivation makes them exact, the simulation is where they stick and get refined, and the defence is where you find out whether they did.

## Coverage map

| Piece | Type | Lesson acceptance criteria addressed |

| 1 — Energy budget derivation | derivation | AC1 (derive per-event energy; state the workload conditions for a fair GPU comparison) |

## Piece 1 — The brain's energy-per-synaptic-event budget

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** 45-60 min

**Learning goals**
- Turn population-level numbers (total power, synapse count, mean firing rate) into a per-event energy figure using nothing but unit arithmetic.
- Identify which of those inputs is doing the real work in the estimate and which is a loose bound.
- State, precisely, the conditions under which "energy per synaptic event" is the right unit to put next to a GPU's "energy per MAC" — and when it silently compares two different things.

**Task**

Using these inputs — brain power draw ≈ 20 W, synapse count ≈ 10^14–10^15, mean firing rate ≈ 1–5 Hz — do the following on paper (typed or scanned, show every step):

1. Estimate total synaptic events per second across the whole brain. State the assumption you're making about how "firing rate" (a per-neuron quantity) turns into "events per second at a synapse" (hint: what do you need to know about fan-out per neuron, and does it cancel out if you work in total synapses rather than per-neuron?).
2. Divide power by events/second to get energy per synaptic event, in joules. Convert to femtojoules (fJ) or picojoules (pJ), whichever is the more natural unit for the result.
3. Redo steps 1-2 at both ends of the synapse-count range (10^14 and 10^15) and both ends of the firing-rate range (1 Hz and 5 Hz). Report the resulting range, not a single number.
4. Find (do not just assert) a modern digital MAC's energy cost. Distinguish **compute-only** cost (the multiply-add itself, on-chip) from **memory-inclusive** cost (fetching the operands from SRAM vs. DRAM/HBM). Cite where each number came from — a datasheet, a paper, a vendor claim — and note which one is closest in spirit to "one synaptic event."
5. State explicitly: what has to be true of an artificial workload before your per-event brain figure is a fair number to set against a GPU's per-MAC figure? Cover at least: (a) whether the ANN workload is itself sparse/event-driven or dense, (b) whether the hardware running it actually skips work for inactive units or evaluates everything every cycle regardless, (c) whether you're comparing compute-only or memory-inclusive energy on the artificial side.

**Deliverable**

A worked derivation (1-2 pages, real arithmetic shown, not just final numbers) ending in: a stated energy-per-synaptic-event range, a stated GPU per-MAC range with its source, the ratio between them, and a paragraph answering point 5.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Correct order-of-magnitude range from real arithmetic (steps shown); correctly identifies that fan-out cancels when working in total synapse count rather than per-neuron; cites a real, checkable source for the GPU MAC figure and correctly separates compute-only from memory-inclusive; the "what has to be true" paragraph names sparsity of the *workload* and skip-on-inactivity of the *hardware* as separate, both-required conditions. |
| Adequate | Correct order of magnitude reached, arithmetic shown, but one of: the fan-out/cancellation reasoning is hand-waved, the GPU figure is asserted without a checkable source, or the "what has to be true" answer names only one of the two required conditions. |
ns Not yet | Final number given without shown arithmetic; range collapsed to a single point without acknowledging the two input ranges; GPU comparison number invented with no source; no engagement with when the comparison is unfair. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Order-of-magnitude target (perishable — grade the reasoning path, not the digit): events/sec ≈ N_synapses × f ≈ (10^14 to 10^15) × (1 to 5) ≈ 10^14 to 5×10^15 events/s. Energy/event = 20 W / that range ≈ 4 fJ to 2×10^2 fJ, i.e. **roughly 1-100 fJ per synaptic event, order of magnitude 10^-14 to 10^-15 J**. Accept anything in that band with shown work; the point of the wide range is that the input ranges are wide, not that you should force a single figure.

GPU reference range (as of ~2024-2025 public figures, itself perishable): a low-precision (8-bit int) on-chip MAC compute-only is roughly 0.1-1 pJ (100-1000 fJ); with local SRAM register/operand access, roughly 1-5 pJ; a MAC whose operands must come from DRAM/HBM is commonly cited around 100s of pJ to ~1 nJ (this traces to the widely-cited Horowitz 2014 ISSCC "Computing's Energy Problem" figures — a 32-bit DRAM read ≈ 640 pJ vs. a 32-bit int add ≈ 0.1 pJ, roughly 4 orders of magnitude apart). So the brain's per-event figure (~1-100 fJ) sits below compute-only silicon MACs by roughly 1-3 orders of magnitude, and below memory-inclusive MACs by 3-5 orders of magnitude. Reject any submission that treats these as one number rather than acknowledging compute-only vs. memory-inclusive are different comparisons.

Required "what has to be true" content: (1) the artificial workload must itself be spatiotemporally sparse — most units inactive at most timesteps — otherwise you are comparing an event-driven system's best case against a dense system's worst case; (2) the hardware must actually be event-driven/asynchronous or gated (skip compute for a non-firing unit), because a clocked dense-matmul GPU pays for every entry whether or not it "would have been a zero"; (3) fair comparison should hold the memory-access assumption constant on both sides — comparing brain-event energy (which is essentially free of a DRAM-style fetch, since the "weight" is local to the synapse) against a GPU MAC that includes an HBM fetch overstates the brain's advantage, since it's really measuring "local analog memristor-like storage" vs "von Neumann fetch," not "spiking vs. dense" per se.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "You have two independent ranges as inputs (synapse count, firing rate) — what does that force about your output?"
- Hint 2: "You need a real number for a GPU MAC's energy cost from somewhere outside this document — where would you look, and how would you tell a compute-only figure from a memory-inclusive one?"
- Hint 3: "Re-read acceptance criterion 1's second half: 'what has to be true of a workload' — that's asking about the *artificial* system's sparsity and the *hardware's* ability to exploit it, not about the brain."
- Never: supply the GPU MAC figure or its source directly. Never do the unit-conversion arithmetic for you. Never confirm or deny whether a specific final number is "right" beyond stating whether it falls in the accepted order-of-magnitude band.

## Piece 2 — Sparsity/event-count simulator with an energy cost model

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** 60-90 min

**Learning goals**
- Build a minimal, self-contained comparison between "dense synchronous evaluation" and "sparse event-driven evaluation" of the same computation.
- Apply an explicit, stated energy cost model (not a black box) to both regimes and read off where the crossover is.
- Connect the result numerically to acceptance criterion 5: what property a workload needs before event-driven wins.

**Task**

Write a single Python file (PyTorch or plain numpy, no dataset downloads — synthetic data only), under ~100 lines, that:

1. Defines a small feedforward network (e.g. 784→128→10 sized, weights random-initialized — this is about counting operations, not training).
2. Generates a batch of **synthetic** input patterns without downloading anything — e.g. `torch.rand` thresholded into a sparse binary image, or a hand-built pattern (a few "on" pixels), so you control the input sparsity directly.
3. Runs the network in two modes on the same inputs:
   - **Dense mode:** one ordinary forward pass; count total MACs (sum of `in_features × out_features` per layer, independent of input values).
   - **Event mode:** encode each input unit as a Poisson spike train over `T` timesteps at a rate proportional to its activation (cap the max rate, e.g. 50-100 Hz equivalent); at each timestep, only units that spike contribute — count total **synaptic events** actually delivered (spikes × fan-out reached), not just spikes.
4. Applies a stated cost model: `energy_dense = MACs × E_mac`, `energy_event = synaptic_events × E_event`, using constants you justify with a one-line comment citing where they came from (can reuse the ranges from Piece 1, or state your own — just be explicit).
5. Sweeps one variable — input sparsity (fraction of "on" pixels) or `T` (timesteps) — over a small range and prints/plots total energy for both modes side by side, so the crossover point (if any, within the swept range) is visible in the output.

**Deliverable**

The script, plus a 5-10 line written note: at what sparsity level (or T) does event mode stop being cheaper than dense mode, given your chosen constants, and does that threshold move if you change `E_event`/`E_mac` by 10x?

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Script runs standalone with no downloads, under ~100 lines; dense MAC count is input-independent (correctly reflects that dense hardware pays regardless of value); event count correctly scales with both spike count and fan-out (not just spike count); cost-model constants are stated and justified, not silently hardcoded; sweep clearly shows a crossover or explains why none exists in range; written note correctly reasons about the sensitivity to the E_event:E_mac ratio. |
| Adequate | Script works and produces a sensible energy comparison, but one of: event count conflates "spikes" with "synaptic events" (misses the fan-out multiplication), cost constants are unexplained magic numbers, or the sweep doesn't clearly locate/discuss a crossover. |
| Not yet | Script requires a downloaded dataset or external data file; dense/event energy computed without a stated cost model (e.g. just spike count reported as "energy"); no sweep, single-point comparison only; script exceeds scope significantly (full training loop, etc. — this is a counting exercise, not a training exercise). |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

There is no single correct energy figure — grade the mechanism. Expected qualitative shape: dense energy is constant across the input-sparsity sweep (it doesn't care about input values). Event energy rises roughly linearly with (sparsity × T × mean rate). For typical assumed ratios (E_mac in the 100s of fJ to low pJ compute-only range, E_event in the 1-100 fJ range from Piece 1 — i.e. E_mac/E_event somewhere between ~10x and ~1000x), and T in the 10-100 timestep range typical of rate-coded SNN simulations, the crossover where event-mode energy exceeds one dense pass typically lands somewhere in the low-to-mid single-digit percent mean population activity — this figure is illustrative and perishable; do not penalize a different crossover point if you's stated constants and T are different and the arithmetic connecting them is consistent. What must hold regardless of constants: (a) as sparsity or T increases, event-mode energy should approach or cross dense-mode energy from below — if a submission shows event mode always cheaper regardless of how dense the sweep gets, that's a bug (event mode has no ceiling in this cost model since it's not being compared per-inference against a *fixed* dense budget correctly, or T isn't actually being multiplied in); (b) the crossover threshold should shift by roughly the same factor as a 10x change in E_event:E_mac ratio (this is the sensitivity check in the written note).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Dense MACs come from layer shapes alone — should the input values change that count at all?"
- Hint 2: "A 'synaptic event' isn't the same as a spike — one spiking neuron with 128 outgoing weights produces how many synaptic events?"
- Hint 3: "If your energy-vs-sparsity plot shows event mode winning everywhere no matter how dense you sweep, check whether T (or rate) is actually being multiplied into your event count."
- Never: write or debug you's cost-model code for them. Never supply a "correct" crossover number as ground truth beyond the qualitative shape described above. Never let a submission pass that downloads MNIST/any real dataset — flag it as scope violation regardless of code quality.

## Piece 3 — Neuromorphic hardware: find your own source, then interrogate it

**Type:** guided reading · **Time:** 75-90 min

**Learning goals**
- Locate a credible, openly-available current source (survey or vendor technical document) on neuromorphic hardware without institutional-access papers.
- Extract the recurring axes hardware differs on from primary/vendor material rather than from a memorized list.
- Identify what a vendor or survey claim does *not* tell you — sparsity assumptions behind an energy number, benchmark methodology, what's on-chip vs. off-chip.

**Task**

This lesson has no assigned reading — Dayan & Abbott predates this hardware entirely, and the two natural papers (Attwell & Laughlin 2001; Roy, Jaiswal & Panda 2019) need institutional access you don't have. Find your own source.

1. Find **one current, openly-available** source that is either (a) a survey/review paper on neuromorphic computing (arXiv is fine — search terms like "neuromorphic computing survey," "spiking neural network hardware review"), or (b) a vendor technical brief/whitepaper for a specific chip (Intel Loihi 2, SpiNNaker2, IBM NorthPole, a memristor-crossbar research group's paper). Note the publication/last-update date — prefer 2022 or later given how fast this field moves.
2. Read enough of it to answer the questions below. You do not need to read it cover to cover.
3. Answer, using your source and citing where in it (section/page/figure) each answer comes from:
   a. What are the recurring axes this source uses (explicitly or implicitly) to differentiate neuromorphic hardware — at minimum, check for: digital vs. analog, on-chip learning vs. inference-only, fixed topology vs. flexible/reconfigurable, and weight/activation precision.
   b. Pick **two** chips or architectures your source discusses (or that you look up briefly alongside it) and fill in those same axes for each, side by side.
   c. Of the axes you found, which ones actually constrain *this project's* design (purely local learning rules, virtual-memristor synapses, graph topology rather than fixed layers)? Which are irrelevant to this project even though they matter for other use cases?
   d. Find where your source states or implies an energy-per-operation number. What sparsity or activity-rate assumption is that number conditioned on — is it stated, or do you have to infer it's a best case? If your source reports a benchmark accuracy or throughput number, what methodology detail (dataset, task, whether it's simulated vs. measured on real silicon, whether comparison hardware is contemporaneous) would you need to check before trusting it?
4. State your source's title, authors/vendor, and URL or DOI at the top of your answer.

**Deliverable**

A written answer (½-1 page) to 3a-3d, with the source cited at the top, and inline pointers (section/page/figure) for each claim you pull from it.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Source is genuinely openly accessible (grader can reach it with the given link, no paywall) and reasonably current; all four axes correctly identified and correctly attributed to specific chips with source pointers; correctly separates "matters for this project" from "matters in general" with a concrete reason tied to the project's local-learning/memristor/graph-topology constraints; explicitly identifies what the energy or benchmark number is silent about (sparsity assumption, dataset, simulated-vs-measured) rather than repeating it uncritically. |
| Adequate | Source is accessible and used, axes mostly identified correctly, but the project-relevance filter (3c) is superficial (e.g. "all of these matter") or the critique of the energy/benchmark number (3d) restates the number without probing its conditions. |
| Not yet | Source is paywalled/inaccessible to the grader, is not actually about neuromorphic hardware, or answers are given without any source pointers (i.e., recalled from general knowledge rather than extracted from the found source) — this defeats the point of the exercise, which is the search-and-extract skill, not the facts. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

There is no fixed source, so there is no fixed set of "correct" chip facts — this is graded on process, not on matching a table. The grader AI should independently sanity-check: (i) the link resolves and is not paywalled, (ii) the source is actually about neuromorphic hardware and is dated reasonably recently, (iii) the four claimed axes are genuinely present in or reasonably inferable from that source rather than invented, (iv) the two named chips are real and the axis values attributed to them are plausible (a grader with general knowledge as of its training should recognize gross errors — e.g. claiming Loihi is analog, or claiming TrueNorth supports on-chip learning, would be factually wrong regardless of source and should be flagged).

Illustrative (perishable, not to be handed to you) reference points a grader can use to sanity-check plausibility, not to grade against directly: Loihi 2 — digital, asynchronous, supports on-chip learning via programmable microcode learning rules, reconfigurable topology, low-bit weights. SpiNNaker2 — digital, many-core ARM-based, software-defined neuron models (not fixed in hardware), supports on-chip learning via software, built for large-scale simulation with numerical flexibility over energy-per-op minimalism. TrueNorth — digital, inference-only (no on-chip learning; weights are set/trained offline and loaded), fixed crossbar topology per core, 1-bit synapses. NorthPole — digital, inference-only, near-memory (not fully in-memory) architecture optimized for low-precision dense inference, notably *not* spiking/event-driven in the same sense as the others — a useful contrast case if a learner picks it. Memristor crossbars (research-stage) — analog, in-memory compute (weight = physical device state, closest in spirit to this project's "virtual memristor" framing), typically inference-focused or with limited/researchy on-chip learning, precision limited by device variability/drift rather than a design choice. If you's source disagrees with these on a specific number, that's fine — sources differ and the field moves; flag only outright category errors (digital/analog, learning/inference-only).

Project-relevance expected reasoning: local learning rule and virtual-memristor framing are most directly constrained by the on-chip-learning axis and the analog-vs-digital / in-memory-vs-von-Neumann axis (a memristor-style synapse is meaningful chiefly on hardware where weight = local physical/analog state, not a fetched digital value). Graph topology (vs. fixed layers) is constrained by the topology-flexibility axis — a chip with fixed crossbar routing per core penalizes irregular/sparse-long-range graph structures differently than a fully reconfigurable router-based chip. Weight precision matters less centrally to this project's premises than to raw accuracy work, since the project isn't chasing ImageNet-scale accuracy parity.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Try arXiv directly with a search like 'neuromorphic hardware survey 2024' or 2025 rather than searching for the named-but-inaccessible papers — you're not trying to find Roy et al., you're trying to find something that covers similar ground and is actually reachable."
- Hint 2: "A vendor technical brief for a real chip (search '<chip name> technical brief' or '<chip name> whitepaper') is a valid source under this exercise — it doesn't have to be an academic survey."
- Hint 3: "For 3d — every energy-per-operation number you'll find is a best case under some assumption. Look for the word 'sparsity,' 'activity,' or 'utilization' near the number, or the benchmark's task description, and if it's not there, say so explicitly rather than treating the number as unconditional."
- Never: name a specific source for you to use — the search is the point. Never supply the illustrative chip facts above to you directly, even as a check on their work — only use them internally to sanity-check plausibility and flag outright errors. Never accept an answer with no source pointers, regardless of how correct the content sounds.

## Piece 4 — Written defence: make (or refuse) the efficiency case

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** 45-60 min

**Learning goals**
- Synthesize the derivation, simulation, and reading into a single argument addressed to a specific skeptical audience.
- State the honest accuracy/tooling gap without it undermining the parts of the efficiency argument that do hold.
- Distinguish "SNNs are efficient" (unsupported slogan) from the narrower, defensible claim this lesson supports.

**Task**

Write 300-600 words addressed to a skeptical examiner who opens with: *"Every efficiency number for spiking hardware I've seen assumes the workload is sparse and the comparison hardware is a GPU running dense FP32 at low utilization. Isn't this just a favorable-conditions argument dressed up as a hardware argument? And even if the energy case holds, your accuracy is worse and your tooling is years behind PyTorch — why should anyone care?"*

Your answer must:
1. State the energy claim you can actually defend (using your Piece 1 figures) and the conditions it depends on — not the unconditional "spikes are efficient" slogan.
2. Concede the accuracy and tooling gap honestly (from Piece 3), including what the ANN-to-SNN conversion criticism is and why a conversion-based accuracy number is a weaker claim than it looks.
3. Give at least one concrete example (with a number or a stated order of magnitude, from your simulation or reading) of a task where event-driven hardware plausibly wins on latency or power, and one where it plainly does not — using acceptance criterion 5's property (what the task needs to have) explicitly.
4. Explain, briefly, why *this project* chooses purely local learning and virtual-memristor synapses anyway — targeting hardware compatibility, not accuracy parity with backprop-trained ANNs on the Step 1-2 benchmarks (MNIST/FashionMNIST/CIFAR-10).

**Deliverable**

300-600 words of prose (not bullet points — this is a defence, it should read as an argument), addressed directly to the examiner's challenge.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | States a conditional, defensible energy claim (references real figures from Piece 1) rather than a slogan; explicitly names the conditions (workload sparsity, hardware's ability to exploit it) as the examiner demanded; honestly states the accuracy/tooling gap and correctly explains why conversion-based accuracy hides training cost/native-training difficulty; gives a concrete win-case and lose-case tied to the sparsity/latency property, not generic examples; ties the project's design choices to hardware-targeting rather than claiming an accuracy or efficiency win the project hasn't earned. All within 300-600 words. |
| Adequate | Covers most of the four required elements but at least one is asserted rather than argued (e.g. states the gap exists without explaining the conversion criticism; gives a win-case example with no number or order of magnitude attached). |
| Not yet | Restates "SNNs are efficient" as an unconditional claim; ignores the accuracy/tooling gap or handwaves past it; no concrete win/lose task example; doesn't connect back to why *this project specifically* makes its design choices; well outside the word count in either direction. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

The defensible claim: brain-scale per-synaptic-event energy is roughly 1-100 fJ (Piece 1), against a GPU MAC that is roughly 100-1000 fJ compute-only or 100s of pJ to ~1 nJ memory-inclusive — a real gap, but conditional on (a) the artificial workload being similarly sparse in space and time, and (b) the hardware being able to skip computation for inactive units rather than evaluating a dense array every clock regardless of value. Absent both, the comparison doesn't transfer, and a dense, high-utilization, batched GPU workload can out-efficiency a naively-implemented spiking equivalent (this is exactly what Piece 2's simulator should have shown at high sparsity/T).

Conversion criticism: ANN-to-SNN conversion trains a standard backprop ANN, then maps its weights/activations onto a spiking substrate (rate-coding the ReLU activations as firing rates, threshold-balancing, etc.). The reported accuracy is close to the source ANN's, but that accuracy was earned by the *ANN's* training procedure (backprop, global loss, often many epochs on GPU), not by anything native to spiking dynamics or local learning — so it hides the fact that "getting an SNN to that accuracy" required abandoning the constraints (locality, no global loss) that motivated using spikes in the first place. It also typically needs a longer inference window (more timesteps = more events = more energy) to approximate the ANN's real-valued activations well, which can erase some or all of the claimed energy advantage. A large gap between natively-trained SNNs (local rules, no conversion) and ANNs, by contrast, is informative about where the real difficulty sits: if a conversion recovers ANN-level accuracy easily but native training does not, the difficulty is concentrated in the learning rule / credit assignment problem, not in the neuron model or hardware.

Win-case / lose-case: win-case should invoke genuine spatiotemporal sparsity plus latency sensitivity — event cameras (DVS-style sensors producing ~10^3-10^5 events/sec from a scene rather than dense frames at fixed frame rate; most pixels silent most of the time) or always-on low-duty-cycle sensing/keyword-spotting (mostly-silent input, wake only on activity, power budget in the µW-mW range where GPU-style dense inference isn't even in consideration). Lose-case: dense, high-activity, already-batched workloads — e.g. large-batch offline image classification/training on saturated GPUs, or any task where the input itself is naturally dense (no meaningful "silence" between events) — the sparsity assumption event-driven hardware depends on simply doesn't hold, so there's no advantage to exploit and the overhead of spike encoding/timesteps can make it worse.

Project tie-in: the project's choice of purely local learning rules and virtual-memristor synapses is a hardware-compatibility bet (targets neuromorphic/in-memory deployment where weight-as-local-physical-state and no-global-loss are required), explicitly not a claim that this approach will match or beat backprop-trained ANN accuracy on MNIST/FashionMNIST/CIFAR-10 in steps 1-2 — those benchmarks are being used as reproduction targets/sanity checks on the architecture, not as an accuracy contest against ANN baselines.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "The examiner's challenge has four parts — check your draft covers all four before worrying about polish."
- Hint 2: "For the conversion criticism: what did the ANN's accuracy actually cost to obtain, and does the SNN inherit that cost or avoid it?"
- Hint 3: "Your win-case example needs a number or order-of-magnitude attached — 'sensors' isn't specific enough, how many events per second, how many µW?"
- Never: write or rewrite sentences of the defence for you. Never accept a submission that never states an actual condition-bearing energy claim (i.e. that only asserts "it depends" without saying what it depends on). Never let word count alone pass or fail a submission — check content against the four required elements first, flag word count separately.

## Completion bar

A5 is passed when all four pieces meet at least "Adequate," AND you can, without consulting their notes: (1) reproduce the per-event energy order-of-magnitude arithmetic from Piece 1 on request, using different input numbers than the ones given here; (2) name two hardware differentiation axes and say which one actually constrains this project's design, with a reason; (3) explain in one or two sentences why a conversion-based SNN accuracy number is a weaker claim than a native-training number; (4) state, without hedging into the "SNNs are just efficient" slogan, the specific condition under which the energy argument holds. Passing opens track C.
