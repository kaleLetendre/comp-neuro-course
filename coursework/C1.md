## Piece 1 — The 3-node chain, by hand

**Type:** derivation · **Time:** ~75 min

**Learning goals**
- Write a layered network's predictive-coding energy as an explicit sum of squared prediction errors.
- Differentiate that energy by hand with respect to a single node's activity.
- Identify, term by term, which parts of the energy a given node's gradient touches and which it doesn't.
- State precisely what "local" means for that gradient, in terms of quantities a real neuron/synapse could access.

**Task**

Consider the 3-node chain x₀ → x₁ → x₂. x₁ predicts... no — x₁ is predicted by x₀, and x₁ in turn predicts x₂. Concretely:

- x₁ is predicted from x₀ by weight w: prediction x̂₁ = w·x₀
- x₂ is predicted from x₁ by weight v: prediction x̂₂ = v·x₁
- Fixed numbers: x₀ = 1, w = 2, v = 0.5, x₂ = 0.9. x₁ = 1.5 for the pointwise questions below.

Work through, showing every step:

1. Define the two local prediction errors e₁ = x₁ − x̂₁ and e₂ = x₂ − x̂₂, and write the full energy E = ½e₁² + ½e₂² as an explicit function of x₀, w, x₁, v, x₂ (no numbers yet).
2. Plug in the numbers above and compute e₁ and e₂ at x₁ = 1.5. Compute E at that point.
3. Take ∂E/∂x₁ by hand — differentiate ½e₁² and ½e₂² separately with respect to x₁, using the chain rule, then add. State explicitly which of the two terms contribute a nonzero derivative and why (for this specific 3-node chain, check both — don't assume).
4. Evaluate ∂E/∂x₁ numerically at x₁ = 1.5.
5. Name the three quantities your final expression for ∂E/∂x₁ depends on. For each one, say which physical component (a specific neuron's error output, or a specific synapse's stored weight) it corresponds to, and explain why computing it never requires reading a value from any node other than x₁'s two immediate neighbours.
6. Set ∂E/∂x₁ = 0 and solve for the value x₁* at which x₁ would stop moving under gradient descent (keep x₀, x₂, w, v fixed at the values above). State in one sentence what "fixed point" means here in terms of ∂E/∂x.

**Deliverable**

A written derivation (PDF, markdown, or legible photographed handwriting) covering steps 1-6 above, with every algebraic step shown — not just final answers. No formula should appear without the line before it that produced it.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All six steps correct with intermediate algebra shown; step 5's locality argument is stated in terms of physical quantities (error-neuron outputs, synaptic weights), not just "it only has x1 in it"; step 6 fixed point correctly solved and interpreted as zero net force from E. |
| Adequate | Steps 1-4 correct with correct numbers; step 5 identifies the three quantities but locality argument is vague ("these are nearby" without saying why nothing further is needed); step 6 attempted, possibly with an algebra slip that's otherwise structurally sound. |
| Not yet | E is written with a sign error, a missing factor of ½, or e1/e2 swapped; ∂E/∂x1 omits one of the two terms without justification; or step 5 lists quantities that include x0 or x2 directly rather than e1/e2/v. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Setup: x̂₁ = w·x₀ = 2·1 = 2, so e₁ = x₁ − 2. x̂₂ = v·x₁ = 0.5·x₁, so e₂ = x₂ − 0.5·x₁ = 0.9 − 0.5·x₁.

1. E(x₁) = ½(x₁ − w·x₀)² + ½(x₂ − v·x₁)² = ½(x₁ − 2)² + ½(0.9 − 0.5·x₁)²

2. At x₁ = 1.5: e₁ = 1.5 − 2 = **−0.5**. e₂ = 0.9 − 0.5·(1.5) = 0.9 − 0.75 = **0.15**.
   E = ½(−0.5)² + ½(0.15)² = ½(0.25) + ½(0.0225) = 0.125 + 0.01125 = **0.13625**.

3. ∂/∂x₁ [½e₁²] = e₁ · ∂e₁/∂x₁ = e₁ · (1) = e₁, since e₁ = x₁ − 2 has slope 1 in x₁.
   ∂/∂x₁ [½e₂²] = e₂ · ∂e₂/∂x₁ = e₂ · (−0.5) = −v·e₂, since e₂ = x₂ − v·x₁ has slope −v in x₁.
   ∂E/∂x₁ = e₁ − v·e₂.
   Both terms contribute here: x₁ appears directly inside e₁ (as the thing being predicted) and inside e₂ (as the input to the prediction of x₂). In this minimal 3-node chain there is no third error term, so there is nothing that trivially drops out — the exercise is to notice that if the chain were longer (e.g. a node x₋₁ feeding x₀ with its own error e₀ = x₀ − u·x₋₁), that additional term would NOT depend on x₁ and would contribute zero to ∂E/∂x₁. Accept an answer that either (a) correctly notes both terms here are nonzero and explains why, or (b) additionally makes the generalization point about a hypothetical third term. Do not require (b), but it is the stronger answer.

4. ∂E/∂x₁ at x₁ = 1.5: e₁ − v·e₂ = −0.5 − 0.5·(0.15) = −0.5 − 0.075 = **−0.575**.

5. The three quantities: **e₁** (the output of x₁'s own, immediately-adjacent error neuron — the bottom-up signal), **v** (the weight of the single synapse connecting x₁ to x₂ — a number stored locally at that synapse), **e₂** (the output of the error neuron immediately downstream of x₁, arriving back at x₁ only via that same synapse, weighted by v). Locality argument: x₁ never needs the raw value of x₀ — x₀'s influence has already been folded into e₁ by x₁'s own error neuron before x₁ ever sees it. Symmetrically x₁ never needs the raw value of x₂ — it's folded into e₂ by x₂'s error neuron. x₁ only reads two error-neuron outputs and one synaptic weight, all of which live at its own site or its immediate neighbours' sites.

6. e₁ = v·e₂ → (x₁ − 2) = 0.5·(0.9 − 0.5·x₁) → x₁ − 2 = 0.45 − 0.25·x₁ → 1.25·x₁ = 2.45 → **x₁* = 1.96**.
   Interpretation: a fixed point of ∂E/∂x is the activity value where the bottom-up error signal and the weighted top-down error signal exactly cancel — the node feels zero net "force" from E, so gradient descent (dx₁/dt = −∂E/∂x₁) stops moving it. This is the same object as an attractor fixed point from A2, applied to a specific scalar function E rather than an abstract energy landscape.

Numeric check for later pieces: E(x₁* = 1.96) = ½(−0.04)² + ½(−0.08)² = 0.0008 + 0.0032 = **0.004** (down from 0.13625 at x₁ = 1.5 — used in Piece 2's monotonic-decrease check).

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Write e1 and e2 as explicit functions of x1 first, before touching E at all. What is d(e1)/dx1? What is d(e2)/dx1?"
- Hint 2: "You have ∂E/∂x1 = e1·(de1/dx1) + e2·(de2/dx1). You already have both slopes from hint 1 — substitute them in symbolically before plugging in numbers."
- Hint 3: "Check whether your ∂E/∂x1 has the shape 'own error minus weight times downstream error'. If yours doesn't look like that, redo the chain rule on the e2 term — the sign of de2/dx1 is where slips happen."
- Never: never state the formula ∂E/∂x1 = e1 − v·e2 outright, even partially; never confirm a numeric answer is "close" without the learner showing the algebra step that produced it; never introduce precision weighting, weight-update rules, or non-chain graph topology into this derivation (out of scope — C2/C3/C4).

## Piece 2 — Settling is gradient descent

**Type:** simulation · **Time:** ~75 min

**Learning goals**
- Implement settling (activity updates only, weights frozen) on the same 3-node chain and confirm it performs gradient descent on E.
- Confirm empirically that the fixed point matches the hand-derived x₁*.
- Structure the code so that the update for a node is architecturally incapable of reading anything non-local — demonstrating the Piece 1 locality claim in code, not just in prose.

**Task**

Build a self-contained script (PyTorch or plain numpy, under ~100 lines) that:

1. Fixes x₀ = 1, x₂ = 0.9 (clamped, as in Piece 1), w = 2, v = 0.5 (fixed weights — no weight learning in this lesson).
2. Initializes x₁ at some starting value (e.g. 0.0 or 1.5 — pick one and say so).
3. Defines `e1(x0, w, x1)` and `e2(x1, v, x2)` and `energy(x1) = 0.5*e1**2 + 0.5*e2**2` as small separate functions.
4. Defines a `local_update(e1, e2, v)` function whose signature takes **only** `e1`, `e2`, `v` — not `x0`, `x2`, or `w` directly — and returns `e1 - v*e2` (the gradient) or the resulting activity delta. This is the architectural locality check: if the function signature needed `x0` or `x2`, that would already be a violation, independent of what the function body does with them.
5. Runs a settling loop: at each iteration compute e1, e2, the gradient via `local_update`, update x1 by a small step against the gradient (`x1 -= lr * grad`), and record the energy at each step.
6. Cross-checks the hand-computed gradient against autograd: build x1 as a tensor with `requires_grad=True`, compute `energy(x1)` and call `.backward()`, and confirm `x1.grad` matches your manual `e1 - v*e2` at that point.
7. After the loop, plots or prints the energy trace and confirms two things numerically: (a) energy is non-increasing at every step (with a small numerical tolerance), and (b) the final x1 is within 1e-3 of x1* = 1.96.

**Deliverable**

The script itself, plus a short (5-10 line) printed or written summary reporting: starting energy, final energy, final x1, number of iterations to convergence, and the autograd cross-check result.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | Script runs standalone; energy trace is strictly non-increasing (or non-increasing within float tolerance) at every step; final x1 within 1e-3 of 1.96; autograd cross-check passes; `local_update` signature genuinely excludes x0/x2/w, and the learner's summary explicitly connects this to Piece 1's locality argument. |
| Adequate | Script runs and converges to the right x1, but the locality restriction is only asserted in comments/prose rather than enforced by the function signature (e.g. `local_update` takes the whole state and just doesn't use x0), or the monotonic-decrease check is eyeballed from a plot rather than asserted numerically. |
| Not yet | Script diverges or converges to the wrong fixed point (check for a sign error in the update direction — gradient descent needs `-=`, not `+=`, or vice versa depending on gradient sign convention); or autograd disagrees with the manual gradient and the discrepancy isn't investigated. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Reference numbers to check the learner's output against (from Piece 1): at x1=1.5, e1=−0.5, e2=0.15, gradient = e1 − v·e2 = −0.575, energy = 0.13625. True minimum at x1* = 1.96, with e1=−0.04, e2=−0.08, energy = 0.004.

Reference update: `grad = e1 - v*e2; x1 = x1 - lr*grad`. This is plain gradient descent on the convex quadratic E(x1) = ½(x1−2)² + ½(0.9−0.5x1)², whose curvature (second derivative) is 1 + v² = 1.25. Gradient descent on a quadratic with curvature L is monotonically decreasing in function value for any lr < 2/L = 1.6; lr in the 0.1-0.5 range converges cleanly in a few dozen iterations. If a learner uses lr ≥ ~1.6 expect divergence or oscillation — that's a legitimate thing for them to discover and explain, not necessarily a failure, if they investigate the curvature bound.

Autograd cross-check: `x1 = torch.tensor(1.5, requires_grad=True); e1 = x1 - 2.0; e2 = 0.9 - 0.5*x1; E = 0.5*e1**2 + 0.5*e2**2; E.backward()` → `x1.grad` should equal −0.575, matching the manual value exactly (this is an exact, not approximate, check since it's the same computation graph).

Locality check: acceptable implementations pass e1, e2, v (three floats/scalars) into the update function and nothing else. A common failure mode to watch for: computing e1 and e2 *inside* `local_update` using x0/x2/w passed in as arguments — that reintroduces non-local dependence into the function that is supposed to represent "what x1's synapse/error-neuron machinery can access." The error computation itself (step 3) is allowed to use x0/w and x2/v respectively, because that computation is what happens locally *at the error neurons*, which sit adjacent to x0 and x2 respectively — the point is that x1's own update, once e1 and e2 already exist as neuron outputs, needs nothing further from x0 or x2.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "What's the sign convention — does `x1 -= lr * grad` move x1 toward lower or higher energy? Check against your hand-derived direction at x1=1.5: which way should x1 move to decrease E?"
- Hint 2: "If autograd disagrees with your manual gradient, print both at the same x1 value and compare term by term — the discrepancy is usually a missing factor of v or a sign flip in de2/dx1, not an autograd bug."
- Hint 3: "For the locality check: if you deleted x0 and x2 from your Python namespace right before calling `local_update`, would the call still work? If not, something not local snuck into the signature."
- Never: never hand over working code for `local_update` or the settling loop; never assert convergence is correct without the learner showing the printed final x1 and energy values; never let lr be chosen without at least asking the learner what determines whether gradient descent on this E converges monotonically (the curvature bound is fair game to hint toward, not to state outright).

## Piece 3 — Reading Bogacz structurally

**Type:** guided reading (`papers/C1_bogacz_2017_free_energy_tutorial.txt`) · **Time:** ~50 min

**Learning goals**
- Recognize the generative-model framing (predict inputs from causes) in a second source, independent of this lesson's own notation.
- Map Bogacz's error/free-energy formalism onto the e1, e2, E already derived by hand.
- Correctly place precision=1 as a special case of a more general treatment, without needing precision-weighted errors for this lesson.

**Task** — answer in your own words; equations in the source file may be mangled by text extraction, so answer at the level of argument structure, not by quoting formulas.

1. Bogacz sets up perception as inferring a hidden cause from a noisy observation, i.e. a generative model running from causes to predictions. Find where he introduces this setup and state, in your own words, which direction the model runs (cause → prediction, or prediction → cause) and why that direction matters for calling it "generative."
2. Locate where Bogacz shows that maximizing the posterior probability of the cause is equivalent to minimizing a sum of two squared error terms. Describe each of his two error terms in words (what each one measures), and say which one plays the role of your e1 and which plays the role of your e2 in the 3-node chain — even though his example may have different dimensionality or symbols.
3. Bogacz's general derivation weights each error term by a precision (inverse variance). Does he ever consider the special case where both precisions equal 1? If you can find it, note where. Either way, explain in one or two sentences why your E = ½e1² + ½e2² (unweighted) is the precision=1 special case of his general expression, not a separate formula.
4. Bogacz gives an update rule for the node representing the estimate of the hidden cause, structured as "this node's own error term minus a weight-scaled downstream error term" (or the equivalent, in whatever notation the extraction preserved). Compare its shape to your ∂E/∂x1 = e1 − v·e2 term by term, in words — which piece of his rule corresponds to e1, which to v, which to e2?
5. Bogacz separates fast dynamics (the estimate settling to a value) from slow dynamics (weights being learned). Find where he makes this two-timescale argument and summarize, in 2-3 sentences, why "settling" — the object you implemented in Piece 2 — is the fast dynamics with weights frozen, and why weight learning being slower is what makes that separation valid rather than an arbitrary implementation choice. Do not describe the weight-update rule itself — that's C2.
6. State in one or two sentences why "free energy" (in Bogacz's terms) at precision 1 is the same object as the E you derived by hand in Piece 1, up to the precision factor.

**Deliverable**

Written answers to 1-6, each a few sentences, citing roughly where in the source text (section heading or nearby distinctive phrase) each answer comes from, since page/equation numbers may not survive extraction.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All six answers correct and correctly located; the e1/e2 correspondence in Q2 and Q4 is stated explicitly and correctly (not just "sounds similar"); Q3 and Q6 correctly identify precision=1 as a special case rather than treating precision-weighting as a separate, unrelated mechanism. |
| Adequate | Most answers correct; Q4's term-by-term correspondence is present but loose (e.g. correctly identifies "own error minus something" without pinning down that the "something" is v-scaled e2); Q5 correctly identifies the two-timescale claim but doesn't connect it to the learner's own settling-loop code. |
| Not yet | Q1 gets the generative direction backwards; Q2/Q4 fail to distinguish which of Bogacz's terms is upstream vs downstream; Q3/Q6 treat precision-weighting as something E is missing rather than as a generalization E is a special case of. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

1. Bogacz's setup: an observation u is generated from a hidden cause v via u = g(v) + noise — the model runs cause → prediction of observation, and "generative" means it specifies how to generate/predict data from causes, which is then inverted (via Bayes) to infer the cause from data. This is the same direction as x0 → prediction of x1 in the chain (a lower node's activity generates a prediction for the node above it).

2. Bogacz shows −log p(v|u) (up to additive constants) decomposes into a sum of two squared-error terms weighted by precisions: one term for the mismatch between the observation u and its prediction g(v) (analogous to your e2, the "output-side" error), and one term for the mismatch between the estimate v and its own prior/prediction (analogous to your e1, the "input-side" error one level down). The precise analogue depends on how the learner maps chain position to Bogacz's single-cause setup, but the correspondence (one term downstream-of/output-side, one term upstream-of/prior-side, relative to the node being estimated) must be stated.
   Grader note: accept either direction of naming (e1↔prior error, e2↔likelihood error) as long as the learner is internally consistent and explains which is which — the graded content is recognizing there are exactly two terms of two different characters, not memorizing which Bogacz symbol is which.

3. Bogacz's numerical worked example in the tutorial does use unequal precisions (to make the general weighting visible) but the general derivation makes clear that setting both precisions to 1 recovers plain sum-of-squared-errors, since precision multiplies the squared error inside the sum before it's minimized — dropping precision to 1 for both terms is not a different derivation, it's the same formula with the weighting factors set to their neutral value. E = ½e1² + ½e2² is exactly this: Bogacz's Σ (precision · error²)/2 with both precisions set to 1.

4. Bogacz's activity update for the estimate node has the shape: rate of change ∝ (precision-weighted own error) − (weight × precision-weighted downstream error), i.e. structurally identical to e1 − v·e2: "own local error minus synapse-weight times the error one level away." The correspondence: his own-error term ↔ e1, his weight ↔ v, his downstream error term ↔ e2. At precision 1 the precision factors disappear and the shapes match exactly.

5. Bogacz treats the neural activity (the estimate) as evolving on a fast timescale toward the value that minimizes free energy for the *current* weights, while weights change slowly based on the same error signals averaged over many fast settlings. This is exactly the standard justification (also used by Whittington & Bogacz 2017, and implicit in the project's own settling loop) for treating settling as "gradient descent on E with weights fixed" as a well-posed sub-problem: on the timescale where activities are converging, weights are approximately constant, so E really is only a function of the activities during that phase. That is precisely what steps/02_pc_graphs and the CartPole 100-iteration settle already implement — weights frozen for the duration of a settle.

6. Free energy, in the Gaussian/precision=1 case, reduces to the negative log of a Gaussian likelihood/prior up to constants, i.e. a sum of squared errors — which is exactly E = ½e1² + ½e2² by construction. "Free energy" and "energy" are the same scalar object in this lesson's restricted setting; the "free" terminology and its generalization to non-unit precision is C3's territory, not needed here.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "Search the text for where he first writes 'u = g(v)' or describes an observation coming from a cause — that's the generative-model paragraph, not necessarily the first equation in the file."
- Hint 2: "There are two error terms in his derivation because there are two levels: v's own value versus its prior, and u versus its prediction from v. Which of those two feels more like 'coming from below' (x0→x1 in your chain) and which more like 'coming from above' (x1→x2)?"
- Hint 3: "If the equations in the extracted text look garbled, don't try to parse the symbols — read the sentence before and after the equation, which usually states in words what it means."
- Never: never supply the missing/mangled equation text from outside knowledge as if reconstructing the paper; never let the learner substitute a quote from Millidge 2021 or another source as if it were Bogacz's own argument — cross-checking against Millidge is fine as a sanity check but the assigned reading is Bogacz; never introduce precision-weighted learning rules into this lesson's deliverable (that's C3).

## Piece 4 — Why locality isn't a coding convenience

**Type:** written defence · **Time:** ~50 min · **Length:** 300-600 words

**Learning goals**
- Argue, from the derivation (not from the codebase's existing structure), that the project's "purely local" learning-rule constraint is a mathematical consequence of what settling is, not an arbitrary implementation choice.
- Use the neuron-pair / error-neuron vocabulary correctly and specifically, tied to the concrete e1/e2 example.
- Correctly scope the claim: this lesson only establishes locality for activity settling, not for weight updates.

**Task**

Write 300-600 words defending this claim: *"The settling loop already implemented in this project (network.py, the Step 1/2 settling loop, the 100-iteration CartPole settle) is not merely inspired by predictive coding — it IS gradient descent on a specific scalar energy E, and the fact that each node's update only touches local quantities is a direct consequence of E's structure as a sum of pairwise terms, not a design choice that happened to be convenient for neuromorphic hardware."*

Your defence must:
- Use the 3-node chain's ∂E/∂x1 = e1 − v·e2 as your concrete example, explaining what e1 and e2 physically are (outputs of specific error neurons) and what v physically is (a specific synapse's stored weight — connect this explicitly to the "virtual memristor" idea: the synapse stores v and only needs e1/e2-adjacent information to be involved in the update, nothing global).
- State explicitly why a hypothetical *non-local* rule (e.g. one where x1's update needed x2's raw activity value directly, bypassing e2, or needed a global loss computed over the whole network) would require information x1 physically cannot have without extra wiring that predictive coding doesn't posit.
- Connect this to the neuron-pair abstraction: explain why the error neuron is not a bookkeeping convenience but the physical carrier of exactly one term in the energy sum — which is what makes "prediction neuron + error neuron, reasoned about as one unit" a principled abstraction rather than a naming convention.
- Explicitly scope the claim: state that this argument establishes locality for the *activity-settling* phase only (weights v, w held fixed); whether the *weight-update* rule is equally forced to be local, or is merely designed to be local, is a separate question deferred to C2. Do not claim this lesson proves weight-update locality.

**Deliverable**

A 300-600 word written defence meeting the four requirements above.

**Rubric**

| Band | Criteria |
|---|---|
| Strong | All four required elements present and correct; the physical-carrier argument for the error neuron is stated precisely (one specific term in the sum, not "represents error" vaguely); the scoping statement explicitly names weight-update locality as open/deferred to C2 rather than silently assuming it. |
| Adequate | Three of four elements present; the "why a non-local rule is physically impossible" argument is asserted rather than derived from the concrete e1/e2/v example; scoping to activity-settling vs weight-updates is present but underdeveloped (mentioned in passing, not explained). |
| Not yet | Defence describes locality only in terms of "it's what the code already does" without deriving the claim from E's structure; conflates activity settling with weight learning as though this lesson covers both; error neuron is described generically ("it computes error") without tying it to one specific term in the energy sum. |

**Grader key** (teacher AI only)

Required claims a strong answer must contain, each independently checkable:
1. An explicit restatement that dx1/dt = −∂E/∂x1 = −(e1 − v·e2) = v·e2 − e1 IS the settling update, i.e. settling is literally gradient descent on this E, not merely analogous to it.
2. e1 identified as the output of the error neuron paired with the prediction neuron for x1 (the bottom-up term); e2 identified as the output of the error neuron paired with the prediction neuron for x2 (arriving at x1 only via the x1→x2 synapse); v identified as the weight physically stored at that one synapse.
3. The counterfactual: a rule needing x2's raw value directly (not via e2) would require x1 to have wiring into x2's activity that predictive coding's architecture doesn't provide — x1 only has a wire to x2's *error* neuron via the existing synapse, weighted by v. This is the crux of the "physically cannot" argument, not just "wouldn't be local by definition."
4. Error neuron as physical carrier of exactly one summand of E: e2's error neuron literally computes and holds the value that is squared and appears as the second term of E — it isn't a metaphor for "measuring error," it is that term.
5. Explicit scoping sentence: this argument is about the settling/activity phase with w, v fixed; whether weight updates (how v itself changes) can be derived to be equally local, or are only designed to be, is left to C2, and this piece must not claim otherwise.

Common overreach to flag: claiming this lesson "proves the whole project's local-learning-rule constraint" — it only proves locality for the *activity* update in this energy-minimization view; weight-update locality (how v changes) is a related but separate claim requiring ∂E/∂v, not covered here.

**Teacher constraints**
- Hint 1: "You've stated that the rule is local — now say why a rule that instead read x2 directly would be impossible, not just non-standard. What wire would that require that isn't there?"
- Hint 2: "You called the error neuron 'the thing that computes error' — can you point to exactly which term of E = ½e1² + ½e2² that specific error neuron's output IS, not just represents?"
- Hint 3: "Reread your last paragraph — are you claiming anything about how weights get updated? If so, cut it; this lesson only covers activities settling with weights frozen."

## Completion bar
