    Lesson spec¶

    C4. Predictive coding on arbitrary graphs (Salvatori et al. 2022)¶

    Prerequisites: C1–C3; A2 and graph topology with reciprocal connections

    Size: two to three sittings

    Source material: Not in D&A — arbitrary-topology PC postdates it by two decades. Salvatori et al. 2022, Learning on Arbitrary Graph Topologies via Predictive Coding (arXiv:2201.13180) — the paper Step 2 reproduces.

    Local copies: papers/C4_salvatori_2022_arbitrary_graph_topologies.pdf.

    Goal: See that going from layered PC to graph PC is a re-indexing, not a new formalism: "layer above/below" becomes "in-neighbours/out-neighbours" in the energy sum, and every update term stays local. Closes the misconception that arbitrary topology needs new maths or breaks locality, and shows what a fixed input/output split was buying and giving up.

    Scope: - Energy generalization: a per-node error term summed over that node's actual neighbours, replacing the layer-indexed sum — same local rule, different neighbour set - Input/output flexibility: any node clampable or free per trial, no reserved input or output layer - The paper's results: associative memory, generation and classification from one trained model; the topologies tested - Cycles and recurrence: whether settling still converges with loops, and what guarantees or threatens convergence - Not covered: spiking neurons on the graph (Step 3 work, not a lesson), clamping patterns in depth (C5), backprop equivalence (C6)

    You should be able to answer: 1. Write the generalized per-node energy term for graph PC and identify exactly which layered-PC term it replaces. 2. In a 3-node fully connected graph A–B–C with A clamped and C free, what does B's prediction depend on, and how does that differ from a layered network where layer l depends only on l−1? 3. Name two of the paper's three task types run from a single trained model without retraining, and state what changes between them. 4. Does energy minimization on a graph with cycles still converge to a fixed point? What property guarantees it, or under what condition can it fail to settle? 5. Why does "any node can be input, output, both or neither" matter for a network that later adds and removes nodes at runtime? Name the mechanism it removes the need for.

    Project tie-in: The theory behind steps/02_pc_graphs/, already built and working, and the direct prerequisite for Steps 3–4 where node roles and topology are not fixed in advance.

    Done when: You writes the generalized energy equation unaided and can say what guarantees or threatens convergence on a cyclic graph.

    Lesson spec: lessons.md → C4. Total time: 5–7 hours across 2–3 sittings. Prerequisites: C1–C3; A2 and graph topology with reciprocal connections.

    Running order¶

    Consume, then apply, then refine:

    audio overview → guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written defence (Piece 4)

    Pieces are numbered by type, not by sequence. The reading front-loads the concepts, the derivation makes them exact, the simulation is where they stick and get refined, and the defence is where you find out whether they did.

    Verified against the source¶

    Checked against papers/C4_salvatori_2022_arbitrary_graph_topologies.pdf. One implementation detail worth putting directly to you, because a reimplementation almost never matches it by accident: the paper reports that "we have tested different activation functions; the most promising seems to be HardTanh", and that value-node learning used a rate of 1.0 with SGD. If you's step 02 code uses tanh, ReLU or a different optimiser, that is a real divergence from the published method — which is precisely what the implementation-check questions are for. Neither choice is necessarily wrong; the point is knowing it was a choice.

    Piece 1 — Re-indexing the energy¶

    Type: derivation · Time: 60–90 min

    Learning goals - Rewrite the layered PC energy as a per-node sum over an explicit neighbour set, and show the two are the same formalism under a relabeling. - Identify exactly which layered term the graph term replaces, and why nothing about locality changes. - Trace, by hand, how a node's update depends on both its own prediction error and the errors of nodes it helps predict — and see this produce non-layered dependency.

    Task

    Part A — generalize the energy. Layered PC (from C1) writes, for a network with layers indexed by ell = 0..L:

    E = sum_{ell=1}^{L} || x_ell − f(W_ell x_{ell−1}) ||^2

    Each layer's error term uses exactly one fixed neighbour set: "the layer below." Do the following:

    1. Define, for a graph with node set V and directed edges (j → i) meaning "j predicts into i," the in-neighbour set N_in(i).
    2. Write the per-node prediction x̂i = sum w_ij f(x_j) and per-node error e_i = x_i − x̂_i.
    3. Write the total energy F = (1/2) sum_{i in V} e_i^2.
    4. State in one sentence exactly which term in the layered E this replaces, and what the replacement is (be precise: it is not "layers become nodes," it is which index set changes and how).
    5. Write the local weight update rule dw_ij/dt = eta * e_i * f(x_j) and confirm in one line why it only needs signals available at the two endpoints of the edge (pre-synaptic activity f(x_j), post-synaptic error e_i) — this is the "virtual memristor" property from CLAUDE.md.
    6. Write the inference (settling) update for a free node i: dx_i/dt = −eta * (e_i − sum_{k : i in N_in(k)} w_ki e_k) Explain in one sentence why the second term exists in the graph case but is usually invisible in a strictly feedforward layered derivation (hint: in a layered net, does a node ever appear in more than one other node's prediction?).

    Part B — worked example, by hand, with numbers given. Take the 3-node fully-connected reciprocal graph A—B—C (every pair has an edge in both directions: A→B, B→A, B→C, C→B, A→C, C→A). Use identity f (no nonlinearity), all weights w = 0.5, integration step eta = 0.1. Clamp x_A = 1.0 for all steps. Initialize x_B = 0, x_C = 0.5 (free).

    Step 1 — compute by hand: x̂_A, x̂_B, x̂_C, then e_A, e_B, e_C, then the updates dx_B and dx_C using the rule from A.6, then the new x_B, x_C. Report F = (1/2) sum e_i^2 before the update.

    Step 2 — repeat once more from the new x_B, x_C. Report the new F and confirm whether it went down.

    Part C — do it yourself. Run Step 3 by hand from your Step 2 outputs (same eta, same clamp on A). Report x_B, x_C, and F after Step 3. State whether F is still decreasing and whether e_A, e_B, e_C are heading toward zero or toward some nonzero fixed point — and if nonzero, say in one sentence why three error terms can't all be driven to zero by only two free variables (x_B, x_C) with x_A pinned away from its neighbours' joint prediction.

    Part D — answer AC2 directly. In this same A–B–C graph, with A clamped and C free: what does B's prediction depend on? Write it out (the actual expression, not just "A and C"). Then contrast explicitly: in a layered net where layer ℓ's prediction depends only on layer ℓ−1, could a hidden unit's value update ever be driven by the error of a unit that is not adjacent to it in the "next layer up" sense the way B here is driven by both e_A and e_C? Name the specific structural reason (bidirectional edges / no acyclic layer order) that makes this possible on a graph and impossible by construction in the layered case.

    Deliverable A written derivation (LaTeX, Markdown with math, or legible handwritten scan) covering Parts A–D, with Part B/C's numeric steps shown (not just final numbers — show x̂_i, e_i, dx_i at each step).

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong Part A energy and update rules are correct and self-derived (not copied from this sheet's Part B mechanics); Part B/C arithmetic matches the grader key to within rounding; Part C correctly identifies convergence to a nonzero fixed point and explains why (df=2 free variables, 3 error constraints); Part D gives the explicit expression for B's prediction and names bidirectionality/no-layer-order as the structural cause.
    Adequate Energy and update rules correct with minor notational slips; Part B arithmetic mostly right (one arithmetic slip tolerated if the method is right); Part D correctly says B depends on both A and C but the layered contrast is vague or missing the structural "why".
    Not yet Energy still written as a layer-indexed sum with nodes substituted in without changing the index set; Part B skipped or only final numbers given with no e_i/x̂_i shown; Part D says B depends on A and C but gives no contrast with layered nets, or gets the direction of dependency wrong (e.g. claims B only depends on A because A is clamped "first").

    Piece 2 — Settling on an adjacency matrix¶

    Type: simulation · Time: 2–3 hours

    Learning goals - Implement PC inference (settling) as pure local gradient descent on an arbitrary adjacency matrix, with no notion of "layer." - Show empirically that one set of trained weights, clamped differently at test time, produces three qualitatively different behaviours — no retraining. - Test whether settling still converges when the graph has cycles, and find a step-size regime where it visibly fails to.

    Task

    Build a self-contained PyTorch or numpy script (<~100 lines, synthetic data only, no autograd used for the weight update — the rule must be the local delta rule from Piece 1, not loss.backward() through the settling loop).

    1. Graph. 6 nodes, values in R. Two triangles sharing a bridge edge: triangle {0,1,2} fully reciprocally connected, triangle {3,4,5} fully reciprocally connected, plus a reciprocal bridge edge (2,3). This graph has cycles by construction.
    2. Synthetic training patterns. Generate 4 fixed 6-vectors (e.g. random but fixed-seed, values in [-1, 1]) — call them P0..P3.
    3. Training. For each pattern, clamp all 6 nodes to the pattern's values (no free nodes), run the local weight update dw_ij += eta_w * e_i * f(x_j) for several passes over all 4 patterns (f = tanh or identity, your choice, but state which). No inference is needed during training since everything is clamped — only weight learning.
    4. Inference engine. Write one function settle(w, x_init, clamped_mask, clamped_vals, steps, eta_x) that runs the Piece-1 update rule for free nodes only, holding clamped nodes fixed, and returns the trajectory of x and of F at every step.
    5. Three clamp configs from the same trained weights (no retraining between them):
    6. Config 1 ("classification-like"): clamp 5 of 6 nodes of a test pattern, leave 1 free. Check the free node settles near the true value for that pattern.
    7. Config 2 ("associative memory / pattern completion"): clamp only 2 of 6 nodes (a small cue) matching one training pattern, leave the other 4 free. Check the settled 6-vector is close (in L2) to the correct full training pattern, not to the others.
    8. Config 3 ("generation"): clamp 1 node only (treat it like a label/seed), leave the remaining 5 free. Check the generated vector is closest, by L2, to the training pattern whose corresponding node value was used as the seed.
    9. Cycles and convergence. Plot/print F vs. iteration for at least one config. Confirm F is monotonically non-increasing for a small eta_x (e.g. 0.05–0.1). Then deliberately pick a large eta_x (e.g. eta_x >= 1.5, or scale up the weights) and show F oscillating or diverging instead of settling. Report the approximate eta_x threshold where behaviour flips from convergent to non-convergent for your specific weights.

    Deliverable The script, plus a short (under one page) results note: final settled vectors and L2 distances to all 4 training patterns for each config (showing the correct one wins), the F-vs-iteration behaviour for a convergent and a non-convergent eta_x, and the observed instability threshold.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong All three configs correctly recover the intended pattern from one set of weights; weight update is provably local (no autograd across the settling loop, e_i and f(x_j) only); cyclic graph settles with monotonic F decrease at small eta_x; a specific eta_x (or weight-scale) is shown to break convergence with F visibly increasing/oscillating, and the note explains why (loop gain / step size) rather than just showing the plot.
    Adequate Configs 1–2 work, config 3 (generation) is weak or only partially distinguishes patterns; monotonic decrease shown at small eta_x; divergence demonstrated but not explained.
    Not yet Fewer than two configs distinguish the correct pattern; weight update secretly uses loss.backward()/autograd through multiple settling steps (violates locality); no divergence case attempted, or F-vs-iteration not reported at all.

    Piece 3 — Reading Salvatori et al. 2022¶

    Type: guided reading · Time: 90–120 min

    Learning goals - Read the paper this project's Step 02 already implements, closely enough to check the implementation against the published method rather than against your own prior assumptions. - Locate the paper's own statement of the generalized energy, its inference/learning schedule, and its convergence discussion for cyclic topologies. - Identify the paper's task types run from a single trained model, and what specifically differs between them (clamping, not weights).

    Task

    Read papers/C4_salvatori_2022_arbitrary_graph_topologies.pdf in full (abstract through discussion; appendices skimmable). Answer the following, citing section/figure/algorithm numbers from the PDF itself:

    1. Find the paper's own definition of the energy function and the per-node error term. Write it in the paper's own notation, then map each symbol to the notation you used in Piece 1 (e.g. their value-node symbol ↔ your x_i, their prediction ↔ your x̂_i). Note anywhere your Piece 1 derivation used a different convention (e.g. sign, which direction "in-neighbour" points) and reconcile it.
    2. Find the paper's inference and weight-update algorithm (look for something like "Algorithm 1" or a training-loop description). Is inference run to convergence before each weight update, or are x and w updated on the same timescale/simultaneously? State which, quoting or paraphrasing the specific line.
    3. Implementation check A: Compare the schedule you found in Q2 against your own steps/02_pc_graphs/ training loop from memory (or by describing what you built, without re-reading the repo for this exercise). Does your implementation match the paper's schedule? If it differs, state the difference precisely (e.g. "I update weights every inference step, not after settling") and what effect that would have on training stability.
    4. Implementation check B: Find what nonlinearity f the paper uses in its main experiments (identity/linear, tanh, ReLU, or other — check the experiments/architecture section, not just the general formalism which may leave f abstract). State what you used in your own implementation and in Piece 2 of this coursework. If they differ, state one concrete consequence of the mismatch (e.g., for a linear f the model's fixed points are a linear/Gaussian system with a closed-form solution, whereas a saturating nonlinearity changes what "converge" can even mean near saturation).
    5. Name two of the paper's three task types demonstrated from a single trained model without retraining (associative memory / denoising, generation, classification — or however the paper phrases them). For your two chosen tasks, state precisely what changes between them (it should not be the weights — say what actually changes: which nodes are clamped, to what).
    6. Find where the paper discusses topology beyond the standard layered case (which topologies are tested — e.g., fully connected, layered as a special case, other structures). Does the paper test anything with cycles specifically, or is "arbitrary topology" argued more generally/theoretically? Report what you find, including if the honest answer is "the paper is thinner on this than I expected."
    7. Find the paper's treatment of convergence of the inference (settling) dynamics. Does it claim/prove convergence to a fixed point for general (possibly cyclic) topologies? What property does it lean on (e.g., energy as a Lyapunov-like quantity decreasing under gradient descent, a specific theorem, or an empirical claim only)? Does it name any condition under which settling could fail (step size, weight magnitude, nonlinearity)?
    8. Does the paper's account of "any node can be clamped or free" match your Piece 1/Piece 2 experience exactly, or does the paper impose any restriction you didn't (e.g., restrictions on which nodes may be output-only, minimum clamped-node count, or architecture constraints for the associative-memory task specifically)?

    Deliverable Written answers to Q1–Q8, each with a specific citation (section number, page, figure, or algorithm label) from the PDF — answers without a locatable citation should be treated as unverified.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong All 8 answered with correct citations; Q3/Q4 (implementation checks) identify a real, specific divergence (or correctly confirm a real match) between your own implementation and the paper, not a vague "seems similar"; Q7 correctly identifies the energy-decrease/Lyapunov-style argument (or whatever the paper actually argues) and states the failure condition named by the paper if one exists.
    Adequate 6–7 of 8 answered with citations; Q3/Q4 attempted but the comparison to your own code is generic rather than specific (e.g. "probably similar" without naming what was checked); Q7 identifies convergence is claimed but not what it rests on.
    Not yet Answers given without citations (i.e., recalled/guessed rather than located in the text); Q3/Q4 skipped or answered only in the abstract ("my implementation follows the paper") with no specific comparison point; Q5 names something not actually in the paper (e.g. lists a task type from a different PC paper, C2/C6).

    Piece 4 — What the layer split was buying¶

    Type: written defence · Time: 45–60 min

    Learning goals - Synthesize Pieces 1–3 into a single argument: graph PC is a re-indexing of layered PC, not new maths. - State concretely what a fixed input/output layer split bought (some guarantee or simplicity) and what it cost (flexibility this project's later steps need). - Connect "any node clampable" to the specific mechanism it removes the need for when topology itself changes at runtime (Step 04's growth/pruning).

    Task

    Write 300–600 words defending or complicating this claim:

    "Going from layered predictive coding to predictive coding on an arbitrary graph is a re-indexing of the same energy function, not a new formalism — and the thing a fixed input/output layer split was buying was never expressiveness, it was a convergence/scheduling guarantee that graph PC has to earn some other way."

    Your defence must: - State the re-indexing explicitly (in-neighbours replace "layer below" — reference Piece 1's energy). - Give one concrete example of what a fixed layer order guarantees "for free" that a general graph does not (draw on Piece 1 Part C or Piece 2's divergence experiment, or Piece 3's Q7 finding — something you actually derived or observed, not an abstract claim). - Give one concrete reason "any node clampable" matters for Step 04 (stress-triggered growth/pruning): name the specific bookkeeping or mechanism (e.g. a fixed input/output layer index, or a role map that must be updated when topology changes) that this flexibility removes the need for. - Take a position on whether the claim as stated is fully correct, or whether it understates/overstates something (e.g., is convergence really the only thing the layer split bought, or does it also simplify weight-update timing, credit assignment, or the C6 backprop-equivalence proof?).

    Deliverable 300–600 words, prose (not bullet points), in your own words.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong Re-indexing stated precisely and correctly (names the actual index-set change, not just "layers become nodes"); the "what layer order buys for free" example is concrete and drawn from your own derivation/simulation/reading rather than asserted; the Step 04 tie-in names a specific mechanism (e.g. no fixed input/output role map to maintain when pairs are added/pruned) rather than a generic "it's more flexible"; takes and defends a real position on the claim rather than agreeing with it wholesale.
    Adequate Re-indexing stated correctly but generically; the "buys for free" example is plausible but abstract/textbook rather than tied to your own worked numbers or code; Step 04 tie-in is present but vague (e.g. "flexible networks are good for pruning" without naming what bookkeeping is avoided).
    Not yet Re-indexing described loosely or incorrectly (e.g. "graphs are just layers with more connections"); no concrete example grounding the convergence/scheduling claim; Step 04 tie-in missing or generic; word count far outside 300–600 or written as bullets/outline rather than prose.
