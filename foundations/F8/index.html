    Replacing spike trains with a rate, and what that buys¶

    Chapters 5 and 6 built networks from spiking units — faithful, but expensive to simulate and hard to analyze. Section 7.2 trades the exact spike train ρ(t) for its trial-averaged firing rate r(t), justified whenever a neuron's input sums over many weakly correlated synapses: the sum's mean scales with the number of inputs, its standard deviation only with the square root, so rates barely perturb the total synaptic current driving the next neuron. That current, I_s, becomes an output rate through a response function, instantaneous (v = F(I)) or low-pass filtered (τ dv/dt = −v + F(I)), the latter giving the rate its own time constant τ_r — which everything downstream inherits, along with F, essentially the only nonlinearity these models allow.

    One update equation, two wiring diagrams¶

    Figure 7.3 — Feedforward network (input u, weights W) versus recurrent network (adds weight matrix M among the outputs). From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    A feedforward network's output is driven only by its input: τ_r dv/dt = −v + F(W·u). A recurrent network adds a second weight matrix M among the output units: τ_r dv/dt = −v + F(h + M·v), with h = W·u folding the feedforward drive into one term. That one substitution — v feeding back into its own equation through M — is essentially all the chapter's added machinery.

    Dale's law constrains M biologically: a neuron's outgoing synapses are all excitatory or all inhibitory, never mixed — why excitatory and inhibitory populations later get separate equations with separately signed matrices. Most of section 7.4 sidesteps that by simply assuming M symmetric. Symmetry hands a clean eigenvector basis; it is also false of real cortex, and rules out certain dynamics — oscillation chief among them — before a single equation is solved. Section 7.5 is what happens once it is lifted.

    What a matrix multiply plus a nonlinearity can already do¶

    Section 7.3 stays feedforward and shows what a single layer already buys: the coordinate transformation a reaching movement needs, combining retinal stimulus location with gaze angle into a body-centered target. The construction is multiplicative — output tuning shifts with gaze gain, not retinal position — yet the output unit is still a weighted sum passed through F.

    Figure 7.6 — Gaze-dependent gain modulation in posterior parietal cortex neurons, and the matching model tuning surface. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    The resolution is gain modulation: input neurons tuned to retinal position s, with gain scaled by gaze direction g, recombine linearly into a function of s + g only if the synaptic weight depends on the sum of each input's preferred retinal and gaze angle — multiplication emerging from addition over a population whose tuning already mixes the two signals, a move every amplification result below repeats.

    Diagonalizing the dynamics with eigenvectors¶

    Section 7.4 opens with the linear approximation F(x) = x — unrealistic, since rates can go negative, but exactly solvable, and the solution is the spine of the chapter. With M symmetric, its eigenvectors e_ν (M·e_ν = λ_ν e_ν) are orthogonal and span the space, so any output vector decomposes as v(t) = Σ_ν c_ν(t) e_ν. Substituting into τ_r dv/dt = −v + h + M·v and projecting onto each e_ν turns one coupled N-dimensional system into N independent scalar equations:

    τ_r dc_ν/dt = −(1 − λ_ν) c_ν + e_ν·h.

    Each mode is a leaky integrator driven by how much of h lies along its own eigenvector — e_ν·h is "how much this mode is asked to do," (1 − λ_ν) the knob on its response. Work through the time constant and steady state that fall out of solving this scalar equation; both are set entirely by λ_ν, which is where question 1 lives.

    Why one eigenvalue near 1 dominates everything¶

    If a single eigenvalue λ_1 sits close to 1 while the rest are comfortably smaller, the 1/(1 − λ_1) factor in that mode's steady state dwarfs every other term, and the output collapses onto a single axis: v_∞ ≈ (e_1·h) e_1 / (1 − λ_1). This is selective amplification — the recurrent weights decide which direction in rate-space gets picked out and magnified, regardless of how the input was shaped.

    Figure 7.8 — Selective amplification in a linear recurrent network: noisy tuned input in, a clean amplified mode out. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    Figure 7.8's network has recurrent coupling M(θ−θ′) built from a single cosine mode with λ_1 = 0.9: a noisy input with no dominant Fourier component comes out with its first harmonic amplified roughly tenfold and every other component untouched. Two eigenvectors sharing the same eigenvalue near 1 generalizes this to a whole subspace rather than a single axis — the degenerate case the chapter flags in passing.

    The edge case is λ_1 exactly 1: the mode's own equation loses its restoring term and becomes a pure integrator, c_1(t) accumulating the running integral of e_1·h — the network holds whatever it was told even after the input returns to zero.

    Figure 7.7 — Burst and integrator neurons maintaining eye position: persistent firing as a running integral of movement commands. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    The brain stem's eye-position system is read as a biological instance: integrator neurons downstream of saccadic burst cells hold a steady rate proportional to accumulated eye movement, matching equation 7.26 for λ_1 = 1 exactly. The catch, stated plainly in the text: this requires λ_1 tuned to 1 with extreme precision, nothing about the mechanism explains how a biological network would arrive at or hold that value, and real systems drift.

    Rectification: what saturation adds and what it costs¶

    Linear theory's eigenvalue condition for bounded behavior is unforgiving: push any λ_ν past 1 and that mode's coefficient grows without bound, forever, regardless of what any other part of the network is doing — the clean statement question 2 asks for, true only because linearity lets every mode be solved in isolation.

    Figure 7.9 — Selective amplification with a rectifying nonlinearity, same task as figure 7.8 but λ exceeds 1. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    Rectification, F(x) = [x]_+, changes this. Figure 7.9 uses λ_1 = 1.9 — unconditionally unstable under the linear rule — and the network still settles to a steady state, because negative rates clip to zero rather than feeding back and compounding. The output resembles the positive half of the linear response, smoothed, with several harmonics amplified together rather than one cleanly isolated.

    Figure 7.10 — Contrast-invariant orientation tuning: weak input modulation in, sharply tuned output whose width does not grow with contrast. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    Figure 7.12 — Winner-takes-all selection by a nonlinear recurrent network. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    Two further results, flagged without full derivation. Figure 7.10's orientation-tuning model takes a feedforward signal weakly modulated by orientation and returns a sharply tuned response whose width stays fixed as contrast rises — shape set by recurrence, not input. Figure 7.12's winner-take-all network goes further: given several comparable input peaks, it amplifies one and suppresses the rest toward zero. Neither is available to a linear network — superposition means its response to a sum of inputs is exactly the sum of its responses to each one, so it can scale a pattern but cannot choose among the alternatives composing it, and has no floor letting a suppressed competitor actually vanish rather than shrink.

    Section 7.4 closes by naming a general tool rather than working every case: for τ_s dI/dt = −I + h + M·F(I), a Lyapunov function L(I) can be built (Cohen-Grossberg) that strictly decreases along any trajectory whenever M is symmetric, F′ > 0, and L is bounded below — such a network cannot oscillate or wander, only settle at a fixed point. Associative memory follows immediately as the first use of that guarantee:

    Figure 7.16 — Associative recall: a network's activity relaxing from a partial cue to one of several stored fixed-point patterns. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    — weights are set so stored patterns become the guaranteed fixed points, and recall is relaxation from a partial cue into the nearest one. Basin size and the connection to Hopfield networks and an explicit energy function belong to their own lesson; this one stops at having the Lyapunov machinery in hand.

    Breaking symmetry: the route to oscillation¶

    Section 7.5 drops the symmetric-M assumption, giving excitatory and inhibitory units separate equations (7.12, 7.13) and, in the simplest case, collapsing each population to one rate, v_E and v_I. This 2D system suits phase-plane analysis: nullclines (where dv_E/dt or dv_I/dt vanishes) are straight lines whose intersection is the fixed point, and linear stability analysis there produces a 2×2 "stability matrix" from MEE, MEI, MIE, MII and the two time constants, whose eigenvalues settle the question.

    Figure 7.17 — Nullclines and fixed point of the E-I model (left), with the stability eigenvalue's real and imaginary parts plotted against τ_I (right). From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    Figure 7.17's right panel: as τ_I varies, the eigenvalue pair is sometimes two real numbers and sometimes a complex-conjugate pair, switching where the imaginary part crosses zero — governed by the sign of the discriminant inside equation 7.53's square root. Work out which sign gives real eigenvalues and which gives complex ones, and what each implies about the trajectory near the fixed point; that is question 3.

    Figure 7.18 — The fixed point is stable: firing rates settling, and the phase-plane trajectory spiraling into the fixed point. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    Figure 7.19 — The fixed point is unstable: firing rates oscillating persistently, the trajectory spiraling out to a limit cycle. From Dayan & Abbott, Theoretical Neuroscience, reproduced from the authors' teaching materials.

    Figures 7.18 and 7.19 show the two outcomes side by side, same model, τ_I moved past the boundary figure 7.17 identifies. Where the chapter goes next — an olfactory bulb model on the same analysis, oscillation frequency, synchrony across coupled E-I pairs — is left for later rather than compressed here.

    A one-paragraph pointer: stochastic networks¶

    Section 7.6 replaces deterministic F with a stochastic rule: binary units whose probability of being active is a sigmoid of total input, the Boltzmann machine. Noted here only as a marker — it reframes network dynamics as sampling from a probability distribution rather than relaxing to a fixed point, a perspective that pays off once learning rules arrive in chapter 8, not before.

    Lesson spec¶

    Goal: Move from single neurons to populations described by firing rates, and show that a linear recurrent network's behaviour is fully characterised by the eigenstructure of its weight matrix. Establish selective amplification, effective time constants and stability as the load-bearing ideas that A2 and A3 each extend in a different direction.

    Scope — what the lesson covers
    • The firing-rate model as an approximation to a spiking population, with its assumptions stated and a regime where it breaks
    • Feedforward networks: what a weight matrix does to an input pattern
    • Linear recurrent networks by eigen-decomposition: selective amplification of eigenvector-aligned components, the effective time constant per mode, and the stability condition
    • Nonlinear saturating recurrent networks: winner-take-all and gain control as consequences of saturation
    • Excitatory-inhibitory networks: the conditions for stable, damped or oscillatory return to equilibrium
    • Stochastic networks as a bridge to energy functions and noise, introduced but not developed
    • Not covered: attractors, basins and Hopfield memory built from the energy function (A2); E-I dynamics developed into rhythms, bands and synchrony (A3)

    You should be able to answer: 1. A linear recurrent network has largest real eigenvalue 0.8. Given membrane time constant tau, what is that mode's effective time constant, and what happens as the eigenvalue approaches 1? 2. State the stability condition for a linear recurrent network in terms of the eigenvalues of the recurrent weight matrix. 3. In a linearised E-I network, what condition on the eigenvalues distinguishes damped oscillatory return to equilibrium from monotonic decay? 4. For an input aligned with an eigenvector of eigenvalue lambda, give the expression for steady-state amplification compared to the feedforward-only case. 5. Name two computations saturation enables that a linear recurrent network cannot perform, and say specifically why linearity precludes each.

    Feeds: A2 (energy functions and recurrent dynamics), A3 (E-I dynamics), F9 (plasticity acts on these architectures).

    Done when: From a given recurrent weight matrix you computes eigenvalues and predicts which input components are amplified, how fast each mode relaxes, and whether the network is stable — without reaching for attractor or oscillation vocabulary.

    Coursework¶

    Consume, then apply, then refine: guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written explanation (Piece 4). Pieces are numbered by type, not sequence.

    Type: derivation · Time: 90-120 min

    Learning goals - Derive the effective time constant and steady-state gain of a single eigenmode of a linear recurrent network, from the network's defining ODE. - State and justify the stability condition for the full network in terms of the eigenvalues of the recurrent weight matrix. - Reduce a linearised excitatory-inhibitory network to a 2x2 eigenvalue problem and derive the algebraic condition that separates damped oscillatory return to equilibrium from monotonic decay.

    Task

    Part A — single-population eigenmode. Take the standard linear recurrent network equation from D&A §7.3: tau du/dt = -u + h + W u, where u(t) is the vector of population firing rates, h is the (constant, step) feedforward drive, and W is the recurrent weight matrix. Let e_a be an eigenvector of W with real eigenvalue lambda_a, and suppose the input is aligned with that eigenvector: h = h_a e_a, u(0) = 0.

    1. Write u(t) = c(t) e_a, substitute, and reduce the vector ODE to a scalar ODE for c(t). Solve it in closed form.
    2. Read off the effective time constant tau_eff(lambda_a) and the steady-state value c_ss.
    3. Define the amplification factor A(lambda_a) as the ratio of c_ss to the steady-state value the same input would produce with W = 0 (feedforward-only). Give A(lambda_a) as a function of lambda_a alone.
    4. Evaluate tau_eff and A at lambda_a = 0.8 with tau = 10 ms. State in words what happens to tau_eff as lambda_a -> 1^-, and why lambda_a = 1 is a singular point of your formula.
    5. Generalise: for an N-dimensional W with eigenvalues lambda_1, ..., lambda_N (assume W diagonalisable), state the condition on {lambda_a} under which every mode of the network is stable (bounded as t -> infinity for bounded h). Justify it from the sign of (1 - lambda_a) in your part-2 solution.

    Part B — excitatory-inhibitory 2x2 reduction. Consider the linearised two-population E-I system from D&A §7.5, written in the same form as Part A with u = (v_E, v_I)^T and a single shared tau: tau du/dt = -u + h + W u, W = [[w_EE, -w_EI], [w_IE, -w_II]], all four w's >= 0.

    1. For W_1 = [[0.5, -1.0], [1.5, -0.5]], compute trace(W_1) and det(W_1), form the characteristic polynomial lambda^2 - trace(W_1) lambda + det(W_1) = 0, and solve for the eigenvalues. State whether they are real or complex.
    2. Repeat for W_2 = [[0.5, -1.0], [0.2, -0.5]].
    3. From your part-1 solution, the decay/growth rate of a mode is governed by (lambda_a - 1)/tau. For each of W_1 and W_2, compute the eigenvalues of (W - I) and state (i) whether the network is stable, and (ii) whether its return to equilibrium after a small perturbation is monotonic or has a damped oscillatory component. Give the general algebraic rule (in terms of trace and det of the 2x2 W, or equivalently the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial) that decides real vs. complex eigenvalues, independent of the specific numbers.
    4. For the complex case, give the angular frequency of the decaying oscillation (omega, in rad/ms) and the decay time constant, using tau = 10 ms.

    Deliverable

    A written derivation (LaTeX, Markdown with math, or clearly legible scanned handwriting) covering all 9 steps, with every intermediate line of algebra shown — not just final formulas. Final numeric answers for steps 4, 6, 7, 9 must be stated explicitly, not left as unevaluated expressions.

    Rubric — how this is marked
    Band Criteria
    Strong All 9 steps correct and shown in full. Correctly identifies 1 - lambda_a as the mode's decay rate and derives A(lambda_a) = 1/(1 - lambda_a) from first principles (not by pattern-matching to a remembered formula). Correctly states the general stability condition as max_a Re(lambda_a) < 1 (not just "lambda < 1" for a single real eigenvalue). Correctly derives and states the trace/det (or discriminant) rule for real vs. complex eigenvalues of a general 2x2 matrix, then applies it to both W_1 and W_2.
    Adequate Steps 1-4 and 6-8 correct with correct final numbers, but the general N-dimensional stability condition (step 5) is stated loosely (e.g. "eigenvalues less than 1" without addressing complex eigenvalues via their real part), or the general trace/det rule in step 8 is not stated explicitly (only the two worked numeric cases are done).
    Not yet Arithmetic errors in the closed-form solution, effective time constant, or eigenvalue computations; amplification factor derived by guessing rather than substitution; oscillatory vs. monotonic distinction not tied to real vs. complex eigenvalues.

    Type: simulation · Time: 75-100 min

    Learning goals - Build a linear recurrent network whose weight matrix has a known, controlled eigenstructure, and recover that eigenstructure numerically. - Verify the effective-time-constant and steady-state-amplification formulas from Piece 1 against direct numerical integration, per eigenmode. - Observe the stability boundary directly (an eigenvalue crossing 1 produces unbounded growth) and observe that a saturating nonlinearity restores boundedness without restoring linearity's clean per-mode formulas.

    Task

    Construct a 3-neuron linear recurrent network with weight matrix W = 0.8 (e1 e1^T) + 0.3 (e2 e2^T) + (-0.5) (e3 e3^T), using the fixed orthonormal vectors e1 = (1,1,1)/sqrt(3), e2 = (1,-1,0)/sqrt(2), e3 = (1,1,-2)/sqrt(6). (These three vectors are mutually orthogonal and unit-norm; you may verify this by hand before coding.) This construction guarantees W is symmetric with exact eigenvalues 0.8, 0.3, -0.5 and eigenvectors e1, e2, e3.

    Simulate tau du/dt = -u + h + Wu by explicit Euler integration with tau = 10 ms, dt = 0.1 ms, for at least 300 ms, starting from u(0) = 0.

    Part 1: For each eigenvector e_a in turn, drive the network with h = 1.0 * e_a (a step input aligned with that eigenvector, held constant). Project u(t) onto e_a at every time step. Record the simulated steady-state value and the simulated time to reach 63.2% of that steady-state value (the empirical effective time constant). Compare both against the analytic tau_eff(lambda_a) and steady-state amplification formulas from Piece 1.

    Part 2: Rebuild W with the first eigenvalue changed from 0.8 to 1.2 (keep 0.3 and -0.5). Drive with h = 1.0 * e1 as before and simulate for 200 ms. Confirm the projection onto e1 grows rather than saturates, and compare its growth to the analytic solution from Piece 1's step-1 formula evaluated at lambda_a = 1.2 (same closed form, now with 1 - lambda_a < 0).

    Part 3: Keep the unstable W from Part 2 (lambda_1 = 1.2). Add a saturating nonlinearity: replace the recurrent drive Wu with an elementwise cap, f(x) = r_max * tanh(x / r_max) with r_max = 5.0, applied to the vector Wu before it enters the ODE. Simulate for 400 ms with the same h = 1.0 * e1. Confirm the projection onto e1 now approaches a finite steady value instead of diverging.

    Write this as under ~100 lines of self-contained Python using only numpy (no downloads, no external data). Print a small table for Part 1 (lambda, predicted tau_eff, predicted steady-state, simulated steady-state, simulated tau_eff) and the key checkpoint values for Parts 2 and 3.

    Deliverable

    The .py script, plus its printed output, plus 3-5 sentences per part stating whether the numerical result matched the analytic prediction from Piece 1 (and by how much, if not exact).

    Rubric — how this is marked
    Band Criteria
    Strong W constructed correctly (numpy's eigendecomposition recovers 0.8, 0.3, -0.5 and eigenvectors matching e1, e2, e3 up to sign); Part 1 table matches analytic predictions to within a few percent for all three modes; Part 2 shows clear unbounded growth matched against the analytic exponential; Part 3 shows convergence to a finite value and you correctly identifies that the saturating network no longer obeys the simple 1/(1-lambda) amplification law because superposition (linearity) has been broken.
    Adequate Part 1 and Part 2 done correctly with correct qualitative conclusions, but Part 3 only shows "it doesn't blow up" without engaging the amplification-formula-breaks-down point, or the effective-time-constant measurement method (63.2% threshold) is applied inconsistently across modes.
    Not yet W's eigenvalues/eigenvectors not verified against the target values before use; input not actually aligned with an eigenvector (so the projection mixes modes and the comparison to Piece 1 is invalid); Part 2's instability not connected back to the sign of (1 - lambda_a); Part 3 nonlinearity applied in a way that makes it a different network (e.g. saturating u instead of the recurrent drive) without noting the difference.

    Task
