    F4. Information theory for neurons

    Lesson spec

    F4. Information theory for neurons

    Prerequisites: F1, F3 (F2 useful for the efficient-coding account of receptive fields)

    Size: one to two sittings — the entropy and mutual-information worked example plus the infomax predictions make one sitting; spike-train entropy rates and estimator bias warrant a second

    Source material: D&A ch. 4 — Entropy and Mutual Information · Information and Entropy Maximization · Entropy and Information for Spike Trains

    Goal: Turn "this neuron carries information about the stimulus" into a computable quantity in bits. Derive entropy and mutual information on a small worked example, connect mutual information to decoder-independent bounds, and use the infomax hypothesis to predict concrete receptive-field properties.

    Scope: - Entropy H(R) and mutual information I(R;S) computed by hand on a small discrete table, in bits - Why mutual information is the right currency: symmetric, assumption-free about the decoder, and an upper bound on any decoder's performance - Infomax and efficient coding: maximising entropy under a fixed output range predicts histogram equalisation; decorrelating natural-image statistics predicts centre-surround receptive fields - Entropy and information rates for spike trains via the direct method, binning trains into words - Why finite data biases entropy estimates downward, and what that means for reported values - Not covered: population codes and correlation-based information, spike-timing decoding beyond entropy rates

    You should be able to answer: 1. Given a two-stimulus, two-response table with specified joint probabilities, compute H(R), H(R|S) and I(R;S) in bits. 2. Why is I(R;S) = I(S;R), and what does that symmetry rule out that a "goodness of decoding" metric would allow? 3. Under infomax with a fixed number of output levels, what response distribution maximises entropy, and what does that imply about the input/output curve relative to the stimulus's cumulative distribution? 4. In the direct method, which two entropy rates are subtracted to get the information rate, and which one needs the stimulus-repeat protocol? 5. Why does the direct method's entropy estimate fall as recording length falls, and what does that imply for comparing estimates across recordings?

    Feeds: A5 (the sparsity and energy argument depends on bits per spike being high while rates are low).

    Done when: Given a small joint distribution or a binned spike train, the learner computes entropy, mutual information and an information rate by hand, and says which receptive-field property a given infomax constraint predicts.

    Lesson spec: foundations.md → F4. Total time: one to two sittings (~3.5-4.5 hours total: ~45 min guided reading, ~60 min derivation, ~75-90 min simulation including running code, ~40 min written explanation). Prerequisites: F1, F3 (F2 useful for the efficient-coding account of receptive fields).

    Running order

    Consume, then apply, then refine: guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written explanation (Piece 4). Pieces are numbered by type, not sequence.

    Piece 3 — Guided reading

    Type: guided reading · Time: ~45 min

    Learning goals - Locate and paraphrase the text's own definitions of entropy and mutual information before deriving anything by hand. - Extract the argument for why mutual information, not decoder accuracy, is the right currency. - Extract the infomax derivation and its receptive-field predictions (fixed-range and natural-image cases). - Extract the direct method's definition and the estimator-bias caveat.

    Task

    Read D&A ch. 4, sections Entropy and Mutual Information, Information and Entropy Maximization, and Entropy and Information for Spike Trains. Answer each question below in 2-5 sentences, in your own words, citing which named section you drew it from. Do not just copy definitions — paraphrase them and show you can apply them to the small example the section itself uses.

    1. How does the text define the entropy of a response distribution, and what does it mean concretely that entropy is measured "in bits"?
    2. How does the text define mutual information I(R;S) in terms of entropy? Give the section's own worked example (or the closest one) and its numeric answer if one is given.
    3. Why does the text present mutual information, rather than a decoding accuracy or reconstruction-error metric, as the measure of "how much a response tells you about the stimulus"? What property of mutual information does the text lean on to make that case?
    4. What is the infomax hypothesis as the text states it, and what response distribution does the text derive as entropy-maximizing under a fixed number of discrete output levels?
    5. What relationship does the text draw between that entropy-maximizing distribution and the cumulative distribution function of the stimulus? (This is the "histogram equalization" result — find where the text names or implies it.)
    6. Separately, what does the text say infomax (via decorrelation) predicts about receptive fields when the input statistics are natural images rather than a single scalar stimulus?
    7. How does the text define the direct method for estimating a spike train's information rate — what is a "word," and what two entropy quantities does the method compute?
    8. Which of those two entropy quantities requires repeated presentations of the same stimulus, and why does that quantity in particular need repeats rather than a single long recording?
    9. What does the text say happens to the entropy estimate as the amount of recorded data decreases, and what mechanism does it give for that?

    Deliverable

    A short document (one paragraph per question) with section citations, ready to hand to the teacher for a content check before you proceed to Piece 1.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong All 9 answered in the learner's own words, each correctly citing its section, each showing the mechanism (not just the conclusion) — e.g. Q3 names the symmetry/decoder-independence property, not just "MI is better."
    Adequate All 9 answered and mostly correct, but 1-2 read as copied definitions without paraphrase, or 1 answer is vague about mechanism.
    Not yet Answers missing, wrong section, or conclusions stated without the supporting mechanism (e.g. "infomax predicts equalization" with no CDF connection).

    Grader key (teacher AI only — verify against the physical text before scoring; do not treat the summary below as a quotation)

    Expected content, to check the learner's paraphrase against, not to hand out: 1. Entropy H(R) = -sum_r P(r) log2 P(r); bits because log base 2; one bit = the information from a binary choice with two equally likely outcomes. 2. I(R;S) = H(R) - H(R|S) (equivalently H(S) - H(S|R)); the text's own worked example is a small discrete table analogous to the one used in Piece 1 here — check the learner reports numeric values consistent with correctly applying the formula to whatever table D&A actually uses, not necessarily matching Piece 1's numbers. 3. Symmetry (I(R;S) = I(S;R)) and the fact that MI makes no assumption about how the response is decoded — it upper-bounds every possible decoder, whereas an accuracy metric depends on the decoder chosen and can understate the channel's true information content. 4. Infomax: choose the encoding/response distribution to maximize H(R) subject to constraints (fixed range or fixed number of levels); the maximizing distribution, for a fixed number of discrete equally-spaced levels, is the one with as close to uniform probability across levels as the constraint allows. 5. The entropy-maximizing input/output curve is (proportional to) the cumulative distribution function of the stimulus — i.e., the optimal nonlinearity is the stimulus's own CDF, producing a uniform output distribution (histogram equalization). 6. For natural images (spatially correlated input), infomax via redundancy reduction predicts decorrelating filters — center-surround (difference-of-Gaussians-like) receptive fields that whiten the correlated input. 7. Direct method: bin spike train into small time bins, group L consecutive bins into a "word"; compute the total response entropy rate from the word distribution over the whole recording, and the noise entropy rate from the word distribution conditioned on (repeats of) the same stimulus; information rate = total entropy rate - noise entropy rate. 8. The noise entropy rate needs repeated presentations of the identical stimulus, because it measures the residual (trial-to-trial) variability in the response given a fixed stimulus — that requires multiple trials with the same stimulus to observe the response's spread. 9. Entropy estimates from finite data are downward biased — the plug-in/naive estimate computed from empirical word frequencies underestimates the true entropy, more severely as the recording (hence sample count) shrinks, because the empirical distribution under-samples rare words; the usual fix is extrapolating estimates from progressively larger data fractions to infinite data.

    Teacher constraints - Hint 1: Point to which of the three named subsections the question's topic sentence appears under. - Hint 2: Ask the learner to first find the section's own formula, then re-derive the numeric example the section uses, before answering in prose. - Hint 3: If the learner conflates "total entropy" and "noise entropy," ask them to describe what changes about the recording protocol between the two. - Never: state the numeric value from D&A's example yourself, or tell the learner which page it is on — you have not read the physical text; only confirm or flag inconsistency in the learner's own report against the expected content above.

    Piece 1 — Entropy and mutual information on a worked table

    Type: derivation · Time: ~60 min

    Learning goals - Build a full joint probability table from marginal and conditional probabilities and compute H(S), H(R), H(R|S), H(S|R) from it, in bits. - Compute I(R;S) two independent ways (via entropy differences, and via the direct joint-distribution sum) and show they agree. - State, with this example's actual numbers, why I(R;S) = I(S;R). - Explain what that symmetry rules out about equating "information carried" with "decoding accuracy."

    Task

    Two equally likely stimuli s1, s2 drive a neuron's response, which takes one of two values r1, r2 (e.g. "response above/below some threshold count in a fixed window"). The channel is:

    P(s1) = 0.5, P(s2) = 0.5 P(r1 | s1) = 0.8, P(r2 | s1) = 0.2 P(r1 | s2) = 0.3, P(r2 | s2) = 0.7

    1. Build the full joint table P(s,r) (4 entries).
    2. Compute the marginals P(r1), P(r2), and H(S), H(R) in bits.
    3. Compute H(R|S) as the probability-weighted average of the conditional entropy given each stimulus (you may use the binary entropy function H_b(p) = -p log2 p - (1-p) log2(1-p) as a shortcut, but show at least one H_b(p) expanded from the log2 definition by hand).
    4. Compute I(R;S) = H(R) - H(R|S).
    5. Recompute I(R;S) directly from the joint table using I(R;S) = sum_{s,r} P(s,r) log2 [P(s,r) / (P(s)P(r))], and confirm it matches step 4 to 3 decimal places.
    6. Compute H(S|R) and I(S;R) = H(S) - H(S|R), and confirm I(S;R) = I(R;S).
    7. In 3-5 sentences: why is this equality forced by the formula in step 5 (look at what happens if you swap the labels s and r in that sum), and what does it rule out about using a "decoder's percent-correct" as a stand-in for how much information the channel carries?

    Deliverable

    A worked sheet (handwritten or typed) with all four table cells, both entropy calculations, both routes to I(R;S) shown side by side, the H(S|R) check, and the written answer to step 7.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong All values correct to 3 decimals, both routes to I(R;S) shown and reconciled explicitly, symmetry demonstrated numerically (not asserted), and step 7 names the specific formula symmetry and the decoder-independence consequence with a concrete failure mode for accuracy-as-information.
    Adequate Joint table and entropies correct; one of the two I(R;S) routes present but not both reconciled, or step 7 states the conclusion without tracing it to the swapped-label argument.
    Not yet Joint table has an arithmetic error propagating through the rest, or I(R;S) computed only one way, or step 7 is a restatement of "MI is symmetric" with no explanation of why or what it rules out.

    Grader key (teacher AI only)

    Joint table: P(s1,r1)=0.40, P(s1,r2)=0.10, P(s2,r1)=0.15, P(s2,r2)=0.35. Marginals: P(r1)=0.55, P(r2)=0.45. H(S) = 1.000 bits (exactly, since P(s1)=P(s2)=0.5). H(R) = -0.55·log2(0.55) - 0.45·log2(0.45) = 0.4744 + 0.5184 = 0.9928 bits. H_b(0.2) = 0.7219 bits (this is H(R|s1)); H_b(0.3) = 0.8813 bits (this is H(R|s2)). H(R|S) = 0.5·0.7219 + 0.5·0.8813 = 0.8016 bits. I(R;S) = H(R) - H(R|S) = 0.9928 - 0.8016 = 0.1912 bits. Direct sum check: terms are 0.4·log2(0.4/0.275)=+0.2162, 0.1·log2(0.1/0.225)=-0.1170, 0.15·log2(0.15/0.275)=-0.1312, 0.35·log2(0.35/0.225)=+0.2231; sum = 0.1911 bits (matches step-4 value to rounding). H(S|R) = H(S) - I(S;R); since I(S;R)=I(R;S)=0.1912, H(S|R) = 1.000 - 0.1912 = 0.8088 bits. Accept anything in the range 0.190-0.192 bits for I(R;S) as correct (rounding path-dependence). Step 7 expected content: the sum in step 5 is symmetric under exchanging (s,r) labels because P(s,r)=P(r,s) and P(s)P(r)=P(r)P(s) — the formula does not privilege either variable, so I(R;S)=I(S;R) is forced by construction, not a coincidence of these numbers. Consequence: any "goodness of decoding" metric (e.g. percent of trials correctly classified by some specific decoding rule) is decoder-dependent — a poor decoder can make the same channel look uninformative, and a different decoder on the same data gives a different accuracy — so accuracy cannot be a stand-in for the channel's actual information content, which is a fixed property of the joint distribution and upper-bounds every decoder's performance including the best possible one.

    Teacher constraints - Hint 1: If stuck on the joint table, ask "what is P(s1) times P(r1|s1)?" — do not compute the cell yourself. - Hint 2: If stuck on H(R|S), point to the binary entropy function shortcut and ask them to expand one term (e.g. H_b(0.2)) by hand to confirm they can derive it, not just plug into a formula. - Hint 3: If the two I(R;S) routes disagree by more than rounding, ask them to recheck the joint table cells sum to 1 and the marginals were computed by summing the right axis. - Never: give any of the numeric entropy, joint-table, or MI values before the learner has submitted their own attempt at that specific step.

    Piece 2 — Simulation: estimator bias and infomax in code

    Type: simulation · Time: ~75-90 min

    Learning goals - Implement the direct method's word-binning entropy-rate estimator and observe it underestimate a known ground-truth rate. - Connect the direction of that bias (always downward, worse with less data) to Jensen's inequality applied to the concave entropy function. - Implement the infomax-optimal nonlinearity (CDF matching) for a non-uniform stimulus and confirm it flattens the output distribution.

    Task

    Run the script below unmodified first; then answer the questions after it.

    import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

def h_b(p):
    """Binary entropy in bits."""
    if p <= 0 or p >= 1:
        return 0.0
    return -p * np.log2(p) - (1 - p) * np.log2(1 - p)

def direct_method_rate(spikes, L, dt):
    """Total-entropy rate of a binary spike train via the direct method:
    bin into words of length L bins, estimate H(word) from empirical
    word frequencies, divide by word duration."""
    n_words = len(spikes) // L
    words = spikes[: n_words * L].reshape(n_words, L)
    codes = words.dot(1 << np.arange(L))
    counts = np.bincount(codes, minlength=2 ** L)
    probs = counts[counts > 0] / counts.sum()
    H_word = -np.sum(probs * np.log2(probs))
    return H_word / (L * dt), n_words

# --- Part A: entropy-rate estimate is downward biased as recording shrinks ---
p_spike, dt, L, n_repeats = 0.1, 0.001, 8, 40
true_rate = h_b(p_spike) / dt  # bins are i.i.d., so true rate is exact

print("Part A -- direct method vs recording length")
print(f"true rate = {true_rate:.2f} bits/s\n")
for T in [500.0, 50.0, 5.0, 0.5]:
    n_bins = int(T / dt)
    ests = [direct_method_rate((rng.random(n_bins) < p_spike).astype(int), L, dt)[0]
            for _ in range(n_repeats)]
    ests = np.array(ests)
    print(f"T={T:>6.1f}s  mean estimate={ests.mean():7.2f} bits/s  "
          f"bias={ests.mean() - true_rate:7.2f} bits/s  std={ests.std():5.2f}")

# --- Part B: infomax histogram equalization ---
def hist_entropy(x, edges):
    counts, _ = np.histogram(x, bins=edges)
    p = counts / counts.sum()
    p = p[p > 0]
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

n, bins = 200_000, 20
s = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=n)          # non-uniform stimulus
sorted_s = np.sort(s)
r = np.searchsorted(sorted_s, s, side="right") / n   # nonlinearity = own CDF

H_in = hist_entropy(s, np.linspace(0, np.percentile(s, 99.9), bins + 1))
H_out = hist_entropy(r, np.linspace(0, 1, bins + 1))
H_max = np.log2(bins)

print("\nPart B -- histogram equalization")
print(f"H_max = log2({bins}) = {H_max:.4f} bits")
print(f"H(input hist)  = {H_in:.4f} bits")
print(f"H(output hist) = {H_out:.4f} bits")

    Then answer:

    1. Report your printed numbers for Part A. Is the bias (mean estimate - true rate) ever positive? Does its magnitude grow monotonically as T shrinks?
    2. The plug-in entropy estimator computed from empirical word frequencies satisfies E[H(p_hat)] <= H(E[p_hat]) = H(p_true), by Jensen's inequality, because entropy is a concave function of the probability vector. Explain in your own words why this inequality forces the direction of the bias you observed (always <= true, never >), and why smaller T (fewer words) makes the gap larger.
    3. In Part B, why is H(output hist) close to H_max while H(input hist) is not? Which specific property of the transform r = searchsorted(sorted_s, s) / n makes this happen?
    4. Part B used a fixed number of bins (20) for both histograms. Relate this to criterion 3's infomax constraint ("fixed number of output levels") — what would you expect to happen to H(output hist) if you doubled bins to 40 without changing n? (You don't have to run it — reason from the definition of H_max.)

    Deliverable

    Your script's console output for both parts, plus written answers to the four questions above (total ~150-250 words for the prose parts).

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong Ran the code, correctly reports monotonically growing bias magnitude with T (in expectation over repeats), explains the Jensen argument correctly including why concavity implies E[H_hat] <= H_true (not just "less data is noisier"), correctly identifies the CDF-transform property, and correctly reasons that H_max would rise to log2(40) but H(output hist) would still track close to it only if n is large enough relative to 40 bins.
    Adequate Ran the code and reports correct numbers; Jensen explanation gestures at "biased low" without connecting it to concavity of H; bins question answered correctly by rote (H_max=log2(40)) without noting the sample-size caveat.
    Not yet Did not run the code / reports numbers inconsistent with a downward-biased, shrinking-with-T pattern; Jensen question answered as "more data is more accurate" with no mention of concavity or the direction of bias.

    Grader key (teacher AI only — reference numbers from running the exact script above with NumPy 2.2.4, seed 0; treat the qualitative pattern — monotonically growing negative bias as T shrinks, output entropy close to log2(20) — as the pass bar, not exact digit matching, since RNG stream details can differ slightly across NumPy versions)

    Part A: true rate = 469.00 bits/s (= H_b(0.1)/dt = 0.4690/0.001). - T=500.0s: mean estimate=468.90 bits/s, bias=-0.10, std=1.28 - T=50.0s: mean estimate=464.92 bits/s, bias=-4.07, std=4.16 - T=5.0s: mean estimate=449.97 bits/s, bias=-19.03, std=14.30 - T=0.5s: mean estimate=385.96 bits/s, bias=-83.04, std=34.90

    Bias is negative at every T tested and grows monotonically in magnitude as T shrinks (this requires averaging over repeats — a single run at small T can show noisy non-monotonic behavior due to high variance at n_words=62; if the learner ran without averaging and got a confusing single-run result, point them at n_repeats).

    Part B: H_max = log2(20) = 4.3219 bits. H(input hist) = 2.9640 bits. H(output hist) = 4.3219 bits (matches H_max, i.e. exactly flat within histogram resolution).

    Q2 expected content: Jensen's inequality for a concave function f gives E[f(X)] <= f(E[X]). Here f = H (concave in the probability vector) and X = the empirical distribution p_hat, whose expectation over resampling equals the true p (empirical frequencies are unbiased estimators of the true cell probabilities). So E[H(p_hat)] <= H(p) always, for any sample size — the bias can never be positive in expectation. As the word count n_words shrinks, p_hat's sampling variance around p grows, and because H is strictly concave, larger deviations of p_hat from p pull H(p_hat) down more (the concavity "curves away" from the true value faster the further you are from it) — so the expected gap H(p) - E[H(p_hat)] grows as n_words shrinks.

    Q3 expected content: the transform maps each sample to its own rank/n, i.e. its empirical CDF value — by construction, the empirical CDF of any continuous sample is (up to discretization) uniformly distributed on [0,1], so binning it into equal-width bins on [0,1] gives equal counts per bin, i.e. maximum entropy for that bin count.

    Q4 expected content: H_max would become log2(40)=5.3219 bits; H(output hist) would still track close to that new H_max as long as n=200,000 remains large relative to 40 bins (average ~5000 samples/bin), since the CDF-transform argument in Q3 doesn't depend on bin count — but if bins were increased far enough relative to n, undersampling would reintroduce the Part-A-style downward bias.

    Teacher constraints - Hint 1: If the learner's single run looks non-monotonic, ask whether they ran with the averaging loop (n_repeats) as given, or modified it to a single draw per T. - Hint 2: For Q2, ask them to state Jensen's inequality for a concave function first, then ask what "expectation of empirical frequency" equals. - Hint 3: For Q3, ask them what distribution the empirical CDF of a continuous variable always has, before pointing at the code line. - Never: run the code and hand the learner your own output before they report theirs; never write or fix code for them — describe the category of bug (e.g. "check what n_repeats controls") rather than the fix.

    Piece 4 — Written explanation

    Type: written explanation · Time: ~40 min · Length: 300-600 words

    Learning goals - Synthesize entropy, mutual information, and infomax into a single coherent account of "what it means for a neuron to carry information." - Explain, without hedging into vagueness, why mutual information rather than decoding accuracy is the right measure. - State concretely what infomax predicts for receptive fields in both the fixed-output-range case and the natural-image (decorrelation) case.

    Task

    Write 300-600 words, for an audience that knows information theory from an ML context but not its neural application, answering: What does it mean, quantitatively, for a neuron's response to "carry information" about a stimulus? Why is mutual information — not a decoder's accuracy, not correlation, not "response magnitude" — the right currency? And what does the infomax hypothesis concretely predict a real neuron's input/output curve or receptive field should look like, in the fixed-range case and in the natural-image case? Use your own numbers from Piece 1 and Piece 2 as illustration where they help (e.g. "in the two-stimulus example, the channel carried 0.191 of a possible 1 bit about which stimulus occurred"). Do not restate D&A's or this coursework's sentences verbatim — write it as your own explanation.

    Deliverable

    A 300-600 word write-up, plain prose (a figure or short table is fine but not required).

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong Correctly and precisely defines information-carrying as reduction in uncertainty about S given R (in bits), uses the learner's own Piece 1/2 numbers as concrete illustration, gives the decoder-independence argument for MI (not just "it's the standard measure"), and states both infomax predictions (histogram equalization under a fixed-range/level constraint; decorrelating/center-surround filters under natural-image statistics) with the mechanism (CDF-matching; whitening correlated input) rather than just the label.
    Adequate Covers all four required elements (definition, why MI, fixed-range prediction, natural-image prediction) but 1-2 are stated as conclusions without mechanism, or omits the learner's own worked numbers.
    Not yet Missing one of the four required elements, or defines "information" loosely (e.g. "how much the neuron reacts") without reference to uncertainty reduction/entropy, or states infomax predictions without distinguishing the fixed-range case from the natural-image case.

    Grader key (teacher AI only)

    Must-hit points: (1) information = bits of reduction in uncertainty about S from observing R, i.e. I(R;S)=H(R)-H(R|S), illustrated with a number the learner actually computed (0.191 bits in Piece 1's example, or a Part-A/B number from Piece 2 used analogously); (2) MI is the right currency because it is symmetric and decoder-independent — it upper-bounds any decoding scheme's extractable information, whereas accuracy is a property of a chosen decoder, not of the channel; (3) fixed-range infomax predicts the input/output curve equals the stimulus's CDF, producing histogram-equalized (uniform) output — directly the Piece 2 Part B result; (4) natural-image infomax (via redundancy reduction/decorrelation) predicts center-surround receptive fields that whiten spatially correlated input. Flag as "not yet" any write-up that treats "information" as a synonym for firing rate, response reliability, or SNR without an entropy/uncertainty framing.
