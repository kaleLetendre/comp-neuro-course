    Lesson spec¶

    F3. Neural decoding¶

    Prerequisites: F1, F2

    Size: one to two sittings — four decoding methods plus a Fisher-information derivation; splits cleanly at discrimination-and-population-decoding versus Fisher-information-and-spike-train-decoding

    Source material: D&A ch. 3 — Encoding and Decoding · Discrimination · Population Decoding · Spike-Train Decoding

    Goal: Establish decoding as the inverse of encoding — given a response, infer the stimulus — by Bayes' rule rather than a new formalism. Cover discrimination via ROC, four population estimators, the Fisher-information bound, and linear spike-train reconstruction as instances of one inference problem.

    Scope: - Encoding p(r|s) versus decoding p(s|r): why they are different problems, and how Bayes connects them - Two-alternative discrimination, ROC curves, and the result that a single well-tuned neuron can match an animal's psychophysical performance - Population decoding compared — population vector, maximum likelihood, MAP, Bayesian — what each assumes and when the population vector is biased - Fisher information and the Cramér-Rao bound on decoding accuracy, and its relation to tuning-curve width - Spike-train decoding: reconstructing a time-varying stimulus from spike timing - Not covered: quantifying readout capacity in bits (F4)

    You should be able to answer: 1. Write Bayes' rule relating p(r|s) and p(s|r). Which does a single-neuron recording directly estimate, and what extra term do you need for the other? 2. In a two-alternative task, which single number computed from the ROC curve equals proportion correct, and how did that compare to the animal's own threshold in the classic MT experiment? 3. Write the population vector estimator. Under what condition on the tuning curves is it unbiased, and what happens when that condition fails? 4. State the Cramér-Rao bound linking Fisher information to estimator variance. As tuning curves narrow with neuron count fixed, does accuracy improve monotonically — what is the trade-off? 5. Write the linear estimator reconstructing a time-varying stimulus from a spike train as a sum over spike times of a kernel.

    Feeds: F4 (mutual information formalises how much can be read out), F11 (the same inference machinery moved inside the model).

    Done when: Given a population of tuning curves and a spike-count vector, you computes the maximum-likelihood and population-vector estimates by hand and says which is biased and why.

    Lesson spec: foundations.md → F3. Total time: ~5-6 hours (one to two sittings). Prerequisites: F1, F2.

    Running order¶

    Consume, then apply, then refine: guided reading (Piece 3) → derivation (Piece 1) → simulation (Piece 2) → written explanation (Piece 4). Pieces are numbered by type, not sequence.

    Piece 1 — Population and Fisher-information decoding by hand¶

    Type: derivation · Time: ~2 hours

    Learning goals - Compute a population-vector estimate from a spike-count vector and a set of cosine tuning curves. - Compute a maximum-likelihood estimate by evaluating a Poisson log-likelihood on a grid. - State the condition under which the population vector is unbiased, and show numerically what happens when it fails. - Derive the Fisher information for a Poisson neuron with a Gaussian tuning curve, and use it to see that narrowing tuning curves does not monotonically improve population accuracy.

    Task

    All three sub-tasks use the Poisson encoding model: neuron i has expected spike count \(E[n_i] = f_i(s)\,T\) over trial length \(T\), and the actual count \(n_i\) is drawn Poisson with that mean.

    Task A — uniform population, population vector vs. ML. Eight neurons have cosine tuning \(f_i(s) = 20 + 20\cos(s - \theta_i)\) Hz, with preferred directions \(\theta_i\) evenly spaced every 45°: 0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°, 225°, 270°, 315°. Over a \(T=1\,\mathrm{s}\) trial with true stimulus \(s = 30°\), the observed spike counts are:

    \(\theta_i\) 0° 45° 90° 135° 180° 225° 270° 315°
    \(n_i\) 37 39 30 15 3 1 10 25
    1. Compute the population-vector estimate: \(\vec v = \sum_i n_i(\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)\), then \(\hat s = \operatorname{atan2}(v_y, v_x)\).
    2. Compute the Poisson log-likelihood (drop the \(\log n_i!\) constant) \(\ell(s) = \sum_i \big[n_i \ln f_i(s) - f_i(s)\big]\) at \(s = 20°, 30°, 40°\), and report which grid point maximizes it.
    3. State whether the two estimators agree with each other and with the true stimulus, to within a few degrees.

    Task B — non-uniform population, same cosine tuning. Keep \(f_i(s) = 20 + 20\cos(s-\theta_i)\) but now the eight preferred directions are clustered: five near 0° (at \(-30°,-20°,-10°,0°,10°\)... use exactly \(\theta_i \in \{-30°, 10°, 20°, 30°, 340°\}\) — five directions within 60° of 0°) and three spread out at \(\{120°, 200°, 280°\}\). Using the noiseless expected rates (i.e. treat \(f_i(90°)\) itself as \(n_i\), no Poisson step) for a true stimulus \(s = 90°\): 1. Compute \(f_i(90°)\) for each of the eight \(\theta_i\). 2. Compute the population-vector estimate from these noiseless rates. 3. State, in one sentence, why the estimate is far from 90° even with zero noise, and name the general condition on tuning-curve coverage under which the population vector is unbiased.

    Task C — Fisher information and tuning-curve width. Five neurons have Gaussian tuning \(f_i(s) = 50\exp\!\big(-(s-\theta_i)^2/2\sigma^2\big)\) Hz with \(\theta_i \in \{-40°,-20°,0°,20°,40°\}\), evaluated at true stimulus \(s=10°\), \(T=1\,\mathrm{s}\). For a Poisson neuron, the Fisher information about \(s\) is \(I_i(s) = T\,f_i'(s)^2 / f_i(s)\), and for independent neurons the population information adds: \(I_{\text{pop}}(s) = \sum_i I_i(s)\). 1. Derive \(I_i(s)\) in closed form for the Gaussian tuning curve above (show \(f_i'(s)\) first). 2. Evaluate \(I_{\text{pop}}(10°)\) for \(\sigma = 20°\), \(\sigma=10°\), \(\sigma=5°\), and \(\sigma=3°\). 3. Using the Cramér-Rao bound \(\mathrm{Var}(\hat s) \geq 1/I_{\text{pop}}(s)\), report the implied lower bound on the standard deviation of any unbiased estimator at each \(\sigma\). 4. State whether accuracy (the CR bound) improves monotonically as \(\sigma\) shrinks, and explain the trade-off in one or two sentences.

    Deliverable

    A written or typed solution set showing every intermediate number (not just final answers) for Tasks A, B, and C, plus one sentence per task answering its final question.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong All three tasks' intermediate numbers match the grader key within tolerance; correctly identifies Task A as unbiased/agreeing and Task B as biased, names "non-uniform coverage of preferred directions" as the failure condition; Task C shows the non-monotonic rise-then-fall pattern and states the trade-off (narrower tuning sharpens active neurons' slopes but shrinks the pool of neurons close enough to the stimulus to respond at all).
    Adequate Gets the arithmetic mostly right (minor rounding/sign slips) and reaches the right qualitative conclusions for at least two of the three tasks, but Task C's non-monotonicity is asserted rather than shown in the numbers, or one task's final-question sentence is vague.
    Not yet Arithmetic errors change the qualitative conclusion (e.g. claims Task B is unbiased, or claims Task C's bound improves monotonically), or intermediate numbers are missing so the answer can't be checked.

    Piece 2 — Simulating three population decoders¶

    Type: simulation · Time: ~1.5 hours

    Learning goals - Implement Poisson spike-count generation from tuning curves and three decoders (population vector, ML, MAP) from scratch. - Observe, across many trials, that population-vector error explodes when tuning-curve coverage is non-uniform while ML stays accurate — the bias from Task B, now shown statistically rather than in one hand-picked case. - Observe that a MAP estimator with a strong prior is pulled away from the ML estimate toward the prior mode.

    Task

    Run the script below (or reimplement it if you prefer to type it yourself — that's more valuable than pasting it). It is under 100 lines, pure NumPy, no downloads.

    import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T = 1.0
R0, RMAX = 20.0, 20.0

def rates(thetas, s):
    return R0 + RMAX * np.cos(np.deg2rad(s - thetas))

def pop_vector(n, thetas):
    vx = np.sum(n * np.cos(np.deg2rad(thetas)))
    vy = np.sum(n * np.sin(np.deg2rad(thetas)))
    return np.rad2deg(np.arctan2(vy, vx)) % 360

def loglik(n, thetas, grid):
    f = rates(thetas[:, None], grid[None, :])
    f_safe = np.clip(f, 1e-9, None)
    return np.sum(n[:, None] * np.log(f_safe) - f, axis=0)

def ml_estimate(n, thetas, grid):
    return grid[np.argmax(loglik(n, thetas, grid))]

def map_estimate(n, thetas, grid, prior_logpdf):
    return grid[np.argmax(loglik(n, thetas, grid) + prior_logpdf(grid))]

def circ_err(est, true):
    return (est - true + 180) % 360 - 180

grid = np.arange(0, 360, 1.0)
thetas_uniform = np.arange(0, 360, 22.5)                    # 16 neurons, evenly spaced
thetas_clustered = np.concatenate(
    [np.linspace(-30, 30, 10), np.linspace(60, 300, 6)]) % 360  # 10 of 16 packed near 0 deg

def run_trials(thetas, n_trials=1000):
    pv_e, ml_e = [], []
    for _ in range(n_trials):
        s_true = rng.uniform(0, 360)
        f = rates(thetas, s_true)
        n = rng.poisson(f * T)
        pv_e.append(circ_err(pop_vector(n, thetas), s_true))
        ml_e.append(circ_err(ml_estimate(n, thetas, grid), s_true))
    return np.array(pv_e), np.array(ml_e)

for name, thetas in [("uniform", thetas_uniform), ("clustered", thetas_clustered)]:
    pv_e, ml_e = run_trials(thetas)
    print(f"{name}: PV mean={pv_e.mean():.2f} std={pv_e.std():.2f} MAE={np.abs(pv_e).mean():.2f} | "
          f"ML mean={ml_e.mean():.2f} std={ml_e.std():.2f} MAE={np.abs(ml_e).mean():.2f}")

def vm_logpdf(x, mu=90.0, kappa=4.0):
    return kappa * np.cos(np.deg2rad(x - mu))

s_true = 200.0
diffs = []
for _ in range(200):
    f = rates(thetas_uniform, s_true)
    n = rng.poisson(f * T)
    ml = ml_estimate(n, thetas_uniform, grid)
    mp = map_estimate(n, thetas_uniform, grid, vm_logpdf)
    diffs.append(circ_err(mp, ml))
print(f"MAP-vs-ML shift toward prior (true={s_true}): mean={np.mean(diffs):.1f} std={np.std(diffs):.1f}")

s_fixed = 90.0
pv_fixed, ml_fixed = [], []
for _ in range(200):
    f = rates(thetas_clustered, s_fixed)
    n = rng.poisson(f * T)
    pv_fixed.append(pop_vector(n, thetas_clustered))
    ml_fixed.append(ml_estimate(n, thetas_clustered, grid))
print(f"clustered, s_true=90 fixed: PV mean estimate={np.mean(pv_fixed):.1f} (true=90) | "
      f"ML mean estimate={np.mean(ml_fixed):.1f}")

    Answer, from the actual printed output: 1. Compare the clustered row's PV mean-absolute-error to the uniform row's. By roughly what factor is it larger? 2. Compare clustered PV MAE to clustered ML MAE. Which estimator degrades under non-uniform coverage and which doesn't? 3. In the fixed-stimulus (s_true=90) run, how far is the average PV estimate from the true 90°, and how does that compare to the average ML estimate? Relate this to Task B of Piece 1. 4. In the MAP line, which direction did the estimate move relative to ML (toward 90° or away from it), and why is that the expected, not broken, behavior of a MAP estimator?

    Piece 3 — Guided reading¶

    Type: guided reading · Time: ~1 hour

    Learning goals - Locate and state the Bayes'-rule relationship between encoding and decoding. - Know the ROC-based discrimination result and its classic empirical example. - Know the four population-decoding methods' assumptions and the Fisher-information/tuning-width relationship, in the text's own terms.

    Task

    Answer the five questions below using D&A ch. 3, sections Encoding and Decoding, Discrimination, Population Decoding, and Spike-Train Decoding. Cite which section each answer came from.

    1. (Encoding and Decoding) Write Bayes' rule relating \(p(r\mid s)\) and \(p(s\mid r)\). Which of the two does a single-neuron recording (repeated stimulus presentations, tallying responses) directly estimate, and what extra term do you need to get the other?
    2. (Discrimination) In a two-alternative discrimination task, which single number computed from the ROC curve equals the proportion correct? What did the classic MT (area MT/V5) motion-discrimination experiment find when comparing a single well-tuned neuron's ROC-based performance to the animal's own psychophysical performance?
    3. (Population Decoding) Write the population-vector estimator. Under what condition on the tuning curves is it unbiased, and what happens when that condition fails?
    4. (Population Decoding) State the Cramér-Rao bound linking Fisher information to estimator variance. As tuning curves narrow with the number of neurons held fixed, does decoding accuracy improve monotonically? What is the trade-off?
    5. (Spike-Train Decoding) Write the linear estimator that reconstructs a time-varying stimulus from a spike train as a sum, over spike times, of a kernel.

    Deliverable

    Five short written answers (2-5 sentences each), each naming the section it draws from.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong All five answers are substantively correct and each cites the right named section; Q3 and Q4 correctly state the unbiasedness condition and the non-monotonic trade-off (not just "narrower is better").
    Adequate Four of five answers correct, or answers correct but missing/wrong section citations.
    Not yet Two or more answers are missing, wrong, or generic enough that they could have been written without opening the book (e.g. "Bayes' rule relates probabilities").

    Piece 4 — Written explanation¶

    Type: written explanation · Time: ~45 minutes

    Learning goals - Synthesize discrimination, population decoding, and spike-train decoding as instances of one Bayesian inference problem rather than four unrelated techniques. - Explain, using your own derivation and simulation numbers, why decoders can disagree.

    Task

    In 300-600 words, explain why decoding is not simply "run the tuning curve backward," and why different decoders (population vector, ML, MAP) can give different answers from the same spike counts. Your explanation must: - State the Bayes'-rule relationship between encoding and decoding, and say concretely what extra ingredient MAP/Bayesian decoding needs beyond the likelihood that ML does not. - Use your own Task B / Piece 2 numbers (the ~56-59° population-vector bias at \(s=90°\) under clustered tuning, versus ML's accuracy there) as evidence for why the population vector's unbiasedness is conditional, not automatic. - Use your Task C numbers to explain why "more neurons or narrower tuning is always better" is false in general, in terms of Fisher information. - Briefly place spike-train decoding (Piece 3, Q5) as the same inference problem in the time domain rather than a different idea.

    Deliverable

    A 300-600 word written explanation (prose, not bullet points), referencing specific numbers from your own Piece 1 and Piece 2 work.

    Rubric

    Band Criteria
    Strong Correctly states Bayes' rule and the extra prior term; cites specific numbers from your own Piece 1/2 work (not generic restatement) to support the bias and Fisher-information points; correctly frames spike-train decoding as the same inference problem, not a separate technique; stays within the word range.
    Adequate Covers all four required points but leans on generic statements ("the population vector can be biased") rather than your own computed numbers in at least one place.
    Not yet Misstates Bayes' rule or the ML/MAP distinction, omits one of the four required points, or is well outside the word range.
