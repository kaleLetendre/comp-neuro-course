F1. What you does after listening.

Context for the generator. This audio is the first step of a lesson, not the whole of it. Afterwards you works through Dayan and Abbott chapter one, does a derivation by hand, and writes code. The overview should leave them equipped for that work and mildly impatient to start it, not feeling the topic is closed.

The derivation. Rate arithmetic on a short spike train, then working out how noisy a rate estimate becomes for a hundred millisecond window compared with a ten millisecond window, on a train at a known average rate.

The simulation. Generate completely random spike trains at a known rate, then recover that rate three ways, by counting, by a sliding window, and by smoothing each spike into a bump. Watch the three estimates disagree with each other and with the truth. Then impose a refractory period and watch the Fano factor fall below one, in a run whose result can be worked out with pencil and paper beforehand.

The written explanation. Several hundred words arguing why the firing rate is not a single well defined number, aimed at a reader who assumed it was.

The useful thing the audio can do is make the questions behind those tasks feel live. Why the three estimates must disagree. What the refractory period is doing to the statistics. Why the completely random reference process is worth caring about even though no real neuron behaves that way. Do not do the tasks. Do not give the numbers they are meant to produce.
